# DEPRESSION — 02 Stress, Cortisol, Genes and Early Adversity

*The stress system sits upstream of almost every other pathway in this series. This note explains how it works, how it breaks, and why some people are far more vulnerable than others.*

## The HPA axis: a three-step relay with a brake

When the brain detects a threat, a chain fires (🟢):

1. **Hypothalamus** releases CRH (corticotropin-releasing hormone).
2. **Pituitary** responds with ACTH.
3. **Adrenal glands** release **cortisol** into the blood.

Cortisol mobilises fuel (raises blood sugar), sharpens attention, suppresses digestion, reproduction and parts of immunity — everything useful for the next few minutes, nothing useful for the next few years.

The system is designed to **switch itself off**. Cortisol binds receptors in the **hippocampus** and **prefrontal cortex**, which tell the hypothalamus to stop. The **amygdala** pushes the other way: it drives the alarm. Health depends on the balance between accelerator (amygdala) and brake (hippocampus, prefrontal cortex). 🟢

Cortisol also has a **daily rhythm**: a sharp rise in the 30–45 minutes after waking (the cortisol awakening response), then a steady fall to its lowest point around midnight. That rhythm is one of the clock signals that organises the whole body. 🟢

## How it breaks in depression

- **The brake fails.** In severe, melancholic depression, cortisol is often high and the system no longer shuts off. In the classic dexamethasone suppression test (a synthetic steroid that should silence the axis), roughly **half of severely depressed inpatients fail to suppress**. 🟢
- **The rhythm flattens.** Evening cortisol stays high instead of falling; the daily curve loses its shape. A flat curve is associated with worse mood, poorer sleep and worse health generally. 🟡
- **Not everyone is high.** "Atypical" depression (oversleeping, overeating, leaden fatigue) and long-standing stress or burnout more often show **low or blunted** cortisol. 🟡 This is one more reason "depression" is several conditions.
- **Glucocorticoid resistance.** After prolonged exposure, immune cells stop responding to cortisol's anti-inflammatory signal — so chronic stress ends up **raising** inflammation ([[DEPRESSION — 03 Inflammation and Sickness Behaviour]]). 🟡

## What chronic cortisol does to the brain

Robert Sapolsky's central point: the stress response is brilliant for a zebra escaping a lion and damaging for a human who turns it on for months over mortgages, conflict and loss.

- In animals, sustained stress hormones **shrink dendrites** (the branches neurons use to connect) in the hippocampus and prefrontal cortex, and **grow** them in the amygdala. 🟢 (animal)
- The result is a brain with a stronger alarm and a weaker brake — more fear, less flexible thinking, poorer memory.
- In humans, people with recurrent depression have **smaller hippocampi** on average (ENIGMA consortium, ~9,000 people). 🟢 Whether stress caused that, or it was there first, is not settled. 🟡
- Much of this is **reversible** in animals when the stress ends — which is the basis for the plasticity story in [[DEPRESSION — 05 Neuroplasticity and Why Antidepressants Take Weeks]]. 🟡

## Stress-diathesis: why the same event breaks one person and not another

A *diathesis* is a pre-existing vulnerability. Depression usually needs both a vulnerability and a load. 🟢

**Genes.**

- Twin studies put the heritability of major depression at about **35–40%** — lower than height or bipolar disorder, but substantial. 🟢
- There is **no depression gene.** The largest genetic studies (2025, ~5 million people) found hundreds of variants, each with a tiny effect. 🟢
- The famous 2003 finding that one serotonin-transporter variant made people depressed under stress **did not replicate** in large studies. 🟢 that it failed.
- What is inherited is better described as **sensitivity** — a nervous system that reacts more strongly to stress and recovers more slowly. 🟡

**Early adversity.**

- The Adverse Childhood Experiences (ACE) study (~17,000 adults) counted things like abuse, neglect, a parent with addiction or mental illness, divorce. People with **four or more** ACEs had roughly **four to five times** the rate of depression. 🟢 (observational, large, repeatedly confirmed)
- Proposed mechanisms: early stress **calibrates** the HPA axis to expect danger, raises lifelong inflammation (adults maltreated as children have higher CRP), and shapes thinking patterns. 🟡
- **Epigenetics:** in rats, the amount of maternal licking changes chemical marks on the cortisol-receptor gene, setting the stress response for life. Similar marks appear in some human studies. 🟢 in rats, 🟠 in humans.
- Adversity raises risk; it does not determine outcome. Most people with high ACE scores do not develop depression. 🟢

## Kindling: the first episode is the expensive one

First depressive episodes are usually triggered by a major life event. Later episodes need smaller triggers, and eventually some arrive with no obvious trigger at all. 🟡 The brain seems to learn the route into depression. The practical consequence: **preventing recurrence matters as much as treating the episode**, and the first year after recovery is the most vulnerable.

## Control is the active ingredient

Martin Seligman's 1960s experiments: dogs given shocks they could not escape later stopped trying even when escape was easy — **learned helplessness**. 🟢 (animal)

Fifty years later, Maier and Seligman reversed the interpretation: passivity is the brain's **default** response to prolonged bad events. What must be *learned* is **control** — the prefrontal cortex detects that actions matter and actively inhibits the brainstem stress centres. 🟡 Stress plus no sense of control is the toxic combination; the same stress with control is far less harmful. Sapolsky's baboon studies say the same about **social rank** and predictability. 🟡

## Example case

The death of someone close is one of the largest stressors a person can face: loss of attachment, loss of routine, often loss of control over what happened. Months of disturbed sleep, early waking near the cortisol rise, and repeated reminders can keep the axis in alarm mode. A family history of depression or an earlier episode would raise the odds that the grief opens onto a depressive episode. The leverage points from this note: restoring some sense of control (small decisions, routines kept), protecting the daily rhythm (fixed wake time, morning light), and treating a first episode seriously to avoid kindling.

## WHAT TO HOLD ONTO

- Stress system = accelerator (amygdala) plus brake (hippocampus, prefrontal cortex). Depression is often a broken brake.
- High cortisol fits severe depression; atypical depression and burnout can show low cortisol.
- Chronic stress strengthens the alarm and weakens the brake — largely reversible.
- Genes and early adversity set sensitivity, not destiny.
- Each episode makes the next easier; prevention of recurrence is part of treatment.
- Uncontrollable stress is the damaging kind. Control is learned, and can be relearned.
