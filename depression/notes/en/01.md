# DEPRESSION — 01 What Depression Is

*Foundation note. Defines what is being talked about in every other note: what depression is, what it is not, and how it differs from grief.*

**Evidence labels used in this series:** 🟢 Strong (replicated, large studies or meta-analyses) · 🟡 Moderate (consistent but limited, or mostly observational) · 🟠 Preliminary (early, small or animal studies) · 🔴 Contested (experts genuinely disagree, or the popular claim outruns the data)

## A syndrome, not a single disease

Depression is diagnosed by **symptoms and time**, not by a blood test or scan. There is no lab marker for it. 🟢

The standard definition (DSM-5-TR) of a **major depressive episode**: at least **five of nine** symptoms, most of the day, nearly every day, for **at least two weeks**, causing real distress or loss of function. At least one of the five must be either **depressed mood** or **anhedonia** (loss of interest or pleasure). 🟢

The nine:

1. Depressed mood (sad, empty, hopeless)
2. Anhedonia — nothing feels rewarding
3. Appetite or weight change (up or down)
4. Sleep change — insomnia or oversleeping
5. Slowed down, or restless and agitated (visible to others)
6. Fatigue, loss of energy
7. Worthlessness or excessive guilt
8. Poor concentration, indecision
9. Recurrent thoughts of death or suicide

**Why this matters:** because any five of nine qualify, two people with the same diagnosis can share almost no symptoms. In the largest antidepressant trial (STAR*D, ~3,700 patients), researchers counted over **1,000 different symptom combinations**; the most common one described fewer than 2% of patients. 🟢 This is the strongest argument for the idea that "depression" is really several conditions under one label — a point the psychologist Robert Pihl makes repeatedly: there are multiple depressions, and no single brain pattern, gene or chemical explains all of them. 🟡

**How common:** roughly 8% of US adults have a major depressive episode in a given year, and about one in five meet criteria at some point in life (NIMH; Hasin 2018). 🟢

## The chemical-imbalance story

**Where it came from.** In the 1950s, two drugs developed for other purposes (one for tuberculosis, one tested as an antipsychotic) were noticed to lift mood. Both raised monoamines — serotonin, norepinephrine, dopamine. A blood-pressure drug (reserpine) that depleted monoamines seemed to cause depression in some people. The inference: depression = too little monoamine. By the 1990s, SSRI marketing had compressed this into "a chemical imbalance — low serotonin." 🟢 (history)

**The logical flaw.** A drug relieving a condition does not show the condition was caused by a shortage of what the drug raises. Aspirin relieves headaches; headaches are not an aspirin deficiency. 🟢

**What testing found.**

- **Tryptophan depletion** (removing serotonin's building block for a few hours) does **not** make healthy people depressed. It lowers mood mainly in people who have been depressed before or have a family history. 🟢 So serotonin seems to matter for *vulnerability* in some people, not as a universal cause.
- A 2022 umbrella review (Moncrieff and colleagues, UCL) found **no consistent evidence** that depression is caused by low serotonin. 🟢 for "no simple deficiency" — this was already the mainstream research view.
- Critics replied that the review was too narrow and that serotonin *signalling* is still involved; a 2023 PET study found reduced serotonin *release* in depressed patients. 🔴 How much serotonin matters is contested. What is not contested: depression is not a simple shortage of one chemical.
- **What this does not mean:** it says nothing on its own about whether antidepressants help. That is a separate question with its own evidence ([[DEPRESSION — 10 Treatments Compared]]).

**Why the myth mattered.** It was meant to remove blame ("an illness, not weakness"). In an experiment, telling people their depression was caused by a chemical imbalance did **not** reduce self-blame — it made them more pessimistic about recovering and more likely to see medication as the only route. 🟡

## The current model: several systems, arrows both ways

Depression is best understood as a state that several interacting systems can push the brain into — and that, once there, pushes those systems further. Each gets its own note:

| System | What goes wrong | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Stress response | Cortisol rhythm flattens; threat system stays on | 02 |
| Genes and early life | Inherited and learned sensitivity to stress | 02 |
| Immune system | Inflammatory signals produce "sickness behaviour" | 03 |
| Brain energy | Mitochondria and insulin signalling underperform | 04 |
| Plasticity | Brain circuits lose the ability to rewire | 05 |
| Sleep and circadian clock | Fragmented sleep, disturbed REM, shifted rhythm | 06–07 |
| Hormones | Low testosterone, thyroid dysfunction | 08 |
| Thinking patterns | Negative view of self, world and future; rumination | 07, 10 |
| Social world | Loss, isolation, loss of role | 09 |

Two cautions run through the whole series:

- **Cause or effect?** Depressed people show raised cortisol, raised inflammation, smaller hippocampi, poor sleep. Some of these are causes, some are consequences of being depressed, most are both. 🟢
- **Different people, different weights.** For one person inflammation may dominate; for another, loss and sleep. This is why no single treatment works for everyone. 🟡

## Grief, depression, and prolonged grief disorder

Grief is not an illness. It is the normal response to losing someone the brain has wired in as part of its map of the world ([[DEPRESSION — 09 Grief Normal and Stuck]]). But grief can coexist with depression, and in a minority it gets stuck.

|  | Normal grief | Major depression | Prolonged grief disorder |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Core feeling | Yearning for the person | Pervasive emptiness, low mood | Intense yearning or preoccupation that does not ease |
| Pattern | Waves ("pangs") triggered by reminders | Constant, not tied to reminders | Constant, organised around the loss |
| Self-worth | Usually intact | Worthlessness, self-loathing | Identity disrupted ("part of me died") |
| Good moments | Still possible, mixed with sorrow | Largely absent | Rare |
| Thoughts of death | Often about joining the person | About own worthlessness or ending pain | About joining the person |
| Timing | Intensity fades over months | Any time | Diagnosed only after 12 months (DSM-5-TR) or 6 months (ICD-11) |

- Since 2013 the DSM no longer excludes depression diagnoses during bereavement: if full criteria are met, depression can be diagnosed even weeks after a death. Supporters said this stops real depression being dismissed as grief; critics said it medicalises normal sorrow. 🔴
- **Most people are resilient.** George Bonanno's long-term studies of bereaved people find the most common path is steady functioning with sadness that comes and goes — not a long collapse. About **one in ten** bereaved adults develop prolonged grief disorder. 🟢
- Prolonged grief and depression are separate conditions: they overlap, but respond to different treatments. 🟢
- Freud drew the same line in *Mourning and Melancholia* (1917): in mourning, the world feels emptied; in melancholia, the self does. A century later, the self-worth test is still the most useful single distinction. 🟡

## Screening and look-alikes

- **Screening questionnaires:** PHQ-9 for depression, GAD-7 for anxiety. Scores track severity and change over time; they do not make a diagnosis on their own. 🟢
- **Rule out medical causes first:** underactive thyroid, anemia, low B12 or folate, low testosterone, sleep apnea, uncontrolled blood sugar, and medication effects (corticosteroids, isotretinoin, interferon, some hormonal drugs). 🟢 The 5-alpha-reductase inhibitors used for prostate enlargement (finasteride, dutasteride) carry FDA label warnings for depression and suicidal thoughts. 🟡 based on post-marketing reports; causation not settled.
- **Men often look different:** irritability, anger, overwork, heavier drinking and physical complaints, rather than visible sadness. This is one reason depression in men is under-diagnosed (NIMH). 🟡

## When to get help now

Thoughts of suicide, inability to function at work or home, hearing or believing things others don't, or a sudden switch to little sleep with racing energy (possible mania, [[DEPRESSION — 12 Seasonal Depression and Bipolar Caution]]) → professional help promptly. In the US, call or text **988** (Suicide & Crisis Lifeline).

## Example case

An adult some months after the death of someone close. Waves of longing come with reminders — a song, a date, an empty chair — and pass. But there are also weeks of flat mood that come from nowhere, loss of interest in things that used to matter, guilt, and waking at 4am unable to get back to sleep.

Reading it through this note: the waves are grief. The flat weeks, anhedonia, guilt and early waking look more like a depressive episode running alongside it. Poor sleep feeds both. The questions that sort it out are duration, whether the low mood is tied to the loss or pervasive, whether self-worth has collapsed, and whether daily life is still working. This is a framework for understanding, not a diagnosis.

## WHAT TO HOLD ONTO

- Depression is a diagnosis by symptoms and time; there is no lab test.
- Five of nine symptoms, two weeks, including low mood or anhedonia — so the label covers very different people.
- "Chemical imbalance" is a marketing compression, not the science. Serotonin's exact role is still argued; a simple shortage is not.
- Several systems push into depression, and depression pushes back on them. Arrows run both ways.
- Grief comes in waves tied to the person; depression is pervasive and attacks self-worth. Most bereaved people are resilient; about one in ten get stuck.
