# DEPRESSION — 09 Grief: Normal and Stuck

*Grief is not an illness. It is the brain relearning a world without someone who was wired into it. This note covers how that relearning normally works, why it sometimes stalls, and what helps when it does.*

## Grief as learning

Mary-Frances O'Connor (*The Grieving Brain*) describes the core problem: the brain built a map in which the loved one is **here, now and close** — it predicts them at the table, on the phone, in bed. After a death, a second kind of knowledge says they are gone. The two coexist for a long time. 🟡

- Every reminder triggers the old prediction ("they'll be home soon"), followed by the error signal of absence. That collision is the **pang** of grief.
- Grieving is the slow process of updating the map — learning, through many repetitions, that the person is not "out there" to be found. 🟡
- In a brain-imaging study, people with prolonged grief showed **reward-system (nucleus accumbens) activity** when shown reminders of the dead person — as if the brain still expected reunion. Those adapting well did not. 🟡 (small study)
- This framing explains why grief is not linear and why it comes in **waves** triggered by reminders.

## The body grieves too

- **The widowhood effect:** mortality rises in the months after losing a spouse. Heart attack risk is sharply raised in the first days after the death of someone close. 🟢
- **Takotsubo ("broken heart") cardiomyopathy** — a temporary heart weakness triggered by acute emotional stress. 🟢
- Severe grief is associated with higher inflammation ([[DEPRESSION — 03 Inflammation and Sickness Behaviour]]), disturbed sleep and raised cortisol. 🟡

## Resilience is the norm

George Bonanno (*The Other Side of Sadness*) followed bereaved people before and after the loss and found distinct trajectories: 🟢

| Trajectory | Rough share | Pattern |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Resilience** | ~50–60% | Sadness and pangs, but daily functioning largely intact from early on |
| Recovery | ~15–20% | Significant distress that eases over a year or two |
| **Chronic grief** | ~10–15% | Severe distress that persists |
| Depressed–improved | ~10% | Depressed before the death (often during caregiving), better after |

Two older ideas did not survive testing:

- **The five stages** (denial, anger, bargaining, depression, acceptance) were never an empirical finding. In data, **yearning** is the dominant feeling from the start and acceptance rises steadily; there is no fixed order. 🟢
- **"Grief work"** — the idea that everyone must confront and work through every painful feeling — is not supported. Some resilient people do little of it. Moments of **laughter and positive emotion** during grief predict better adjustment, not denial. 🟡

## Oscillation and continuing bonds

- **Dual process model** (Stroebe and Schut): healthy grieving moves back and forth between **loss-oriented** time (missing, remembering, crying) and **restoration-oriented** time (new routines, roles, distraction, rest from grief). Getting stuck on either side is the problem. 🟡
- **Continuing bonds:** keeping an inner relationship with the dead person — talking to them, keeping objects, marking anniversaries — is normal and often helpful. This contrasts with Freud's view in *Mourning and Melancholia* (1917) that mourning means detaching emotional energy from the lost person. 🟡 Freud's lasting insight is the other half of that essay: in melancholia the person's **self-regard** collapses, which is still the most useful line between grief and depression ([[DEPRESSION — 01 What Depression Is]]).

## When grief gets stuck: prolonged grief disorder

Diagnosis (ICD-11 after **6 months**, DSM-5-TR after **12 months**): persistent, intense **yearning** for or **preoccupation** with the deceased, plus several of — identity disruption ("part of me died"), marked disbelief, avoidance of reminders, intense emotional pain, difficulty re-engaging with life, emotional numbness, feeling life is meaningless, intense loneliness — causing real impairment. 🟢

**About 1 in 10** bereaved adults meet criteria; more after sudden or violent deaths, the death of a child or spouse, or the COVID-era isolated deaths. 🟢

**Risk factors:** sudden, violent or preventable death; high dependency on the person; prior depression or anxiety; little social support; **avoidance** of reminders; **rumination** about why it happened. 🟡

## What helps when grief is stuck

- **Prolonged grief disorder therapy** (Katherine Shear): about 16 sessions combining grief-focused exposure (revisiting the story of the death), imaginal conversations with the deceased, and restoration goals. It outperformed standard interpersonal therapy (about **51% vs 28%** response). 🟢
- **Antidepressants** (citalopram) did **not** reduce grief symptoms themselves, but did help co-occurring depressive symptoms. 🟢 Medication treats the depression riding alongside, not the grief.
- Everyday supports: sleep protection, regular structure, physical activity, social contact that tolerates talking about the dead person, rituals and meaning-making (religious or not). 🟡
- **When grief and depression run together**, both need attention; treating the depression can make grieving possible again. 🟡
- Violent or traumatic deaths can add **PTSD** ([[DEPRESSION — 13 Trauma, Substances and Screens]]). 🟢

## Example case

Some months after a death: pangs at reminders, comfort in some memories, a few good days — that part is grief doing its work. But if months on there is constant yearning, avoidance of anything connected with the person, a sense that life has no point without them, and long stretches of flat mood with guilt and 4am waking, two things may be happening: grief that is stalling, and a depressive episode alongside it. Each has a distinct, evidence-based treatment. The useful questions are not "how long is normal" but "is life slowly re-opening, or narrowing?"

## WHAT TO HOLD ONTO

- Grief is the brain relearning that someone is gone; pangs are prediction meeting absence.
- Most bereaved people are resilient; about 1 in 10 get stuck.
- No fixed stages; yearning dominates and eases. Laughter in grief is healthy.
- Moving between loss and restoration is the healthy pattern; keeping a bond is normal.
- Prolonged grief has its own therapy; antidepressants treat the depression, not the grief.
