# DEPRESSION — 04 Brain Energy: Mitochondria and Insulin

*The brain is the most energy-hungry organ in the body. This note asks what happens to mood when its energy supply — mitochondria and insulin signalling — underperforms.*

## An expensive organ

The brain is about **2% of body weight** and uses about **20% of resting energy**. 🟢 Most of that goes to keeping neurons ready to fire and to rebuilding synapses. An energy shortfall shows up first in the most demanding functions: motivation, concentration, flexible thinking, mood regulation — which is to say, the symptoms of depression.

## Mitochondria: more than batteries

Mitochondria turn glucose and fat into ATP, the cell's energy currency. They also:

- start the synthesis of **steroid hormones**, including cortisol 🟢
- help regulate **neurotransmitter** release and recycling at synapses 🟢
- trigger **inflammation** when damaged (damaged mitochondrial fragments look like bacteria to the immune system) 🟡

In depression, studies find signs of mitochondrial strain: lower energy production in muscle and blood cells, altered mitochondrial DNA, and reduced glucose use in parts of the prefrontal cortex on PET scans. 🟡 Whether this causes depression or follows it is unresolved.

**Chris Palmer's ***Brain Energy*** proposes that all mental illness is a metabolic disorder of the brain, with mitochondria as the common pathway. It is a useful lens and a strong motivation to take metabolic health seriously. As a single unifying theory, it is **not established**. 🔴 His clinical evidence for ketogenic diets in serious mental illness comes from case series and small pilot studies (e.g., a 2024 Stanford pilot, 21 participants, no control group). 🟠

## Brain insulin resistance

Insulin is not only a blood-sugar hormone. In the brain it supports **synaptic plasticity** in the hippocampus, regulates **dopamine** clearance in reward circuits, and influences appetite and mood. 🟡

- People with type 2 diabetes have about **twice the rate** of depression. 🟢 (observational, consistent)
- In a Dutch cohort, people who developed insulin resistance were significantly more likely to develop a first major depression over the next nine years. 🟡
- The link runs both ways: depression worsens glucose control through cortisol, poor sleep, inactivity and diet. 🟢
- Psychologist Robert Pihl highlights registry data showing differences in glucose, triglycerides and cholesterol **10+ years before** a depression diagnosis. 🟡
- Benjamin Bikman's framing — insulin resistance as an upstream driver of many chronic diseases — applies here as a mechanism: high insulin and impaired insulin signalling travel with visceral fat and inflammation ([[DEPRESSION — 03 Inflammation and Sickness Behaviour]]). 🟡

## Diet: the strongest trial evidence

| Study | What it found | Evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **SMILES** (Jacka, 2017) — 67 adults with moderate–severe depression, modified Mediterranean diet coaching vs social support, 12 weeks | Remission **32% vs 8%** | 🟡 small, not blinded |
| HELFIMED (2017) | Mediterranean diet plus fish oil improved depression over 3 months | 🟡 |
| PREDIMED sub-analysis | Mediterranean diet linked to lower new-onset depression, especially in people with diabetes | 🟡 |
| Ultra-processed food umbrella review (BMJ, 2024) | Higher intake associated with more depression — rated "highly suggestive" | 🟡 observational |

Felice Jacka, who led SMILES, founded the field of **nutritional psychiatry**. Her own caution: the trials are small and diet works best as part of a broader plan, not as a replacement for treatment. 🟡

The working pattern: vegetables, fruit, legumes, whole grains, nuts, olive oil, oily fish two or more times a week; little processed meat, refined sugar or ultra-processed snacks. Regular meals with adequate protein matter more for energy and irritability than any single food. 🟡

## Other energy levers

- **Exercise** is the most powerful known stimulus for building new mitochondria and restoring insulin sensitivity — one reason it is antidepressant ([[DEPRESSION — 10 Treatments Compared]]). 🟢 for the metabolic effect.
- **Sleep loss** induces insulin resistance within days. 🟢
- **Creatine** (an energy buffer in brain and muscle) shows promise as an add-on to antidepressants in small trials, mostly in women. 🟠
- **Thyroid hormone** sets the metabolic rate of every cell, including neurons ([[DEPRESSION — 08 Hormones Testosterone and Thyroid]]).
- **GLP-1 drugs** (semaglutide and similar): early observational data are mixed — some show lower depression risk, others raised concerns. 🔴

## Example case

After a loss, eating often becomes irregular: skipped meals, convenience food, more sugar or alcohol in the evening. Combined with poor sleep and less movement, this can push insulin sensitivity down within weeks. The mood effect is not dramatic on any single day, but it lowers the floor: less energy, more irritability, foggier thinking. Regular meals with protein, a daily walk and protected sleep are low-cost ways to restore the brain's energy supply while the harder emotional work continues.

## WHAT TO HOLD ONTO

- The brain uses a fifth of the body's energy; mood and motivation are what fail first when supply drops.
- Mitochondria also make steroid hormones and trigger inflammation when damaged.
- Diabetes roughly doubles depression risk; insulin resistance predicts first episodes.
- "All mental illness is metabolic" is a hypothesis, not a finding.
- Mediterranean-style eating has the best trial evidence; the trials are small.
- Exercise and sleep are the main levers for brain energy.
