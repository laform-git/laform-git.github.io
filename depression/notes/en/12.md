# DEPRESSION — 12 Seasonal Depression and Bipolar Caution

*Two conditions where light and sleep are central — one where they are the treatment, one where the same tools can cause harm.*

## Seasonal depression

**Pattern.** Depressive episodes that start in autumn or winter and lift in spring, for at least two years running ("seasonal pattern" in DSM-5-TR). Winter episodes are typically **atypical**: oversleeping, carbohydrate craving, weight gain, heavy fatigue. 🟢

**Latitude.** Prevalence rises with distance from the equator — in one US survey from about 1% in Florida to about 9% in New Hampshire. 🟡 Milder "winter blues" are far more common.

**Mechanisms.** 🟡

- **Phase delay:** short days and weak morning light let the internal clock drift later than the sleep schedule (Alfred Lewy's hypothesis).
- **Longer melatonin signal** on long nights.
- **Serotonin transporter** activity rises in winter on PET scans, lowering available serotonin.

**Treatment.**

- **Bright light:** 10,000 lux for about 30 minutes within an hour of waking — response often within 1–2 weeks. 🟢 Light boxes should filter UV; sitting about arm's length away, not staring into it.
- **Dawn simulation** (gradually brightening bedroom light before waking). 🟡
- **CBT adapted for SAD** matched light therapy in the first winter and had fewer recurrences the following winter. 🟡
- **Bupropion XL** started in autumn is FDA-approved to prevent seasonal episodes. 🟢
- Vitamin D has not shown reliable benefit unless deficient. 🟡

## Bipolar disorder

**What it is.** Episodes of depression plus at least one episode of **mania** (bipolar I) or **hypomania** (bipolar II). About 1% of adults have bipolar I and a further 1–1.5% bipolar II or related forms. 🟢

**Mania / hypomania signs:** decreased need for sleep **without feeling tired**, racing thoughts, pressured speech, inflated self-confidence, unusual spending, risk-taking or sexual activity, irritability. Hypomania is milder and can feel like simply being productive. 🟢

**Why it matters for depression treatment.**

- Bipolar disorder often **first appears as depression**; the average delay to correct diagnosis is many years. 🟡
- Clues that a depression might be bipolar: onset before 25, many short episodes, family history of bipolar disorder, postpartum onset, psychotic features, atypical features, and especially **past hypomania on an antidepressant**. 🟡
- Antidepressants **alone** can trigger mania or rapid cycling in bipolar disorder; tricyclics and SNRIs carry higher switch risk than SSRIs or bupropion. 🟢
- Effective bipolar treatments are different: lithium (which also lowers suicide risk), quetiapine, lurasidone, lamotrigine. 🟢

**Light and sleep: the caution.** 🟢

- **Sleep loss is one of the strongest triggers of mania.** Even one short night can tip a vulnerable person.
- **Bright light therapy** and **sleep deprivation therapy** can induce mania — in bipolar depression, light is used at lower doses or at midday under supervision. 🟡
- **CBT-I sleep restriction** ([[DEPRESSION — 07 The Insomnia–Depression Loop and CBT-I]]) needs supervision for the same reason.
- Stable daily rhythm protects: **interpersonal and social rhythm therapy** stabilises wake time, meals and activity and reduces relapse. 🟡
- Blue-blocking glasses in the evening reduced manic symptoms as an add-on in a small trial. 🟠
- Cannabis and stimulants raise mania risk. 🟡

## Example case

Grief in a dark season compounds: shorter days weaken the clock just as loss disrupts sleep and routine. Morning light, fixed wake time and dim evenings help both. One question is worth asking before using bright light, sleep restriction or an antidepressant: has there ever been a period of days with little sleep, high energy and unusual behaviour? If so, the plan changes, and it belongs with a clinician.

## WHAT TO HOLD ONTO

- Seasonal depression is atypical in pattern and responds well to morning bright light.
- Bipolar disorder often starts as depression; past hypomania changes the treatment plan.
- Antidepressants alone can switch bipolar patients into mania.
- Sleep loss, bright light and sleep deprivation can trigger mania; stable rhythm protects.
