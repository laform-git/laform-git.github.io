# DEPRESSION — 06 Sleep and Emotion: REM as Overnight Therapy

*Sleep is not only rest. It is when the brain processes the emotional events of the day. This note covers what REM sleep does for mood, how depression distorts sleep, and why sleep itself can be used as treatment.*

## The one-night experiment

After a single night of sleep deprivation, healthy volunteers shown upsetting images had about **60% greater amygdala reactivity** than rested volunteers, and weaker connection between the amygdala and the prefrontal cortex that normally calms it. 🟡 (small study, n≈26, but consistent with later work)

The tired brain is an alarm with the brake disconnected — the same pattern as depression ([[DEPRESSION — 02 Stress, Cortisol, Genes and Early Adversity]]). Sleep-deprived people also swing more toward both negative reactions and impulsive reward-seeking. 🟡

## REM as "overnight therapy"

REM sleep (stage definitions in [[PERFECT SLEEP — 01 Sleep Architecture]]) has an unusual chemistry: emotional and memory regions (amygdala, hippocampus) are highly active, while **noradrenaline** — the brain's stress chemical — is almost completely switched off. It is the only time in the 24 hours this happens. 🟢

Matthew Walker's hypothesis: REM lets the brain **replay emotional memories without the stress chemistry**, keeping the information but stripping some of the emotional charge — "sleep to remember, sleep to forget." 🟡 Supporting evidence: emotional reactivity to an image tends to fall after a night with good REM. Contradicting evidence: several studies found REM *preserves* or even strengthens emotional reactions. 🔴 The idea is plausible and influential, not settled.

**PTSD fits the model:** in many people with PTSD, noradrenaline stays high during REM, nightmares replay trauma without softening it. 🟡 But a large trial of prazosin (a drug that blocks noradrenaline) for nightmares was negative. 🔴

**A note on Walker's *Why We Sleep*:** the mechanisms are generally sound; several headline claims are overstated or wrong (for example, some figures on sleep and mortality or cancer risk, and a mis-described WHO declaration). 🔴 Use the book for mechanisms, check the numbers.

## What depression does to sleep

Sleep changes are part of the diagnosis and among the most consistent biological findings in depression: 🟢

- **Short REM latency:** first REM arrives early (sometimes within 45–60 minutes instead of ~90).
- **More REM early in the night**, more intense eye movements, less deep sleep (N3).
- **Early-morning waking** with inability to return to sleep — characteristic of melancholic depression.
- In atypical and seasonal depression, the reverse: **oversleeping**.

This looks like a REM system that is overdriven — and that is one reason older theories proposed REM suppression as the antidepressant mechanism.

## REM suppression and antidepressants

- Most antidepressants (SSRIs, SNRIs, tricyclics, MAOIs) **suppress REM** and delay its onset. 🟢
- Selective REM deprivation was once shown to lift mood, and REM suppression was proposed as the core mechanism. 🟡
- But some effective antidepressants (bupropion, some others) do not suppress REM, so suppression is **not required**. 🟢
- REM suppression also has costs: vivid or disturbing dreams, REM rebound on stopping, and possible effects on emotional memory processing. 🟡 Details in [[DEPRESSION — 10 Treatments Compared]].

## Using sleep as treatment: chronotherapy

- **Total sleep deprivation** (staying awake one full night) produces rapid mood improvement in roughly **40–60%** of depressed patients — within a day. 🟢 Most relapse after the next night's sleep. 🟢
- **Triple chronotherapy** — one night awake, then several nights going to bed progressively earlier, plus morning bright light — can hold the gain for weeks. 🟡
- Why it works is unclear: resetting the clock, a surge of adenosine-linked plasticity, or reducing an overdriven REM system. 🟠
- **Caution:** sleep deprivation and bright light can trigger **mania** in bipolar disorder ([[DEPRESSION — 12 Seasonal Depression and Bipolar Caution]]). This is a supervised hospital treatment, not a home experiment.

## The clock matters as much as the sleep

Depression is associated with a **weakened, mistimed circadian rhythm**: flatter daily curves of body temperature, cortisol and melatonin, and often a delayed clock. 🟡 Fixed wake time, morning light and dim evenings strengthen the rhythm and are antidepressant in their own right ([[DEPRESSION — 10 Treatments Compared]]). 🟡

## Example case

In bereavement, sleep often breaks in two places: difficulty falling asleep as thoughts loop, and waking at 3–4am when REM is densest and dreams are most vivid. Dreams of the dead person are common and, for most people, more comforting than distressing. 🟡 If REM really does soften emotional memories, then protecting the second half of the night — the REM-rich hours — protects one of grief's natural processing tools. Alcohol, which suppresses REM and fragments the late night, works directly against it.

## WHAT TO HOLD ONTO

- One bad night makes the amygdala far more reactive and weakens the prefrontal brake.
- REM is the only time the brain processes emotion with stress chemistry switched off.
- "Overnight therapy" is a strong hypothesis, not a proven fact.
- Depression brings REM early and wakes people early; most antidepressants suppress REM.
- One night of sleep deprivation lifts mood in about half of depressed people — briefly, and not safely at home.
- Rhythm (fixed wake, morning light, dark evenings) matters as much as hours.
