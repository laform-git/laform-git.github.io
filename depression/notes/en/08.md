# DEPRESSION — 08 Hormones: Testosterone and Thyroid

*Several hormonal problems produce symptoms that look exactly like depression. Some should be ruled out before anything else; some interact with depression treatment. Cortisol is covered in [[DEPRESSION — 02 Stress, Cortisol, Genes and Early Adversity]]; insulin in [[DEPRESSION — 04 Brain Energy Mitochondria and Insulin]].*

## Testosterone

**What it does in the brain.** Androgen receptors are dense in the hippocampus, amygdala and hypothalamus. Testosterone influences motivation, drive, energy, libido and stress reactivity — partly directly and partly after being converted to **estradiol** (via the enzyme aromatase) or to neurosteroids that calm the brain. 🟡

**Low testosterone and mood.**

- Men with hypogonadism (clinically low testosterone with symptoms) have higher rates of depressive symptoms; fatigue, low libido, low motivation and poor concentration overlap heavily with depression. 🟡
- The overlap works both ways: depression, obesity, poor sleep, opioids and chronic illness all **lower** testosterone. 🟢
- Testosterone also falls with sleep loss — one week of 5-hour nights lowered daytime levels by 10–15% in young men. 🟡 (small study) See [[PERFECT SLEEP — 11 Hormones and Sleep]].

**Does treatment help mood?**

| Evidence | Finding |
| --- | --- |
| Meta-analysis of 27 RCTs (Walther, 2019, ~1,900 men) | Testosterone treatment reduced depressive symptoms modestly, mainly in men with low levels and at higher doses |
| TRAVERSE depression sub-study (2023, hypogonadal men with low-grade depressive symptoms) | **No significant improvement** in depressive symptoms vs placebo |
| Men with normal testosterone | No good evidence that adding testosterone treats depression |

The practical reading: in genuinely hypogonadal men, restoring testosterone may help energy, libido and mood modestly; it is **not an antidepressant** for men with normal levels. 🟡

**Too much or swinging levels.** Supraphysiological doses (as in anabolic steroid use) are associated with irritability, aggression and occasionally mania; stopping them causes withdrawal depression. 🟡 Large peaks and troughs between doses can produce mood swings. 🟠 Stable levels are the goal of therapy.

**Testing.** Diagnosis requires symptoms plus low **morning** total testosterone on **two separate days**, because levels peak in the morning and vary day to day. 🟢

## Thyroid

Thyroid hormone sets the metabolic rate of nearly every cell, including neurons. 🟢

- **Hypothyroidism** (underactive thyroid) causes fatigue, low mood, slowed thinking, weight gain, cold intolerance, constipation, dry skin — easily mistaken for depression. Screening with **TSH** (plus free T4 if abnormal) is standard before diagnosing depression. 🟢
- **Subclinical hypothyroidism** (slightly high TSH, normal T4): the link to depression is weak and inconsistent; treating it rarely fixes mood. 🟡
- **Thyroid antibodies** (autoimmune thyroiditis, Hashimoto's) are associated with depression and anxiety even when hormone levels are normal. 🟡
- **Hyperthyroidism** (overactive) causes anxiety, irritability, insomnia, palpitations — can mimic anxiety disorders or agitated depression. 🟢
- **T3 augmentation:** adding T3 (the active thyroid hormone) to an antidepressant can help people who have not responded, even with normal thyroid tests. In STAR*D's third step, T3 and lithium gave similar remission rates (roughly 25% vs 16%), with T3 better tolerated. 🟡
- **Lithium** can cause hypothyroidism — thyroid monitoring is standard with it. 🟢

## Vitamin D

Technically a steroid hormone.

- Low vitamin D is associated with depression in observational studies. 🟡
- In the large VITAL-DEP trial (~18,000 adults over 50), 2,000 IU/day for about 5 years did **not prevent** depression or improve mood in the general population. 🟢
- Supplementation may help people who are actually deficient. 🟡 Check the 25-OH vitamin D level rather than guessing.

## Women: the reproductive transitions (brief)

- Depression risk rises around **perimenopause** (roughly 2–4 times higher for new episodes), postpartum (10–15% of mothers) and premenstrually in some women. 🟡
- Zuranolone, a neurosteroid drug acting on GABA receptors rather than serotonin, is FDA-approved for postpartum depression. 🟢
- Hormonal contraceptives are associated with higher depression rates in some users, especially adolescents. 🟡

## Check before diagnosing depression

TSH (free T4 if abnormal), morning total testosterone on two days in men with symptoms, 25-OH vitamin D, HbA1c and fasting glucose, complete blood count, B12 and folate. 🟢 These are the standard medical rule-outs listed in [[DEPRESSION — 01 What Depression Is]].

## Example case

An adult in mid-life, grieving and sleeping badly, notices low energy, low drive and loss of interest. Some of this is grief, some may be a depressive episode — and some may be hormonal: sleep loss lowers testosterone, stress shifts thyroid and cortisol rhythms, and mid-life brings its own changes. Checking the hormonal floor (thyroid, testosterone where relevant, vitamin D, blood sugar) separates what can be corrected medically from what needs psychological and behavioural work. Where someone is already on hormone therapy, stable levels — not peaks and troughs — are what matter for mood.

## WHAT TO HOLD ONTO

- Low testosterone and depression overlap in symptoms and cause each other.
- Testosterone treatment helps mood modestly in truly low men at best; not an antidepressant for normal levels.
- Hypothyroidism mimics depression and must be ruled out; T3 can boost antidepressants.
- Vitamin D does not prevent depression in people who are not deficient.
- Reproductive transitions raise depression risk in women.
