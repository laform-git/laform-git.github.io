# DEPRESSION — 07 The Insomnia–Depression Loop and CBT-I

*Insomnia is not just a symptom of depression; it is one of its strongest causes and the commonest thing left over after recovery. This note explains the loop and the treatment that breaks it.*

## The numbers

- People with insomnia have about **twice the risk** of developing depression later (meta-analysis of 21 studies). 🟢
- Insomnia that persists after a depressive episode remits is one of the strongest predictors of **relapse**. 🟢
- Insomnia is the **most common residual symptom** in people whose depression has otherwise responded to treatment. 🟢
- In a longitudinal study the psychologist Robert Pihl describes (~500 people, ~6 years), early sleep problems were the strongest predictor; combined with **repetitive negative thinking**, they led to depression. 🟡

## How the loop works

**Hyperarousal.** Chronic insomnia is less about low sleep drive than an over-alert brain: higher evening cortisol, faster heart rate, more high-frequency brain activity even during sleep. 🟡 The brain stays partly on guard.

**Conditioned arousal.** After enough nights lying awake, the bed itself becomes a cue for wakefulness and worry — the classic sign is falling asleep on the sofa, then waking fully on getting into bed. 🟢

**Rumination.** Night is when distraction disappears. Repetitive negative thinking (going over losses, mistakes, the future) raises arousal, which blocks sleep, which weakens the prefrontal brake on negative thought the next day ([[DEPRESSION — 06 Sleep and Emotion REM as Overnight Therapy]]). 🟡

**Biology.** Short or broken sleep raises inflammation ([[DEPRESSION — 03 Inflammation and Sickness Behaviour]]), lowers BDNF and worsens insulin sensitivity — three depression pathways at once. 🟢

## The 3P model: why insomnia outlives its cause

Arthur Spielman's model explains why insomnia often continues long after the event that started it: 🟢

|  | What it is | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Predisposing** | Baseline vulnerability | Anxious temperament, light sleeper, family history, older age |
| **Precipitating** | The trigger | Bereavement, illness, job loss, a medical procedure |
| **Perpetuating** | Habits adopted to cope that keep it going | Going to bed early or staying in bed late "to catch up", napping, clock-watching, alcohol as a sleep aid, irregular wake times, worrying about sleep |

The trigger often fades. The **perpetuating habits** keep the insomnia going, and they are the target of treatment.

## CBT-I: the first-line treatment

Cognitive behavioural therapy for insomnia is recommended **first-line for chronic insomnia, ahead of sleeping pills**, by the American College of Physicians and European guidelines. 🟢 It typically takes 4–8 sessions. Components:

1. **Sleep restriction** — limit time in bed to roughly the actual average sleep time (with a floor, usually around 5–5.5 hours), at a fixed wake time. This builds sleep pressure and consolidates sleep; time in bed is extended as efficiency improves. The most powerful component, and the hardest for the first two weeks. 🟢
2. **Stimulus control** — bed only for sleep (and sex); if awake for about 20 minutes, get up, do something calm in dim light, return when sleepy. Same wake time every day, no matter the night. 🟢
3. **Cognitive work** — challenging beliefs such as "if I don't sleep 8 hours, tomorrow is ruined," which feed arousal. 🟡
4. **Wind-down and relaxation** — a buffer hour before bed; scheduled "worry time" earlier in the evening to empty the mind on paper. 🟡
5. **Sleep hygiene** — weak on its own; useful only as part of the package. 🟢

**Effects:** benefits equal to sleeping pills in the short term and better after treatment stops. 🟢 Digital programmes (and the free VA app *CBT-i Coach*) show solid effects. 🟢

**Effect on depression:** CBT-I reduces depressive symptoms even when depression is not targeted. 🟢 In a trial of older adults with insomnia, CBT-I **halved** the rate of new depression over three years (about 12% vs 26%). 🟢 (one large RCT)

**Cautions:** sleep restriction causes temporary sleepiness — care with driving. It is not appropriate unsupervised in **bipolar disorder** (sleep loss can trigger mania, [[DEPRESSION — 12 Seasonal Depression and Bipolar Caution]]), seizure disorders, or untreated sleep apnea.

## Physical causes of waking

CBT-I treats the brain's contribution. It does not fix **physical** causes of waking — sleep apnea, a full bladder at night, reflux, pain, restless legs, hot flushes. These need their own diagnosis and treatment ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 02 Nocturia and Broken Sleep]]). 🟢 Even then, CBT-I principles help with the part that is behavioural: how quickly sleep returns after a waking.

## Sleeping pills

Z-drugs and benzodiazepines produce sedation, not normal sleep architecture, carry dependence, falls and next-day impairment, and are recommended only short-term. 🟢 Low-dose sedating antidepressants (trazodone, mirtazapine) are widely used; evidence for trazodone in insomnia is modest. 🟡 Details in [[PERFECT SLEEP — 09 Sleep Medications and Aids]].

## Example case

After a death, many people go to bed earlier to escape the evening, lie awake replaying events, nap in the afternoon, and use a drink to get to sleep. Months later the grief has softened, but the sleep has not: these perpetuating habits are now running the insomnia. The CBT-I approach — fixed wake time, less time in bed, out of bed when awake, a worry list written before bed, no alcohol as a sleep aid — targets exactly these, and in doing so lowers the risk that the grief tips into a depressive episode.

## WHAT TO HOLD ONTO

- Insomnia doubles depression risk and predicts relapse.
- Chronic insomnia is an over-alert brain plus a bed that has become a cue for wakefulness.
- The trigger fades; the coping habits keep the insomnia going.
- CBT-I is first-line, beats pills long-term, and reduces depression.
- Sleep restriction is the strongest component — and needs care in bipolar disorder.
- Physical causes of waking need their own treatment.
