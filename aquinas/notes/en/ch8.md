
# Barron — Thomas Aquinas, Ch.8: Aquinas on Ethics & Happiness
*Aquinas's ethics starts with happiness, not law: an analysis of desire, four false candidates for beatitudo (wealth, honor, power, pleasure), God as the only good that satisfies, the cardinal virtues, and the eternal–natural–positive law cascade.*

*Source: Bishop Robert Barron, Peterson Academy course "Thomas Aquinas," Lecture 8 (transcript). Final lecture of eight; it closes the course's exitus-reditus arc by treating ethics as the return to God. Transcript complete: it ends with Barron's closing blessing.*

## Why This Matters
This is the payoff lecture of the course. The first seven lectures followed the *exitus*, everything coming forth from God (God, attributes, Trinity, creation, providence, the human person). Ethics is the *reditus*: how a human being, body and soul, made in God's image, gets back to God. Barron's main move is to flip the common picture of Catholic morality. It does not start with prohibitions. It starts with happiness, the one thing everyone wants, and only reaches law at the very end, as a tool for building the virtues that lead to happiness.

## Starting Point — Teleology, Not Prohibition
- Thomas's ethics is **teleological** (Greek *telos*, "goal" or end), following Aristotle. The question is: how do we get back to the one from whom we came? ✅
- The return is not a dull cycle like Nietzsche's eternal return. Things come forth good, sin interrupts, and God brings everything back **enhanced**. Because God has no need of the world, everything God does is for the world's good, so the return is an enhancement of our humanity. ✅
- Barron expects listeners to assume a Catholic ethicist will "say no, especially about sex." He answers that Thomas affirms the goodness of sexuality (callback to the anthropology lecture) and is "not an Augustinian or Platonist" on this. 🟡 (Barron's contrast; a broad characterization of Augustine)
- **Where law shows up:** in the second part of the *Summa*, law is first treated in **Question 90**, after 89 questions and "hundreds of pages." So Catholic morality as "all about law" is the wrong picture, at least for the Church's "common doctor." ✅
- Golf-teacher analogy: a coach says "no" a lot, not because he enjoys it, but because you are after excellence. The "no's" serve the goal.

## The Analysis of Desire — Everything Ends at Happiness
- Contrast with Buddhism (Barron says he is "no great expert"): nirvana as blowing out the candle of desire. Christianity **directs** desire rather than extinguishing it. Desire is good and from God; it just gets disordered. 🟡 (his summary of Buddhism, offered secondhand)
- Teilhard de Chardin: you would not get out of bed in the morning unless you believed in God. ✅ (as quoted)
- **The "Russian doll" method:** take any desire and keep asking why. Why attend this lecture? To learn about Aquinas. Why? To learn about religion. Why? It touches the deepest things. Why? Meaning, purpose, happiness. Every chain ends at "because it makes me happy." Barron runs the same chain on golf (reaches happiness fast, since golf is sought largely for its own sake), on teaching this course, and on eating breakfast cereal.
- **Jefferson** gave a right to "the pursuit of happiness" but never defined it. The tradition, by contrast, cares a great deal about defining what beatitudo actually is.
- **Holy Grail:** Barron links the medieval Grail quests (emerging around Thomas's time) to the search for ultimate happiness: the will seeking its great goal through proximate desires, sometimes down wrong paths, sometimes stopping too soon. 🟡 (his interpretive link)

## The Four False Candidates for Beatitudo
Aquinas tests candidates for the ultimate good (Barron cites Question 2 of the second part of the *Summa*).

| Candidate | Why it fails (Aquinas, as Barron presents him) | Barron's images |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Wealth** | Artificial wealth (money) only symbolizes natural wealth; natural wealth (food, clothing, shelter, transport) serves bodily contentment, so it is a means, not the end | Midas; Tolkien's dragon on its gold; Chicago skyline |
| **Honor, fame, glory** | Honor is a "flag of virtue" meant to point others to a good deed; human judgment is fickle and often wrong | Chesterton's little circling train; Paul Simon's "every generation throws a hero off the pop charts" |
| **Power** | Always subordinated to an end beyond itself, so it is ambiguous: good or bad depending on use | The One Ring tempting even Gandalf |
| **Bodily pleasure** | "A trifle compared to the good of the soul" | The hedonist tradition; "greatest pleasure for the greatest number" |

### Wealth
- Aquinas on possessions: "For when we already possess them, we despise them and seek others... this very fact shows that they are imperfect and that the sovereign good does not consist therein." ✅
- The addictive pattern: the house, the car, a few months of contentment, then the bigger house across town.
- **Joseph Campbell's skyline observation:** the tallest building in ancient and medieval towns was the church; in the Renaissance, palaces and civic buildings; today, towers of wealth. In Chicago the three tallest (Willis/Sears, John Hancock, Aon) are insurance companies, and the cathedral where Barron was ordained is dwarfed. 🟡 (Barron's description; whether all three are strictly "insurance companies" is his framing)
- Paul Tillich: the concupiscent desire to "cram the whole world into your mouth." You would still be hungry.

### Honor and glory
- Nothing wrong with honor itself. The sickness is getting **addicted to the flag** instead of the good it marks.
- Aquinas: "Virtue's true reward is happiness itself, for which the virtuous work. Whereas if they work for honor, it would be no longer a virtue, but rather ambition." ✅
- Ambrose (cited by Thomas): happiness cannot lie in being praised, because human beings are fleeting and often mistaken. Thomas: "human knowledge often fails, especially in contingent singulars such as our human acts. For this reason, human glory is frequently deceptive." ✅
- Psychological point: if you live for glory, your happiness is "utterly in the hands of other people." The saints cultivate detachment, which Barron calls *insouciance*, "without care" about what people think, because they know what they seek.

### Power
- The ring of power scene: for a moment Gandalf (Ian McKellen) wants the ring, which Barron calls the most frightening scene in the films.
- Once you know the real end, you will know how to use power, fame and wealth well. Mistaking them for the end is where trouble starts. ✅

### Pleasure
- Aquinas: "that good which is fitting to the body, and which causes bodily delight, is not man's perfect good, but is quite a trifle as compared with the good of the soul." ✅
- Barron is careful: this takes nothing back from Thomas's reverence for the body. The soul's pleasures (knowledge, virtue, union with others and with God) simply outweigh it.

## The Answer — Only God Satisfies the Will
- Parallel to the mind: the intellect wants to know everything about everything; the will wants not a particular good but **goodness itself**. ✅
- Aquinas: "The object of the will is the universal good... nothing can lull the human will but the universal good. This is to be found not in any creature, but in God alone, because every creature has goodness only by participation. Thus, God alone can satisfy the will of the human being." ✅
- **Sin as addiction:** we are hungry for God but put wealth, honor, pleasure or power in God's place. The buzz wears off, faster each time, and we need more. Barron's timings ("about 15 minutes," "11 minutes," "ten minutes") are rhetorical. He names pornography addiction as a pastoral reality that exploits exactly this dynamic, and he says honor-addiction can destroy a life as surely as alcohol or pornography. 🟡 (pastoral observation, not data)
- **John of the Cross** (Carmelite, a couple of centuries after Thomas, who studied him closely): we have an "infinite cavern" that only the infinite God can fill, yet we keep throwing worldly things into it. Barron says John "learned that from Thomas Aquinas." 🟡 (influence claim as Barron states it)
- **A teleological argument for God:** earlier lectures argued from efficient causality (unmoved mover, uncaused cause). This one argues from **final causality**: there is no infinite regress of desires, so something ultimately motivates every act of the will, and that is God. 🟡 (Barron frames it as "another argument for God's existence")
- **The hard principle:** what we most want, we grasp only inchoately in this life. "By nature, I'm ordered to something supernatural." It can be anticipated (the Mass as heaven on earth in one sense) but not possessed this side of death, which makes life "wonderful and painful," a holy longing. ✅

## The Virtues — How to Get There
Virtues are inner capacities that order us properly toward an end. Barron draws them from Aristotle, with Thomas's own spin.

| Virtue | Role in Barron's scheme |
| --- | --- |
| **Justice** | Doing the right thing; Plato's "rendering to each his due" |
| **Temperance** | Holds off **inner** resistance to justice: disordered emotions and attachments |
| **Courage** | Holds off **external** threats to justice: attack, harm, being targeted |
| **Prudence** | Practical moral know-how: what the just act looks like here and now (the quarterback reading the defense and calling an audible) |

✅ Barron presents these four only; the theological virtues are not discussed in this lecture.

## Law — The Last Piece, Not the First
- Barron notes he has "not wagged my finger once" until now. Law **disciplines action so as to inculcate the virtues**, which order us to beatitudo. Parenting example: a child who cannot yet grasp the whole picture gets rules that habituate him toward virtue. ✅

| Level | What it is | Example from the lecture |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Eternal law** | The mind of God: what God knows and wills, and God wills our beatitudo | — |
| **Natural law** | Moral principles "written, as it were, in our nature": basic goods like knowledge, sociability, religion, arts, friendship (Barron's list) | Protecting life |
| **Positive law** | Concrete written laws of a society | 65 mph limit, drug laws, taxes |

- Just positive law must be grounded in natural law, which rests on eternal law. Hence Aquinas would answer "don't legislate morality" with: that is all you ever legislate. Even a speed limit protects life. ✅
- **Martin Luther King, Letter from Birmingham City Jail:** replying to sympathetic white pastors who urged him to slow down, King argued the Jim Crow laws were repugnant to the natural law and so to the eternal law, and cited Aquinas. Barron calls it a distinctively American and explicitly Catholic text, and says he cited it at the Library of Congress. ✅

## The Close — Off the Wheel of Fortune
- The **Wheel of Fortune** on Gothic cathedrals: a king at the top; a falling man saying *Regnavi* ("I have reigned"); a pauper at the bottom, *Sum sine regno* ("I am without a kingdom"); a climber, *Regnabo* ("I shall reign"). That is life lived for wealth, pleasure, power and honor: the wheel always turns. ✅
- At the center of the wheel is **Christ**, "the same yesterday, today, and forever." Don't live on the rim; live at the center. The whole Thomistic program, law to virtue to beatitudo, is meant to move us there.
- Final frame: the course itself imitated *exitus-reditus*. "You could know as much as Thomas Aquinas about God, but if you're stuck on the Wheel of Fortune, what good is it to you?" The moral path, Thomas's *via*, is the way back to God.

## Punchlines & Quotable Facts (Lecture 8)
- **Law first appears at Question 90.** Catholic ethics begins with happiness, not prohibition.
- **"Everybody wants beatitudo."** Every chain of desire ends at "because it makes me happy."
- **Honor is a "flag of virtue."** The sickness is being addicted to the flag.
- **"God alone can satisfy the will of the human being."** Every creature is good only by participation.
- **"By nature, I'm ordered to something supernatural."** The longing cannot be fulfilled in this world.
- **Temperance guards against inner threats, courage against outer ones, prudence reads the moment.**
- **"That's all you ever legislate: morality."** Positive law rests on natural law, which rests on eternal law.
- **Don't live on the rim of the wheel.** Christ is the still center.

## Key Corrections to Carry Forward
- 🔴 "Catholic morality is mostly rules and no's": in the *Summa*, law comes 89 questions in; ethics starts with beatitudo.
- 🔴 "Christianity wants to kill desire": it orders desire to its true end; desire is from God.
- 🔴 "Wealth, honor, power or pleasure is the goal": each is a real good, but a partial one; treated as ultimate, each becomes addictive.
- 🔴 "Honor is bad": honor is fine as a flag of virtue; the error is chasing the flag.
- 🔴 "You can't legislate morality": on Thomas's view every just law encodes a moral good.
- 🔴 "Full happiness is available in this life": it can be anticipated (liturgy) but only completed beyond death, by grace.

## Practical Takeaway
Study-level takeaways: (1) run the "Russian doll" test on a desire when it feels urgent and see where it ends; (2) when a good stops satisfying and you want "more," Barron reads that as the cavern speaking, not as a sign you need a bigger dose; (3) use the four virtues as a diagnostic: is the problem knowing the right thing (prudence), inner pull (temperance), or outer fear (courage)?; (4) read rules as scaffolding for virtue rather than as the point.

## Questions to Learn From (Socratic) — with Answer Options
**1. Given where law appears in the *Summa*, what does Barron say this shows about Aquinas's ethics?**
A. Law is the foundation, and everything before Question 90 is preliminary metaphysics
B. \* Ethics begins with beatitudo and the virtues; law comes late, as a means of forming virtue toward happiness
C. Aquinas thought law was unimportant for the moral life
*Why B: law appears at Question 90, after 89 questions, and Barron presents it as what "inculcates the virtues, which in turn order me toward beatitudo."*

**2. Given Aquinas's account of honor as a "flag of virtue," which statement best captures the problem?**
A. \* Honor is legitimate as a signal pointing others to real good; the disorder is becoming addicted to the flag instead of the virtue it marks
B. Honor is always sinful and should be refused
C. Honor is the true reward of virtue, so pursuing it is the right motive
*Why A: Thomas says virtue's true reward is happiness itself; working for honor turns virtue into ambition, and praise is often mistaken anyway.*

**3. Given Barron's four virtues, which one addresses a person who knows the right thing but fears being attacked for doing it?**
A. Temperance, because it orders the emotions
B. Prudence, because it reads the situation
C. \* Courage, because it holds off external threats to justice
*Why C: in Barron's scheme temperance handles inner resistance, courage handles outer threats, and prudence works out what the right act looks like here and now.*

**4. Given Barron's reading of the Letter from Birmingham City Jail, why could King refuse to "wait"?**
A. Because positive law has no authority at all
B. \* Because the Jim Crow laws contradicted the natural law, and therefore the eternal law, so they lacked the grounding that makes positive law just
C. Because Aquinas taught that any unpopular law may be ignored
*Why B: on the cascade Barron describes, positive law is just only when it reflects natural law, which rests on eternal law; King appealed to Aquinas on exactly this point.*

## Open Threads
- ✅ **Transcript complete:** it ends with the exitus-reditus wrap-up and "God bless you all."
- **Grace and the supernatural end:** Barron says beatitudo is reached "only through the grace of God" but does not develop grace, the theological virtues (faith, hope, charity), or how they relate to the cardinal ones. Worth reading alongside Thomas's own treatise.
