
# Barron — Thomas Aquinas, Ch.7: Aquinas's Anthropology
*Aquinas's account of the human being: body-soul unity over Platonic dualism, the soul as form of the body, the agent intellect's grasp of immaterial form as the case for the soul's immortality, bodily resurrection, and the imago Dei as the mind's restless hunger for God.*

*Source: Bishop Robert Barron, Peterson Academy course "Thomas Aquinas," Lecture 7 (transcript). Seventh of eight lectures; follows Providence and the Problem of Evil (Ch.6) and sets up Ethics & Happiness (Ch.8). Transcript complete; it ends on Psalm 42 with no formal wrap-up, consistent with Barron's continuous-talk style.*

## Why This Matters
This is the lecture where the course turns from God to us. After the approach to God, the attributes, the Trinity, creation and providence, Barron asks what kind of creature God provides for in a distinctive way: the one made in His image. Aquinas's answer is a careful middle path between two errors that still shape how people talk about themselves: Platonic dualism (the "real me" is a soul trapped in a body) and a reductive view that collapses the soul into matter. One section is heavy on epistemology (how the mind abstracts form); it is the hinge of the whole argument for the soul's immortality, so it gets a table.

## Opening Frame — Two Loose Ends
- **The grandfather from Lecture 6:** the man whose two granddaughters had a terrible disease. Barron told him, "I don't have an answer," but "the quest is a holy one," because he was wrestling with God like Job, not rejecting Him. ✅
- **The Bible's posture on suffering:** we live between God's fidelity and God's inscrutability. Image: a torn single page of a great novel scattered to the wind, read without knowing the chapter or the book. ✅
- **Aquinas the biblical man:** nine years in a Benedictine monastery steeped in the Psalms; clinging to his Dominican habit when his brother tried to rip it off; a year imprisoned by his family, memorizing large parts of Scripture; daily Mass, then assisting at a second, rarely without "copious tears." His philosophy is always in service of revelation. ✅
- **Anselm's definition of theology:** *fides quaerens intellectum*, "faith seeking understanding." Faith comes first; the restless mind then uses philosophy and the sciences to understand it more deeply. ✅

## Where Anthropology Sits in Aquinas's Order
Barron recaps the sequence: approach to God → attributes → Trinity → creation (*bonum diffusivum sui*, "the good is diffusive of itself," so creation comes from generosity, not need) → providence (God in all things by essence, presence and power). Humans receive a particular providence because we are made in God's image and likeness: not in the body, since God has none, but in mind, spirit and soul. ✅
- Barron's thesis: to be human is, at the deepest level, to be **ordered to God**, "haunted by God," drawn like a magnet. He says the atheists he has debated are often the most haunted. 🟡 (his pastoral observation)
- Against secularism: denying this ordering moves people into "a very bad psychological and spiritual state"; discovering the image and ordering it to its source is "coming alive." 🟡 (his judgment)

## Genesis — Clay and Breath
- God molds a figurine from clay, then breathes His spirit into it. Barron stresses that the Bible speaks here in poetry and metaphor, not scientific or philosophical language. ✅
- **The clay:** we are of the earth, related to "our animal cousins": hungry, thirsty, tired, feeling pleasure and pain. Don't deny we are animals. ✅
- **The breath:** Hebrew *ruach*, Greek *pneuma*, Latin *spiritus*. Not the air in the lungs (that belongs to the clay) but the spiritual dimension that cannot be reduced to the physical. ✅
- The basic question of all philosophical anthropology, per Barron: how do we think together this hybrid of earth and spirit? Evidence for the spiritual side: abstract thought, theoretical mathematics, designing complex machines, aspiring to God.

## Two Traditions on Body and Soul

|  | Dualist line (Plato → Descartes) | Aristotle | Aquinas |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Image of the soul** | Prisoner in a prison; later "ghost in a machine" | Form of the body: its organizing, energizing pattern (*energeia*) | Form of the body, present in every part "as God is present to the entire universe" |
| **The body** | Obstacle tied to a passing world | Intimately one with the soul | Good, from God; to be cherished "by a friendship of love" |
| **Death** | The prison break; philosophy is "a rehearsal for death" | Open question: does the soul survive? | The soul survives (it is immaterial) but awaits the resurrection of the body |
| **Danger** | Too much dualism | Reducing the soul to the body | Walks between both |

### The dualist trajectory as Barron sketches it
- **Plato:** the soul deals with perfect Forms; the body ties us to the passing sensible world. Socrates calmly drinks the hemlock because death is the "prison break" he lived for. ✅
- **Gnostics** (2nd to 4th centuries): a divine spark imprisoned in matter as punishment; the material world made by a lower, fallen deity; hence asceticism and deep suspicion of sex and reproduction. Irenaeus fought them. ✅
- **Albigensians:** neo-Gnostics in southern France; the Dominicans were founded in part to combat them, so Aquinas's own order was born in this fight. ✅
- **Descartes:** methodic doubt, *cogito ergo sum*, and a split between *res cogitans* (thinking thing) and *res extensa* (extended things). "Ghost in the machine" is a later philosopher's label (Barron doesn't name him; it is usually credited to Gilbert Ryle 🟡). Barron says modern philosophy is "haunted" by this picture.
- **Today:** Barron argues that "the real me is a woman, but I'm stuck in this man's body" is the same ancient dualism resurfacing in gender ideology. 🟡 (his cultural argument, stated polemically)

### Aristotle's correction and the worry it raised
Aristotle, at Plato's Academy for 20 years, called the soul the **form of the body**: not external shape but the energizing pattern that makes this body what it is. ✅ When his texts reached the West, critics thought him shaky on God, on creation, and especially on the soul. If Plato's danger is too much dualism, Aristotle's is collapsing the soul into the body, which raises the Christian question: does the soul survive death?

### Aquinas's choice
He sides with Aristotle because Genesis says everything God made is good, so the body is not a prison. For a thousand years Christian thinkers had mostly used Plato; Barron, who reveres Augustine, calls him "somewhat problematically Platonic" on this point. 🟡 (Barron's reading; Augustine scholarship would nuance it) Aquinas then brings "something of Plato" back in to secure the soul's immateriality and immortality. Barron calls this "one of the most deft dances" of his intellectual life.

## The Aristotelian Side — Unity of Body and Soul
Barron's texts, as quoted in the lecture:
- **ST I, q.75:** "A particular human being is composed of this soul, of this flesh, and of these bones." The real me is all of it. ✅
- **Commentary on 1 Corinthians:** ***Anima mea non sum ego*, "I am not my soul."** Barron calls it revolutionary for the 13th century and aims it at today's "real me in here" language. ✅
- **ST I, q.93:** "The whole human soul is in the whole body and also in every part of the body, just as God is present to the entire universe." ✅
- "The soul is in the body, **not as contained by it, but rather as containing it.**" (no reference given 🟡) Barron offers it as "a point for meditation for the rest of the month."
- **"We ought to cherish the body"** (Barron cites "the second part of the Summa, Question 25"): our body's substance is "not from an evil principle, as the Manichees imagined, but from God," so we cherish it "by a friendship of love." Tied to the Louis IX banquet story: "That will settle the Manichees." ✅
- **Against Origen:** Origen held that souls pre-existed in a spiritual realm and landed in bodies because of sin. Aquinas had "no time for that": we are not here as punishment. ✅ Barron also warns that too much Christian spirituality turns into chastising the body.
- **Sexuality (*Summa contra Gentiles* III):** natural inclinations come from God; all perfect animals have a natural inclination to carnal union; "therefore, it's impossible for carnal union to be evil in itself." ✅ Barron contrasts this with Augustine, whom he describes as seeing sexuality as fallen and tolerated for procreation. 🟡 (his characterization)
- **Knowledge through the senses:** Aquinas's approach to God is *a posteriori*. Contrast Plotinus, who sought to extricate the soul from the body to be "alone with the alone." Barron's shorthand: Plato points up to the Forms, Aristotle points out to the world. ✅
- **Sacraments:** why both matter (water, oil, bread, wine, hands) and form (the words)? "Because we have bodies and souls." If we were angels, we wouldn't need sacraments. ✅
- **Resurrection:** heaven is not escape from the body ("That's Plato. That ain't the Bible"). The risen Christ ate and drank with the disciples (Acts 10), ate fish before them (Luke), and said "a ghost doesn't have flesh and bones as I do." Barron folds the invitation to Thomas into the Luke scene; the touching of the wounds by Thomas is in John 20. 🟡 *Compendium Theologiae*: the soul will resume "a human body made up of flesh and bones and equipped with the same organs it now possesses." ✅

## The Platonic Side — Why the Soul Is Immaterial
The objection Barron raises himself: has Aquinas tied soul and body so tightly that the soul loses its spiritual integrity and its immortality? The answer runs through Thomistic epistemology, "a philosophy of knowledge."

| Step | Term | What happens | Barron's example |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Senses | Eyes take in data from the world | Looking at the podium |
| 2 | *Phantasm* | The data is impressed on memory as an image | Remembering his mother and father, his first day of school |
| 3 | *Intellectus agens* (agent intellect) | Asks *quid sit?* ("What is it?") and abstracts the form, the intelligible pattern, from the particular | "That's a podium"; the chemist's "That's H₂O" |
| 4 | Passive intellect | Receives the abstracted form: understanding, insight | Seeing what every possible podium shares |

- **The argument:** when the mind grasps a pure form, it leaves the world of particular sensible things. The number seven is not the drawn 7, not seven objects, not the word S-E-V-E-N; those are symbols of what is understood. The quadratic formula has no color, shape or size. 2 + 2 = 4 is true here, on Mars and "in any possible world." ✅
- **Conclusion:** if the mind were "just brain firings," it could not reach this level; it does, so the mind in its essential structure is **immaterial**. Barron grants that the brain could explain sense experience and, "I guess," memory, but not intellection at this level. ✅ (the standard Thomistic argument as he presents it)
- **Plato got this right:** in the cave parable, the mediating step before leaving the cave is mathematics, which opens "a properly spiritual world." ✅
- **Immortality follows:** the body sickens and disintegrates, but because of its immaterial quality the soul survives the death of the body. Aquinas is "an Aristotelian Platonist... a Platonic Aristotelian," all in service of Genesis and the resurrection. ✅
- **But the separated soul is not the full human:** "A disembodied soul is not a human being," so it is "not really" happy without its body. The consummation is the soul reunited to an elevated, resurrected body. Eschatology is deferred to "another day." ✅
- **Near-death experiences:** Barron finds the modern research interesting and tells of a patient who, out of his body, saw a red gym shoe on a windowsill on the far side of the hospital, later found there. His verdict: measurable data about the soul surviving? "Yeah, maybe." 🟡 (anecdote, no source given)

## The Imago Dei — The Restless Intellect
- Genesis's image and likeness "comes roaring up into the tradition": something in us is like God and therefore wants to return to God. ✅
- **Aquinas (quoted, no reference given):** "Our intellect never understands so many things that it could not understand more. ✅
- The agent intellect never rests: podium → artifact → material thing → matter → ... Each pattern makes it want a wider one. Bernard Lonergan (20th-century Thomist): the mind wants "to know everything about everything." ✅
- **The endpoint:** not another true thing but truth itself, not any particular being but *ipsum esse*, being itself. In Christian terms, the **beatific vision**. That restlessness is the imago Dei at work. ✅
- Heidegger's *die Frage nach dem Sein* ("the question after being") translated as "What's it all about?" That question is "the hunger for God"; don't let secularism suppress it.
- **Barron's analogy:** *Close Encounters of the Third Kind*, where people marked by the light become obsessed, sculpting a mountain they don't understand, until a few reach Devil's Tower for the meeting. "We're marked by God... and we won't rest until we find God."

## Punchlines & Quotable Facts (Lecture 7)
- ***Anima mea non sum ego*: "I am not my soul."** The real me is body and soul together.
- **"The soul is in the body, not as contained by it, but as containing it."** The anti-prison image.
- **Plato: prisoner in a prison. Descartes: ghost in a machine. Aristotle and Aquinas: form of the body.**
- **"We ought to cherish the body by a friendship of love."** Against the Manichees.
- **"If we were angels, we wouldn't need sacraments."** Matter and form because we are body and soul.
- **The risen Christ ate fish.** Resurrection, not escape: "a ghost doesn't have flesh and bones as I do."
- **2 + 2 = 4 is true in any possible world.** Grasping pure form shows the intellect is immaterial, hence the soul is immortal.
- **"A disembodied soul is not a human being."** Survival is not the goal; resurrection is.
- **"I don't want just another true thing. I want the truth itself."** The imago Dei as restless intellect.

## Key Corrections to Carry Forward
- 🔴 "The real me is my soul": for Aquinas the real me is this soul, this flesh, these bones together.
- 🔴 "The body is a prison or a punishment": Genesis says it is good; Aquinas rejects Plato, the Gnostics, the Manichees and Origen on this.
- 🔴 "Heaven means leaving the body behind": the Christian hope is the resurrection of the body; a separated soul is not a complete human.
- 🔴 "Aquinas is just Aristotle baptized": he takes Aristotle's unity of body and soul but keeps Plato's insight that the intellect is immaterial.
- 🔴 "Catholic tradition sees sex as evil in itself": Aquinas argues the natural inclination to carnal union comes from God and cannot be evil in itself.
- 🔴 "Thinking is just brain activity": Aquinas grants the brain a role in sensation and memory, but argues that grasping pure form exceeds matter.

## Practical Takeaway
Study-level takeaways: (1) when you hear "the real me is inside," test it against *anima mea non sum ego*; (2) the soul as form of the body is the single idea that ties together the Creed's resurrection of the body, sacramental matter and form, and Theology of the Body; (3) the four-step chain (senses, phantasm, agent intellect, passive intellect) is the core of the argument for the soul's immortality, worth being able to say from memory; (4) Barron's practical reading of the imago Dei: your endless "why?" is not a distraction from faith but the image of God pulling you toward truth itself.

## Questions to Learn From (Socratic) — with Answer Options
**1. Given Aquinas's line "Anima mea non sum ego," how would he answer someone who says "the real me is inside, and my body is just the vehicle"?**
A. He would agree, since the soul is immortal and the body is not
B. \* He would disagree: the human being is this soul, this flesh and these bones together, so the body belongs to the real me
C. He would say the body is the real me and the soul is a later theological addition
*Why B: Barron uses this line precisely against "the real me in here" talk; Aquinas affirms the soul but denies that it alone is the person.*

**2. Given Barron's account of the agent intellect, what is the argument for the soul's immateriality?**
A. Near-death experiences prove the soul leaves the body
B. The soul must be immaterial because Plato said so
C. \* The intellect grasps pure forms, such as the number seven or 2 + 2 = 4 true in any possible world, which have no size, color or location, so the power that grasps them cannot be merely material
*Why C: the argument runs from what the mind can know (universal, immaterial forms) to what the mind must be. Barron treats NDEs only as a "maybe," not as the proof.*

**3. Given that Aquinas affirms the soul's immortality, why is the separated soul after death still not the goal?**
A. \* Because a disembodied soul is not a complete human being; the consummation is the soul reunited with a resurrected, elevated body
B. Because the soul eventually dissolves without the body
C. Because only angels are meant to have souls without bodies, so the human soul is lost
*Why A: Barron says the separated soul survives but is "not really" happy, since "a disembodied soul is not a human being"; the Christian hope is resurrection.*

**4. Given Aquinas's reasoning about the sacraments, why do they use both matter and form?**
A. Because the Church inherited the practice from pagan rites
B. \* Because we are body and soul, so God reaches us through physical things as well as words; angels, having no bodies, would not need sacraments
C. Because words alone are not valid in canon law
*Why B: Barron gives Aquinas's answer as "that simple": we have bodies and souls, so grace comes through water, oil, bread, wine and hands, specified by words.*
