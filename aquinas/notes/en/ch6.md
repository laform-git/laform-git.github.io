
# Barron — Thomas Aquinas, Ch.6: Providence and the Problem of Evil
*How God provides for the world without competing with it: the author-and-novel metaphor, providence over particulars, freedom for excellence, Molina's middle knowledge, evil as privation, and Job's answer to suffering.*

*Source: Bishop Robert Barron, Peterson Academy course "Thomas Aquinas," Lecture 6 (transcript). Sixth of eight lectures; follows the doctrine of creation (Ch.5) and closes the "God and the world" arc before Aquinas's anthropology (Ch.7). Transcript complete: the lecture ends with "Thanks," followed by a short audience question on Tillich and Aquinas.*

## Why This Matters
Barron calls the problem of evil "the problem of problems," the irritant in the oyster that produces the pearl of theology. This lecture is where all the earlier metaphysics (God as *ipsum esse*, creation *ex nihilo*, non-competitive causality) pays off pastorally. If God is the author of everything, are we puppets? If God is all-good and all-powerful, why is a child going blind? Barron's answer has two moves: get the *picture* of God right (author, not character), and get the *nature* of evil right (a lack, not a rival). He is honest that the second half gestures toward a solution rather than delivering a neat answer, as with the Trinity.

## The Master Metaphor — Author to Novel
- **Tolkien and *The Lord of the Rings*, Dickens and *Oliver Twist*:** the author is responsible for every character, plot, sentence and semicolon. His providence over the text is **absolute**. Yet the author is **nowhere in the story** as a character. ✅
- This is Barron's preferred metaphor for providence: total responsibility without being "one fussy cause" among others. It rests on the previous lecture: creation *ex nihilo* means nothing pre-exists God's ordering, and creation is continual (*creatio continua*), "a relation to the Creator with newness of being" (*quaedam relatio ad Creatorem cum novitate essendi*), moment to moment. ✅
- **The architect:** the architect's mind is everywhere in a designed room, but he is nowhere in the room. ✅
- **1 and 2 Samuel:** God never appears as a character, and the story reads perfectly well as politics and economics, yet every page assumes God is working his purposes out. Barron contrasts this with earlier texts where God walks in the garden "like a human character," which he calls a more primitive level of revelation 🟡 (his reading). He mentions he wrote a commentary on 2 Samuel.
- **Square and cube** (from an earlier lecture): a cube contains infinitely many squares without crowding them. God is the ground of being on a different level, so he can let the world have its own integrity "even as He operates ineluctably through it." ✅
- **Isaiah, a favorite of Aquinas:** "Lord, it is You who has accomplished all that we have done." We act; God accomplishes, non-competitively.
- **The cake:** you need flour, sugar and heat, not "flour and God"; yet afterward you thank God for the cake, because he is the condition for all of it coming together. (Barron borrows it from an unnamed theologian.)

| Picture of God | Consequence |
| --- | --- |
| A being among beings, "one character" | Every divine act competes with ours; providence looks like domination |
| Author / architect / cube (*ipsum esse*) | God is responsible for all of it and lets each thing act by its own powers |

## The Universality of Providence
- **Thomas's answer: providence is universal and extends to particulars.** Against philosophers who limited God's care to spiritual or "higher" things, he follows the Book of Wisdom: God "orders all things sweetly," reaching "from end to end mightily." Because God is *ipsum esse* and creates everything *ex nihilo*, he must be involved in every particular. ✅
- Barron's retreat line to priests: "God has brought us together." You cannot hide behind "He cares about presidents, not me." So praying grace over lunch at Denny's makes sense. ✅
- **Angel and worm:** the angel participates more fully in God's *to-be*, but God is **no closer** to an angel than to a worm. ✅
- Providence is **personal**: God has mind, will and love, so his presence to all things is a knowing, loving presence. Barron sets this against the "deist game" of pushing God far away. ✅
- **The order of the whole.** From the *Summa Contra Gentiles*, Book III: among created things, "what God cares for most is the order of the universe." Barron calls this pre-modern "as a compliment": less obsessed with the individual, keener on the totality. Image: a general drawing an unruly army toward one great purpose. Genesis: individual creatures are good, the whole is *very* good. ✅
- **No domination:** God does not need the world, so he has no interest in dominating it. His whole purpose toward it is "to let it be and to will it good," a metaphysical claim, not a sentimental one. ✅

## "For People of Faith, There Are No Coincidences"
- Barron attributes the line to John Paul II 🟡 (attribution as stated, source not given) and reads it Thomistically: at the level of particular causes, "what a happy coincidence" is a fair description, but not at the level of providence.
- **Aquinas's king and two messengers:** a king sends two servants by different routes, intending them to meet. To them it is chance; to the king it is design. ✅
- Jean-Pierre de Caussade: "Whatever happens to us is either directly or indirectly the will of God." Barron: your life will change in the measure that you take that in. 🟡 (spiritual counsel, not argument)

## Providence and Human Freedom
Barron concedes the novel analogy "limps" here: Tolkien's characters have no freedom; we have mind and will.

### Two ideas of freedom

|  | Freedom of indifference (modern) | Freedom for excellence (classical, Thomas) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Definition | Capacity to choose yes or no without coercion, exterior or interior | Ordering of desire so the achievement of the good becomes first possible, then effortless |
| Image |  | A fluent English speaker; a pianist who has internalized the rules |
| Growth means | More options | Fewer distractions and attachments between you and the good |
| Source | Barron borrows the terms from Dominican Servais Pinckaers |  |

- Barron's examples: struggling in French at Paris seminar tables during his doctoral work (unfree), versus speaking English (free); a beginner versus an accomplished musician. ✅
- **Luring, not coercing.** If someone arranged a Tiger Woods lesson at the TPC Stadium Course in Phoenix, or coffee with Bob Dylan, to get Barron to a place for another purpose, he would go freely and the arranger would still get what he wanted. God, who knows us better than we know ourselves and can arrange all circumstances, draws freedom by placing a good in front of it. Providence awakens freedom; it does not override it. ✅ (Barron's analogy)
- **Molina's middle knowledge** (post-Reformation debate): besides knowing what *will* be and what *could* be, God knows what *would* be, *scientia media*, what each free creature would freely do in any circumstances. He then "ratifies" the world in which free creatures accomplish what he wants. Barron calls it "a very interesting proposal" in the same non-competitive space. 🟡 Molina was a Jesuit; Barron jokes about mentioning him to Dominicans and Jesuits. This is not Aquinas's own position, and it remains disputed among Thomists; the lecture only hints at that.

## The Problem Stated — Steelmanned
- **Pastoral reality:** a grandfather told a young Father Barron after a homily on providence that it "did not help": one granddaughter had a barely understood disease that would blind and then kill her, and the younger one, with the same genetic condition, now cries in bed at night. Barron treats him as the *cri de cœur* of the human race.
- **The trilemma**, attributed by Barron to John Stuart Mill 🟡 (it is more often traced to Epicurus and Hume): if God is omniscient he knows of evil; omnipotent, he can stop it; omnibenevolent, he wants to. Evil exists, so no such God.
- **Aquinas's own objection** in *utrum Deus sit* ("whether God exists"): if one of two contraries is infinite, the other is destroyed (infinite heat leaves no cold); God is infinitely good; so there should be no evil; but there is. Barron: "darn good argument," anticipating Mill by centuries, proof that Thomas steelmans. ✅
- **Thomas's reply** (riffing on Augustine): "Since God is the highest good, He would not allow any evil to exist in His works, unless His omnipotence and goodness were such as to bring good even out of evil." God permits evil to bring about a good that could not otherwise be achieved. ✅

## Evil as Privation (*privatio boni*)
- Evil is **not something positive**, not a principle standing over against the good, but a **lack of a good that ought to be there**. Examples: a cavity in a tooth, cancer compromising an organ, moral turpitude twisting a good will and mind. ✅
- **Nazgûl:** when killed, there is nothing under the cloak, which Barron reads as Tolkien's comment that evil is a kind of non-being 🟡 (his reading).
- **Against dualism:** never speak of God creating evil or of evil as a coequal principle. *Star Wars* (light side vs. dark side) is the latest version of the old gnostic cosmologies. The devil is good in his being, mind and will; the evil is the perversion of these. ✅
- St Paul: "Where sin abounds, grace abounds the more," a metaphysical as well as moral statement. ✅
- **Chesterton test:** you can think about good without evil, but not evil without good; evil is parasitic. And the deeper puzzle is the *problem of good*: why there should be good at all. ✅
- Left open: what the snake is doing in the garden.

## Moral Evil and Physical Evil

| Type | Examples | Barron's answer | Difficulty |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Moral evil** |  | God wants lovers, not marionettes; freedom includes the possibility of abused freedom | "Relatively easy" |
| **Physical evil** | The granddaughters' disease, an earthquake killing thousands, a child's death | God permits it for a good that would not otherwise obtain, which we sometimes see and often cannot | "The really hard one" |

- Sometimes we can see the good: the lost job that led to a better one, the breakup that preceded the right marriage, the illness that opened a new vision. "Can we always see it? I can't." ✅

## Job — The Divine Strategy
- Barron rejects the New Atheist claim that believers never faced evil: the two greatest texts on it are the **Book of Job** and **The Brothers Karamazov**, both from the Judeo-Christian tradition. 🟡 (his judgment)
- Job is righteous, so the **Deuteronomistic principle** (good rewarded, bad punished) fails. His wife: "Curse God and die." His three friends do well to sit seven days in silence, then badly by insisting he must have sinned. Job keeps his innocence and calls God into the dock. ✅
- God answers from the **whirlwind** (sand kicked up, nothing seen clearly), in the longest divine speech in the Bible. He gives no lecture on privation; he takes Job on a tour of creation ("Where were you when I laid the foundations of the world?"), ending with **Behemoth and Leviathan** (perhaps a crocodile and a whale; uncertain). The point: "You're part of a book that's infinitely larger than your chapter." ✅
- **The three-year-old after surgery:** a father sat with his son in pain, whose look said "why are you doing this to me?" He had an answer but could not, even in principle, explain it to a child. Barron: "That's God in the Book of Job."
- **Multi-level chess:** God plays an infinite variety of linked games; from one board, it looks like dumb suffering.
- **Conclusion: surrender.** The God who can't be grasped but can't be hidden from; acceptance in love and faith that God is perfecting the world he made, "probably the best we can do." 🟡 Barron frames this as the limit of the answer, not a full solution.

## Audience Q&A — Tillich and Aquinas
Asked about Paul Tillich (modern liberal) versus Aquinas (classical): both use ontology. From Tillich, a Protestant, Barron learned a **Christological reading of metaphysics**, which led him to read Aquinas's Christological texts to interpret the metaphysical ones (his doctoral work). Tillich, a chaplain in the First World War, wrote theology "for people whose world had collapsed," in touch with Freud and modern psychology.

## Key Corrections to Carry Forward
- 🔴 "God is a character in the story": he is the author, present everywhere and nowhere in it.
- 🔴 "Providence only covers big or spiritual things": it extends to particulars.
- 🔴 "If God governs everything, we're puppets": God lures freedom by placing goods before it; non-competitive causality.
- 🔴 "Freedom means maximum options": classical freedom is freedom *for excellence*, the ordered desire that makes the good effortless.
- 🔴 "Evil is a force equal to God": evil is a privation, parasitic on the good.
- 🔴 "God creates evil": God permits evil to draw a greater good from it.
- 🔴 "Christians never faced the problem of evil": Job and Karamazov are its two greatest texts (Barron's claim).

## Practical Takeaway
(2) Treat "coincidences" as invitations to look for providence, while not pretending to know the plot. (3) Measure freedom by how easily you reach the good, not by how many options you keep open. (4) With a suffering person, imitate Job's friends' first week (silence and presence), not their speeches.

## Questions to Learn From (Socratic) — with Answer Options
**1. Given Barron's author-to-novel metaphor, why does God's total providence not make him "one cause among many"?**
A. Because God only sets the world in motion and then withdraws, as the deists say
B. \* Because, like an author, God is responsible for every detail while not appearing as a character, so his causality is on a different level and does not compete with creatures'
C. Because God controls only the main plot and leaves the subplots to chance
*Why B: Tolkien and Dickens are responsible for every comma yet nowhere in the story; Barron uses this, plus the square and cube, to show non-competitive causality.*

**2. Given Aquinas's reply to the objection from evil in *utrum Deus sit*, which statement fits his position?**
A. \* God permits evil only because his omnipotence and goodness can bring a good out of it that could not otherwise be achieved
B. Evil is a real substance God created to test human beings
C. Evil proves God is good but not omnipotent
*Why A: Thomas, following Augustine, says the highest good would not allow evil in his works unless he could bring good even out of evil; evil itself is a privation, not a created substance.*

**3. Given Barron's reading of the Book of Job, what is God's strategy in the whirlwind speech?**
A. He explains the privation theory of evil to Job in detail
B. \* He breaks Job's narrow focus by showing him the vastness of creation, implying Job is part of a story infinitely larger than his chapter
C. He confirms the friends' view that Job's suffering is punishment for hidden sin
*Why B: God gives no theory; like the father of the three-year-old after surgery, he has an answer Job could not take in, so he opens Job's view to the whole of providence.*
