# Q104 - Why did the Church persecute Jews?

*Related: [[Pius XII and the Holocaust]], [[Q102]] (Crusades-era attacks on Jews), [[Anti-Catholic Charges]]*

The Church has been wrongly accused of anti-Semitism throughout the centuries. Critics often cite verses such as [Matthew 27:24-26](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew+27%3A24-26) ("his blood be on us and on our children"), [Matthew 23:37](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew+23%3A37), and [John 5:16-18](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=John+5%3A16-18) — but these refer to the specific individuals who opposed Christ at the time, not a general condemnation of the Jewish people, whom the Church has always regarded as the chosen people of the Old Covenant.

Bad Catholics have, regrettably, persecuted Jews at points in history, though the Church never condoned it. In the disorganized People's Crusade of 1096, untrained crusaders robbed and killed Jews to fund their venture; separately, Count Emicho of Leiningen launched his own attacks along the Rhine that year, killing hundreds in Worms alone despite local bishops sheltering as many Jews as they could. Hungarian Catholic knights eventually stopped the slaughter, and Pope Urban II — who had launched the First Crusade — condemned the attacks. Similar episodes recurred in 1146 (stopped by St. Bernard of Clairvaux), in the 13th century amid political instability, and after the 1347 Black Death, when Jews were falsely blamed for poisoning wells; Pope Clement IV issued a bull denouncing the theory and excommunicating the attackers. In every case, these were crimes committed by individuals against Church teaching, not policy of the Church itself.

Pope Pius XII, sometimes labeled "Hitler's Pope" in anti-Catholic circles, in fact did the opposite of what the epithet suggests. Contemporary Jewish leaders praised him extensively: Chaim Weizmann, later Israel's first president, wrote in 1943 that "the Holy See is lending its powerful help wherever it can, to mitigate the fate of my persecuted co-religionists." Rabbi Israel Zolli, chief rabbi of Rome, said in 1944 that "the Vatican has always helped the Jews… the Pope and the Vatican were indefatigable in working to save Jews," and converted to Catholicism the following year, taking the baptismal name Eugenio in Pius XII's honor. Moshe Sharett, later Israel's foreign minister and prime minister, thanked Pius XII personally in 1945 "on behalf of the Jewish public for all they had done in various countries to rescue Jews," and Golda Meir called him "a great servant of peace" in 1958. Historian and former Israeli diplomat Pinchas Lapide estimated that Pius XII's interventions saved roughly 860,000 Jewish lives.

The Vatican maintained official neutrality during World War II, which everyone — including Hitler — understood to favor the Allies unofficially; that neutrality also kept the Nazis from invading or bombing the Vatican, but the Church's later critics twisted it into a charge of collaboration. Rolf Hochhuth's 1963 play *The Deputy* first popularized the "silent pope" narrative, though qualified historians, prominent Jewish leaders, and the Anti-Defamation League all refuted it; the same slander resurfaced decades later in a wave of books beginning with John Cornwell's 1999 *Hitler's Pope*. In reality, Pius XII spoke openly against Nazism until Christmas 1942, when he judged that further public statements would provoke worse reprisals — a judgment borne out when Dutch bishops' July 1942 condemnation of Nazi persecution triggered harsh retaliation that claimed victims including Edith Stein and Anne Frank. Pius XII meanwhile sheltered Jews throughout Rome and the Vatican, even converting his own Castel Gandolfo bedroom into a maternity ward for Jewish women and infants.

St. Augustine wrote in *Contra Faustum*: "Whoever destroys Jews in this way shall suffer sevenfold vengeance." Vatican II's *[Nostra Aetate](https://www.vatican.va/archive/hist_councils/ii_vatican_council/documents/vat-ii_decl_19651028_nostra-aetate_en.html)* taught that "what happened in His passion cannot be charged against all the Jews, without distinction, then alive, nor against the Jews of today… the Church decries hatred, persecutions, displays of anti-Semitism, directed against Jews at any time and by anyone." And the International Theological Commission's *Memory and Reconciliation: The Church and the Faults of the Past* acknowledges soberly that "the balance of these relations over two thousand years has been quite negative," that Nazi persecution "was not made easier" only by Nazi ideology but also by "anti-Jewish prejudices embedded in some Christian minds and hearts," and calls Christians to an ongoing "act of repentance."

Further reading: *Vatican Secret Archives: Unknown Pages of Church History* by Grzegorz Górny and Janusz Rosikoń, and *Bearing False Witness: Debunking Centuries of Anti-Catholic History* by Rodney Stark.
