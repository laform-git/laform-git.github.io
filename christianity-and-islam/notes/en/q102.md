# Q102 - Why did the Church wage the Crusades against Muslims?

**Short answer.** The Crusades were not a war against the Muslim religion as such; they were a defensive response to Turkish aggression. After Muslims conquered Jerusalem in 638, roughly four centuries of uneasy but largely peaceful coexistence followed, with Muslim governors tolerating Christian residents and pilgrims (while taxing them more heavily and denying them political equality). The rise of the Fatimid Muslims and Seljuk Turks in the 11th century brought new persecution of Christians and closed the pilgrim roads. The Turks then expanded westward by force, crushing the Byzantine army at Manzikert in 1071 and setting their sights on Constantinople. The Eastern Emperor Alexius I Comnenus appealed to the West for help; Pope Urban II answered by proclaiming the First Crusade at the Council of Clermont in 1095, hoping also to heal the Great Schism of 1054. The legitimate self-defense of a threatened people is justified in principle, and individual crusaders' crimes do not make the venture itself unjust.

**The longer history.** After Clermont, Blessed Peter the Hermit recruited thousands of untrained peasants for the disastrous "People's Crusade" of 1096, most of whom never reached the Holy Land. Separately, Count Emicho of Leiningen led an army that robbed and murdered Jews along the Rhine to fund the campaign — an atrocity the Church never approved and local bishops tried in vain to stop.

The official First Crusade succeeded where the People's Crusade failed: the conquered lands were divided into the Kingdom of Jerusalem, the Earldom of Tripoli, the Principality of Antioch, and the Earldom of Edessa, with Godfrey of Bouillon — too humble to call himself king in Christ's city — elected "Advocate of the Holy Sepulcher." The peace briefly enriched Western culture as pilgrims learned mathematics, medicine, and philosophy from Muslim scholars. But the new kingdoms proved hard to govern, and by 1144 the Turks under Zengi recaptured Edessa, prompting Pope Eugene III to call the Second Crusade, preached across Europe by St. Bernard of Clairvaux. St. Bernard also worked, imperfectly, to prevent renewed massacres of Jews, invoking the Psalms on their behalf — but in 1146 a rogue monk named Radulf incited fresh Rhineland attacks before St. Bernard personally intervened to stop them. The Second Crusade itself ended in a strategic blunder: crusader kings attacked Damascus, a former ally, lost it to the enemy, and returned home having gained nothing.

The Third Crusade followed Saladin's 1187 recapture of Jerusalem. Emperor Frederick Barbarossa drowned en route; Kings Philip II of France and Richard I of England quarreled constantly, and Richard's brutal massacre of 3,000 prisoners at Acre in 1191 stains his record permanently. The Crusade ended with a treaty dividing the coast between the two sides.

The Fourth Crusade (1201–1204) never even reached the Holy Land: diverted by a Byzantine succession dispute, the crusaders sacked Constantinople itself, deepening the rupture between East and West — a tragedy Pope Innocent III bitterly condemned. The unofficial "Children's Crusade" of 1212 ended in mass death and, for some, enslavement by unscrupulous merchants — again, wholly unintended by the Church. The Fifth through Eighth Crusades all likewise failed to hold Jerusalem, though St. Francis's peaceful 1219 visit to Sultan Malik al-Kamil during the Fifth Crusade led the Muslim rulers to entrust the Holy Land's Christian shrines to Franciscan custody, a role the order still fulfills today. The final Christian stronghold, Acre, fell to the Mamelukes in 1291 — a fall marked by a massacre of 60,000 residents, a century after Richard's own massacre in the same city.

**Evaluating the Crusades.** These points do not excuse every crusader or every atrocity, but they place the venture in context: the Crusades were a defensive response to real Turkish aggression, not an assault on Islam as a religion; most crusaders bore enormous personal cost, dying en route, in battle, or in poverty; more diplomacy might have been tried, though it likely would have failed against the Seljuks specifically; and massacres, while never approved by the Church or most commanders, were common practice in medieval siege warfare on both sides — the Mameluke sultan Baibars's slaughters of Christians were considerably larger and more brutal than anything committed by crusaders, a fact rarely mentioned by critics.

The [Just War Theory](https://www.vatican.va/archive/ccc/index_en.htm) that St. Augustine developed in *The City of God* and St. Thomas Aquinas refined in the *Summa Theologiae* offers the proper framework: just cause, legitimate authority, right intention (diplomacy attempted first, reasonable chance of success, proportionate means), and protection of non-combatants once war is underway. See CCC 2263-2265 and 2308-2317.
