
# Posner — The Pharmaceutical Industry, Ch.4: Regulation Revolution
*How Estes Kefauver's 1959 drug-industry hearings, the Sackler and Henry Welch scandals, and the thalidomide near-miss produced the 1962 Kefauver-Harris Amendments, which required proof of efficacy but left patents and pricing power untouched.*

*Source: Gerald Posner, Peterson Academy course "The Pharmaceutical Industry," Lecture 4 (transcript). Fourth of eight lectures; follows Ch.3 Selling Sickness and sets up Ch.5 Mother's Little Helper. The lecture ends with three audience Q&A exchanges (off-label prescribing, government versus competition, Big Food and Big Pharma). Transcript is complete.*

**In plain words:** until 1962, a US drug company only had to show that a drug was fairly safe, never that it actually worked. Senator Estes Kefauver spent years investigating high prices, copycat drugs and misleading ads, and proposed a bill to shorten patents. The industry killed that bill. Then the thalidomide disaster in Europe, kept out of the US by one FDA reviewer, Frances Kelsey, brought a new law in 1962: drugs must now be proven to work, factories are inspected and bad drugs can be recalled. But patents and prices were left alone. Posner's facts are marked ✅ and his opinions 🟡.

**Words in this note:**
- **FDA**: U.S. Food and Drug Administration
- **AMA**: American Medical Association
- **FTC**: U.S. Federal Trade Commission
- **TRT**: testosterone replacement therapy
- **DHEA**: a hormone made by the adrenal glands that the body turns into sex hormones
- **ApoB**: apolipoprotein B; counts all the cholesterol particles that can clog arteries

## Why This Matters
This is the lecture where modern drug regulation is born. Before 1962, a US drug company only had to show a drug was reasonably safe. It never had to show the drug worked, never had to register with the FDA, and never had an inspector at the door. Posner's main argument is a repeating cycle: **tragedy, then reform, then industry resistance**. The reform that finally passed was a law on safety and efficacy (proof the drug works). The industry accepted it because the part it feared most (shorter patents and the loss of control over prices) was dropped. Knowing what the 1962 law did and did not do is the starting point for reading any prescription label, any drug ad and any "FDA-approved" claim. Posner is an investigative journalist (author of *Pharma*), not a scientist, so his historical facts are marked ✅ when stated clearly and his interpretations 🟡.

## Opening Recap — Doctors, Drug Companies and Homeopathy
- Posner's half-step back to Lecture 3: the AMA (American Medical Association) wanted to get rid of competition from homeopathy (a system of extremely diluted remedies) and "quacks." In 1900 the US had **20 homeopathic schools**; in **1950 the last one closed**. ✅
- That same year, by his figures, the top 10 American drug companies were filling **80% of the world's prescriptions and taking 95% of the profits**, having replaced the ruined German industry. ✅ (as stated)
- In Germany, homeopathy had done well alongside Merck, Bayer and Schering and was treated as competition, not something to wipe out. 🟡 His contrast.
- His reading: doctors and drug companies became allies because each protected the other's monopoly. 🟡 Interpretation.

## The Core Problem — Patients Pay for Innovation
- New drugs bring hope and risk, and "sometimes patients become the test market." Harm "becomes visible only after the profits are banked." 🟡 His framing.
- A recall (pulling a drug off the market) stops sales, but nobody refunds what was already sold. ✅ (his observation)
- Every advance (antibiotics, anesthesia, chemotherapy, hormone treatments) cuts both ways.

## Kefauver — From Mob Hearings to the Drug Industry
- **Estes Kefauver**: 6'3", lanky, stooped, thick glasses, strong Southern accent; an unlikely TV star. ✅
- His **1950 hearings on organized crime** were shown on TV and became, in Posner's phrase, "the True Crime 1950s reality show." Time magazine wrote that dishes sat in sinks and department stores emptied. About **90% of TV households (about 30 million Americans)** watched at some point. The writer David Halberstam called him "a Southern Jimmy Stewart." ✅
- He ran for president in **1952 and 1956** and lost the nomination to Adlai Stevenson both times. He was Stevenson's running mate in 1956 and lost to Eisenhower. ✅
- As head of the Senate antitrust subcommittee (which looks at unfair business practices), he investigated steel, cars and bread for price-fixing, then turned to drugs in **1959**. Posner believes no Congress today, of either party, would run an investigation this deep. 🟡 Opinion.

### What Kefauver found: one-product companies

| Company | Dependence (as stated) |
| --- | --- |
| Parke-Davis | 45% of sales from antibiotics |
| Lederle | 40% antibiotics |
| Pfizer | 33% antibiotics |
| Lilly | 20% antibiotics |
| Merck | 40% from Diuril, a blood-pressure drug |
| Upjohn | 20% from Orinase (Posner calls it "a derivative" of Diuril 🟡; Evidence check: Orinase is tolbutamide, a sulfonylurea used for type 2 diabetes, not a diuretic derivative: PubChem) |
| Smith Kline & French | Nearly a quarter from Thorazine, a drug for psychosis |

- He suspected the firms used **cross-licensing** (letting each other use their patents) to divide up markets. He subpoenaed (legally ordered to appear) **19 companies** and their heads about costs and price-fixing. ✅
- The industry's line, then and now: prices are high because research is expensive, and patents are needed to earn back the investment. ✅
- Executives who testified included **Francis Brown (Schering), John Connor (Merck) and Harry Loynd (Parke-Davis)**. Coming after the mob, steel and bread hearings, they looked to the public like "mobsters" in quieter suits. 🟡 His reading.

### Kefauver's agenda

| Target | What he wanted |
| --- | --- |
| Patents | Check whether 17-year monopolies were needed; patents gave "unfettered pricing power" (freedom to charge anything), unique to the US after WWII (European systems negotiated prices through boards) |
| Me-too drugs | Stop patent protection for slight changes |
| Marketing | Expose misleading claims made to doctors |
| Weak oversight | Give the FDA "teeth" |
| Science | Proof that drugs **work**, not just that they are safe; open sharing of trial data; informed consent in trials (patients told the risks and agreeing); stricter factory standards |

## The Sackler and Welch Scandals
- Posner got the committee files through FOIA (the Freedom of Information Act). Staff wrote a memo on a "**Sackler Empire**": by his count the family ran **24 companies** under different names, sharing phone numbers and addresses (15 and 17 East 62nd Street). They covered research, testing, consumer research, advertising and journal publishing, and all looked independent. ✅ (his research)
- Investigators found over 20 such companies but admitted that **money and time** limited what they could find. The Sacklers slipped down the list of priorities and "emerged mostly unscathed." ✅
- **Henry Welch**, head of the FDA's Antibiotics Division, co-ran an antibiotics journal with Arthur Sackler's partner **Félix Martí-Ibáñez**. He earned about **$287,000** from the journals, against an FDA salary of about **$17,000**, and joked inside the FDA that his salary could not even pay the taxes on his outside income. 🟡 Posner misspoke twice (said $27,000, then $17,500) before settling on $17,000; the $287,000 figure stays the same.
- Welch resigned in **May 1960** when the head of HEW (the health department) demanded it, with a defiant letter. The FDA's own investigation found him the only "bad apple" and cleared the agency. ✅ Posner's tone suggests he doubts that verdict. 🟡
- **Medical Tribune**: Arthur Sackler's newspaper for doctors, controlled through his web of companies, backed and funded by **Georges Doriot** (Posner calls him a famous Harvard professor). Sackler's column "One Man and Medicine" served clients such as Pfizer, Merck and Hoffmann-La Roche. ✅ Posner also mentions a title he calls "The Medical Journal"; it is unclear whether this is a separate publication or a slip for the Tribune. 🟡

### Sigmamycin and the eight doctors who did not exist
- **Sigmamycin** (Pfizer) was the FDA's first approved **fixed-dose antibiotic combination** (two antibiotics in one pill): two parts tetracycline, one part oleandomycin (a weak relative of erythromycin). ✅
- The ad promised "superior control of infectious disease through superior control of the changing microbial population," with no studies behind it. Welch, while head of FDA antibiotics, gave a keynote speech calling it the **"third wave"** of antibiotics. ✅
- In 1960 a *Saturday Evening Post* writer (Lear) saw a leaflet (🟡 the journalist John Lear wrote for the *Saturday Review*, not the *Saturday Evening Post*; unverified here) with endorsements from eight doctors, complete with addresses, phone numbers and office hours. None of the numbers worked and letters came back undelivered: **the doctors did not exist**. ✅
- Arthur Sackler, called before Congress, blamed "an over-eager copywriter at Pfizer" and escaped responsibility. Posner calls him the "puppet master" who edited every ad. 🟡
- It was legal because of an **FTC loophole** (the FTC is the Federal Trade Commission, which polices advertising): ads aimed at doctors were exempt, on the theory that doctors would not fall for false information. The fake-doctor episode showed they did. ✅

## Pricing Evidence From the Hearings

| Item | US price | Comparison (as stated) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A tranquilizer (calming drug) | $3.25 | 75 cents in Argentina (Posner: "over four times"); about half the US price in the UK |
| Antibiotics | $18 | US price more than twice that in several European countries |

- From 1950 to 1960 the industry put about **$600 million** back into buildings, equipment and research, and launched **400 new drugs**, most of them variations on existing ones. Antibiotics were cheap to make in fermentation tanks first built with WWII government support. ✅
- Posner's observation on tactics: when reform comes up, the industry "out-sciences you" (warns that reform will "cripple innovation" and stop "the next great cure for cancer"). 🟡

## The Bill That Died — S.1552, the Drug Industry Antitrust Act (April 1961)
Posner calls it a Senate Resolution; it was a Senate bill. 🟡 Its main parts (as Posner lists them; Evidence check below):

| Provision | Detail |
| --- | --- |
| Patent term | Cut from **17 years to 5** (European terms ran 3 to 7, averaging 5) |
| Compulsory licensing | After 3 years, any company offering a royalty (a fee) must be allowed to make the drug (✅ Greene 2012) |
| Cross-licensing | Banned |
| Me-too patents | Must show "a significant therapeutic benefit" (✅ Greene 2012: a patent only for a therapeutic effect "significantly greater" than the unmodified drug) |
| Patent extensions | Must show an improvement better than any generic (this hit Lilly) |
| Advertising | "Full and accurate information," list all reported side effects, "the whole truth" |

- 🟡 Evidence check: the historical account we checked describes S.1552's forced licensing after 3 years, its limit on patents for small molecular changes, FDA review of claims that a drug works and of advertising, and generic names. It does not mention a cut from 17 to 5 years, so treat that figure as unverified (Greene 2012).
- The industry's objections: it will ruin innovation, stop life-saving drugs, and is "unconstitutional and anti-American," a step toward European-style government-run medicine. ✅
- Posner says Arthur Sackler pushed the "anti-American" line hardest, while being a card-carrying Communist Party member who gave jobs to blacklisted writers. 🟡 Posner's claim.
- A whispering campaign said Kefauver was senile or paid by "Reds" (Communists). By the end of summer 1961 the bill was "dead in the water." ✅ (🟢 In June 1962 the Kennedy administration and the industry offered a replacement with no patent language, and S.1552 was seen as dead until thalidomide revived a gutted version: Greene 2012.)
- Later: the patent term went from 17 to 20 years, never down. ✅ (as stated)

## Thalidomide — "To the Rescue"
- Thalidomide was sold in Europe, mostly without prescription, as a calming and anti-nausea drug for pregnant women. About **10,000 babies** worldwide were born with severe deformities of the limbs and organs, plus thousands of deaths before birth. ✅ (over 10,000 children: Vargesson 2015)
- **Frances Kelsey**, a new FDA medical reviewer and one of few women there, doubted the European studies. Under the **60-day rule**, an application that got no answer within 60 days was approved automatically. So she kept declaring the paperwork incomplete and asking for more data. ✅
- At the time, US law required proof of **safety only**, not that the drug worked. Her single decision protected American babies and brought the stalled bill back to life. ✅
- The pattern repeats 1937: Elixir Sulfanilamide killed 107 people and produced the 1938 law. ✅

## The 1962 Kefauver-Harris Amendments — Seven Changes

| # | Change |
| --- | --- |
| 1 | Drug manufacturers must **register with the FDA** (never required before) |
| 2 | The **60-day automatic approval** rule was scrapped |
| 3 | Regular **on-site inspections** of factories |
| 4 | **Quality-control standards** (before, the honor system); Posner forgot this one and added it later |
| 5 | **Power to recall** drugs judged "an imminent hazard to the public health" or that "failed to deliver their therapeutic claims"; later used to kill most fixed-dose antibiotic combinations (🟢 the follow-up review of whether drugs approved 1938–62 worked, called DESI, had classed about 600 medicines as ineffective by the early 1970s: Greene 2012) |
| 6 | Ads to doctors must strike "a **fair balance**" between selling and accuracy (not defined in the law) |
| 7 | **Proof that the drug works**: "substantial evidence" from "adequate and well-controlled studies," and useless drugs removed |

- Posner calls #7 the biggest change and the start of the modern system of medical trials, and calls the 1962 law the largest single change in US drug law, ending the industry's **self-regulation** (policing itself). ✅ / 🟡 (the ranking is his)
- What was dropped: **patents and prices**. Internal industry memos he has seen show "a sigh of relief." ✅
- Afterwards, the FDA was swamped through the 1960s. Factories abroad (most antibiotics are now made overseas) always know when inspectors are coming, so they "very seldom have any problem." 🟡

## Legacy — What Approval Does and Does Not Mean
- Given the choice, company heads would take costly medical trials over 5-year patents. 🟡 His thought experiment.
- The industry pushes for **faster review**; the FDA later let companies pay fees to have drugs reviewed faster. Posner says "almost three quarters" of the FDA budget comes from these fees, then "over half." 🟡 The two figures conflict; either way he sees the industry paying its own regulator as "a bad sign." ✅ Evidence check: in fiscal 2022 user fees were 46% of the FDA's total budget and 66% of its human-drugs program budget (ASPE 2023), so "over half" fits drug review and "almost three quarters" overstates it. (User fees began with the 1992 Prescription Drug User Fee Act.)
- **Approval does not guarantee safety.** Drugs approved later still caused cancers and blood clots. He cites early hormone replacement and the first birth-control pill (1960, which he says had ten times the hormones needed and calls "the first lifestyle drug"). The real trial is the public. ✅ / 🟡 (the "ten times" figure is his)

## Punchlines & Quotable Facts (Lecture 4)
- **"The same drugs that can heal can also harm, and too often that harm becomes visible only after the profits are banked."**
- **The last US homeopathy school closed in 1950**, down from 20 in 1900.
- **Welch: about $287,000 from journals on a $17,000 FDA salary.**
- **Eight doctors endorsed Sigmamycin; none existed.**
- **S.1552 wanted 5-year patents; the industry kept 17.**
- **Frances Kelsey and the 60-day rule:** one reviewer's delay saved American babies from thalidomide.
- **1962: the first time a drug had to be shown to work.**
- **"Approval is a snapshot in time."**
- **The cycle: tragedy, reform, industry resistance.**

## Key Corrections to Carry Forward
- 🔴 "FDA approval has always meant a drug works": proof that a drug works was only required from 1962.
- 🔴 "Doctors can't be fooled by ads": the Sigmamycin fake-doctor leaflet fooled them, and ads aimed at doctors were unregulated until 1962.
- 🔴 "The 1962 law reined in drug prices": it did not touch patents or prices; that is why the industry accepted it.
- 🔴 "Approved means safe in the long run": approval is a snapshot; use by large numbers of people reveals side effects later.
- 🔴 "Thalidomide hurt Americans as it did Europeans": the US mostly escaped it because of Frances Kelsey.
- 🔴 "The Welch scandal showed a corrupt FDA": the FDA's own review blamed only Welch (Posner doubts it, but that was the official finding).

## Practical Takeaway
Posner's "smarter patient" list, as study points, not medical advice: (1) **ask whether a drug is new**, how long it has been on the market and how many kinds of people have used it; older, well-studied drugs have a longer safety record; (2) **be skeptical of marketing claims**, including TV drug ads aimed at the public (since 1997), which grew out of Arthur Sackler's 1950s idea that the industry could "create consumer demand"; (3) **report unusual side effects** to your doctor, since a drug's safety record is built after approval; (4) never assume regulators caught everything.

## Questions to Learn From (Socratic) — with Answer Options
**1. Given what Posner says the 1962 Kefauver-Harris Amendments required for the first time, which statement is correct?**
A. They cut drug patents from 17 years to 5
B. \* They required companies to show, with substantial evidence from adequate and well-controlled studies, that a drug works for its intended use
C. They banned all advertising of prescription drugs to doctors
*Why B: proof that the drug works was the big new requirement; the patent cut was in S.1552 and was dropped, and ads were held to a "fair balance," not banned.*

**2. Given that the drug industry fought S.1552 but accepted the 1962 law, what best explains the difference?**
A. \* The 1962 law kept 17-year patents and unfettered pricing power, which the industry valued more than avoiding new safety costs
B. The 1962 law was weaker on safety than S.1552
C. Thalidomide convinced executives that regulation was good for business
*Why A: Posner says internal memos showed relief that patents were untouched; the industry accepted the higher cost of trials in exchange for keeping control of prices.*

**3. Given the Sigmamycin fake-doctor pamphlet, what lesson does Posner draw?**
A. Pfizer's copywriter acted alone, so the system worked
B. Fixed-dose combinations were proven more effective than single antibiotics
C. \* Doctors are as vulnerable to misleading advertising as the public, which undercut the FTC exemption for ads aimed at doctors
*Why C: the exemption assumed doctors would see through false claims; eight doctors who did not exist persuaded real prescribers.*

**4. Given Frances Kelsey's role, how did she keep thalidomide off the US market under the law of the time?**
A. She proved in her own trials that thalidomide caused birth defects
B. \* She used the one lever the 60-day automatic-approval rule allowed, repeatedly finding the application incomplete and requesting more safety data
C. She invoked the FDA's power to demand proof of efficacy
*Why B: the requirement to prove a drug works did not exist yet, and an unanswered application was approved after 60 days, so asking for more data was her only tool.*

## Open Threads
- ✅ **Transcript complete:** the lecture closes with audience questions after the Ch.5 preview; no gaps.
- **Off-label prescribing** (using a drug for something it was not approved for; from the audience questions): Posner calls it "one of the greatest loopholes." Examples: minoxidil (a blood-pressure drug, now used for hair growth), Botox (which he says was first approved as an orphan drug, meaning a drug for a rare disease, for a throat stricture 🟡; Evidence check: as Oculinum it was licensed on 29 December 1989 for strabismus and blepharospasm, both eye-muscle conditions: Scott 2023; then used for looks; Allergan earned more from off-label use), Viagra, and Lupron used off-label as a puberty blocker. He says doctors gained the right to prescribe off-label in 1938 🟡 (off-label use rests on the FDA not regulating medical practice, not on a 1938 provision: Wittich 2012; see Ch.2). Promised in a later lecture.
- **Fees for faster review** and their share of the FDA budget: settled: 46% of the FDA's budget and 66% of human-drug review in fiscal 2022 (ASPE 2023).
- **Biologics** (medicines made from living cells): he says the FDA only got power over them in the 1970s 🟡; innovation goes on, but he wishes there were fewer me-too drugs.
- **Moving production abroad:** India and China can make drugs at about 20% of the US cost.
- **Big Food and Big Pharma** as occasional allies: streptomycin sprayed on Florida citrus crops despite worries about antibiotic resistance.
- **Ch.5 preview:** Librium and Valium (Valium the first drug to earn $100 million a year, then the first billion-dollar drugs), amphetamine diet centers, and Arthur Sackler again, this time for Hoffmann-La Roche.
