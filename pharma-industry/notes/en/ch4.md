
# Posner — The Pharmaceutical Industry, Ch.4: Regulation Revolution
*How Estes Kefauver's 1959 drug-industry hearings, the Sackler and Henry Welch scandals, and the thalidomide near-miss produced the 1962 Kefauver-Harris Amendments, which required proof of efficacy but left patents and pricing power untouched.*

*Source: Gerald Posner, Peterson Academy course "The Pharmaceutical Industry," Lecture 4 (transcript). Fourth of eight lectures; follows Ch.3 Selling Sickness and sets up Ch.5 Mother's Little Helper. The lecture ends with three audience Q&A exchanges (off-label prescribing, government versus competition, Big Food and Big Pharma). Transcript is complete.*

## Why This Matters
This is the lecture where modern drug regulation is born. Before 1962, a US drug company only had to show a drug was reasonably safe; it never had to show the drug worked, never had to register with the FDA, and never had an inspector at the door. Posner's thesis is a repeating cycle: **tragedy, then reform, then industry resistance**. The reform that finally passed was a safety-and-efficacy law, and the industry accepted it because the part it feared most (shorter patents and loss of pricing power) was dropped. Knowing what the 1962 law did and did not do is the baseline for reading any prescription label, any drug ad and any "FDA-approved" claim. Posner is an investigative journalist (author of *Pharma*), not a scientist, so his historical facts are marked ✅ when stated clearly and his interpretations 🟡.

## Opening Recap — Doctors, Drug Companies and Homeopathy
- Posner's half-step back to Lecture 3: the AMA wanted to eliminate competition from homeopathy and "quacks." In 1900 the US had **20 homeopathic schools**; in **1950 the last one closed**. ✅
- Same year, by his figures, the top 10 American drug companies were running **80% of the world's prescriptions and 95% of the profits**, having replaced the decimated German industry. ✅ (as stated)
- In Germany, homeopathy had flourished next to Merck, Bayer and Schering and was treated as competition, not something to extinguish. 🟡 His contrast.
- His reading: doctors and drug companies became allies because each enforced the other's monopoly. 🟡 Interpretation.

## The Core Problem — Patients Pay for Innovation
- New drugs carry promise and risk, and "sometimes patients become the test market." Harm "becomes visible only after the profits are banked." 🟡 His framing.
- A recall stops sales but nobody refunds what was already sold. ✅ (his observation)
- Every advance (antibiotics, anesthesia, chemotherapy, hormone therapies) is double-edged.

## Kefauver — From Mob Hearings to the Drug Industry
- **Estes Kefauver**: 6'3", lanky, hunched, thick glasses, strong Southern drawl; an unlikely TV star. ✅
- **1950 organized-crime hearings** were televised and became, in Posner's phrase, "the True Crime 1950s reality show." Time magazine wrote that dishes stood in sinks and department stores emptied. About **90% of TV households (about 30 million Americans)** watched at some point. David Halberstam called him "a Southern Jimmy Stewart." ✅
- He ran for president in **1952 and 1956**, lost the nomination to Adlai Stevenson both times, was Stevenson's running mate in 1956 and lost to Eisenhower. ✅
- As chair of the antitrust subcommittee he investigated steel, auto and bread for collusion, then turned to drugs in **1959**. Posner believes no Congress today, of either party, would mount a probe this deep. 🟡 Opinion.

### What Kefauver found: one-product companies

| Company | Dependence (as stated) |
| --- | --- |
| Parke-Davis | 45% of sales from antibiotics |
| Lederle | 40% antibiotics |
| Pfizer | 33% antibiotics |
| Lilly | 20% antibiotics |
| Merck | 40% from the antihypertensive Diuril |
| Upjohn | 20% from Orinase (Posner calls it "a derivative" of Diuril 🟡; Evidence check: Orinase is tolbutamide, a sulfonylurea used for type 2 diabetes, not a diuretic derivative: PubChem) |
| Smith Kline & French | Nearly a quarter from the antipsychotic Thorazine |

- He suspected the firms used **cross-licensing** to carve up markets. He subpoenaed **19 companies** and their CEOs on costs and collusion. ✅
- The industry line, then and now: prices are high because R&D is expensive, and patents are needed to recoup the investment. ✅
- Executives who testified included **Francis Brown (Schering), John Connor (Merck) and Harry Loynd (Parke-Davis)**. Coming after the mob, steel and bread hearings, they looked to the public like "mobsters" in quieter suits. 🟡 His read.

### Kefauver's agenda

| Target | What he wanted |
| --- | --- |
| Patents | Check whether 17-year monopolies were needed; patents gave "unfettered pricing power," unique to the US after WWII (European systems negotiated prices through boards) |
| Me-too drugs | Stop patent protection for slight modifications |
| Marketing | Expose misleading claims to doctors |
| Weak oversight | Give the FDA "teeth" |
| Science | Proof of **effectiveness**, not just safety; clinical-data transparency; informed consent in trials; stricter manufacturing standards |

## The Sackler and Welch Scandals
- Posner obtained the committee files by FOIA. Staff wrote a memo on a "**Sackler Empire**": by his count the family ran **24 companies** under different names, sharing phone numbers and addresses (15 and 17 East 62nd Street), covering research, testing, consumer research, advertising and journal publishing, all appearing independent. ✅ (his research)
- Investigators found over 20 such entities but admitted their findings were limited by **budget and time**; the Sacklers dropped down the priority list and "emerged mostly unscathed." ✅
- **Henry Welch**, head of the FDA's Antibiotics Division, co-ran an antibiotic journal with Arthur Sackler's partner **Félix Martí-Ibáñez**. He earned about **$287,000** from the journals against an FDA salary of about **$17,000**, and joked inside the FDA that his salary could not pay the taxes on his outside income. 🟡 Posner misspoke twice (said $27,000, then $17,500) before settling on $17,000; the $287,000 figure is consistent.
- Welch resigned in **May 1960** at the HEW Secretary's demand, with a defiant letter. The FDA's internal investigation found him the only "bad apple" and absolved the agency. ✅ Posner's tone suggests he doubts that verdict. 🟡
- **Medical Tribune**: Arthur Sackler's publication, controlled through his corporate web, endorsed and funded by **Georges Doriot** (Posner calls him a renowned Harvard professor). Sackler's column "One Man and Medicine" served clients such as Pfizer, Merck and Hoffmann-La Roche. ✅ Posner also mentions a title he calls "The Medical Journal"; it is unclear whether this is a separate publication or a slip for the Tribune. 🟡

### Sigmamycin and the eight doctors who did not exist
- **Sigmamycin** (Pfizer) was the FDA's first approved **fixed-dose antibiotic combination**: two parts tetracycline, one part oleandomycin (a weak relative of erythromycin). ✅
- The ad promised "superior control of infectious disease through superior control of the changing microbial population," with no studies behind it. Welch, while FDA antibiotics chief, gave a keynote calling it the **"third wave"** of antibiotics. ✅
- In 1960 a *Saturday Evening Post* writer (Lear) saw a pamphlet (🟡 the journalist John Lear wrote for the *Saturday Review*, not the *Saturday Evening Post*; unverified here) with endorsements from eight doctors, complete with addresses, phone numbers and office hours. None of the numbers worked and letters came back undeliverable: **the doctors were fictitious**. ✅
- Arthur Sackler, called before Congress, blamed "an over-eager copywriter at Pfizer" and escaped responsibility. Posner calls him the "puppet master" who edited every ad. 🟡
- It was legal because of an **FTC loophole**: ads to doctors were exempt from oversight, on the theory that doctors would not fall for misinformation. The fake-doctor episode showed they did. ✅

## Pricing Evidence From the Hearings

| Item | US price | Comparison (as stated) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A tranquilizer | $3.25 | 75 cents in Argentina (Posner: "over four times"); about half the US price in the UK |
| Antibiotics | $18 | US price more than twice that in several European countries |

- From 1950 to 1960 the industry reinvested about **$600 million** in infrastructure and R&D and launched **400 new drugs**, most of them variations on existing ones. Antibiotics were cheap to make in fermentation tanks first built with WWII government support. ✅
- Posner's observation on tactics: when reform comes up, the industry "out-sciences you" (reform will "cripple innovation" and stop "the next great cure for cancer"). 🟡

## The Bill That Died — S.1552, the Drug Industry Antitrust Act (April 1961)
Posner calls it a Senate Resolution; it was a Senate bill. 🟡 Its main provisions (as Posner lists them; Evidence check below):

| Provision | Detail |
| --- | --- |
| Patent term | Cut from **17 years to 5** (European terms ran 3 to 7, averaging 5) |
| Compulsory licensing | After 3 years, any company offering a royalty must be licensed (✅ Greene 2012) |
| Cross-licensing | Banned |
| Me-too patents | Must show "a significant therapeutic benefit" (✅ Greene 2012: a patent only for a therapeutic effect "significantly greater" than the unmodified drug) |
| Patent extensions | Must show an improvement better than any generic (this hit Lilly) |
| Advertising | "Full and accurate information," list all reported side effects, "the whole truth" |

- 🟡 Evidence check: the historical account we checked documents S.1552's compulsory licensing after 3 years, its limit on patents for molecular modifications, FDA review of efficacy claims and advertising, and generic names; it does not mention a cut from 17 to 5 years, so treat that figure as unverified (Greene 2012).
- Industry opposition: it will ruin innovation, stop life-saving drugs, and is "unconstitutional and anti-American," a step to European-style socialized medicine. ✅
- Posner says Arthur Sackler pushed the "anti-American" line hardest while being a card-carrying Communist Party member who gave jobs to blacklisted writers. 🟡 Posner's claim.
- A whisper campaign said Kefauver was senile or paid by "Reds." By the end of summer 1961 the bill was "dead in the water." ✅ (🟢 In June 1962 the Kennedy administration and industry offered a substitute with no patent language, and S.1552 was seen as dead until thalidomide revived a gutted version: Greene 2012.)
- Later context: the patent term moved from 17 to 20 years, never down. ✅ (as stated)

## Thalidomide — "To the Rescue"
- Thalidomide was sold in Europe, mostly over the counter, as a sedative and anti-nausea drug for pregnant women. About **10,000 babies** worldwide were born with severe limb and organ malformations, plus thousands of fetal deaths. ✅ (over 10,000 children: Vargesson 2015)
- **Frances Kelsey**, a new FDA medical reviewer and one of few women there, doubted the European studies. Under the **60-day rule**, an application not answered in 60 days was automatically approved, so she kept declaring the paperwork incomplete and requesting more data. ✅
- At the time US law required proof of **safety only**, not efficacy. Her single decision protected American babies and revived the stalled bill. ✅
- The pattern mirrors 1937: Elixir Sulfanilamide killed 107 people and produced the 1938 law. ✅

## The 1962 Kefauver-Harris Amendments — Seven Changes

| # | Change |
| --- | --- |
| 1 | Drug manufacturers must **register with the FDA** (never required before) |
| 2 | The **60-day automatic approval** rule was scrapped |
| 3 | Periodic **on-site inspections** of plants |
| 4 | **Quality-control standards** (before, the honor system); Posner forgot this one and added it later |
| 5 | **Recall power** for drugs judged "an imminent hazard to the public health" or that "failed to deliver their therapeutic claims"; later used to kill most fixed-dose antibiotic combinations (🟢 the follow-up efficacy review of drugs approved 1938–62, DESI, had classed about 600 medicines as ineffective by the early 1970s: Greene 2012) |
| 6 | Ads to doctors must strike "a **fair balance**" between promotion and accuracy (undefined in the law) |
| 7 | **Proof of efficacy**: "substantial evidence" from "adequate and well-controlled studies," and therapeutically useless drugs removed |

- Posner calls #7 the biggest and the start of the modern clinical-trial system, and the 1962 law the largest single change in US drug law, ending industry **self-regulation**. ✅ / 🟡 (the ranking is his)
- What was dropped: **patents and pricing**. Internal industry memos he has seen show "a sigh of relief." ✅
- Aftermath: the FDA was swamped through the 1960s. Foreign plants (most antibiotics are now made abroad) always know when inspectors are coming, so they "very seldom have any problem." 🟡

## Legacy — What Approval Does and Does Not Mean
- Given the choice, CEOs would take costly clinical trials over 5-year patents. 🟡 His thought experiment.
- Industry pushes for **expedited review**; the FDA later let companies pay fees for faster review. Posner says "almost three quarters" of the FDA budget comes from these fees, then "over half." 🟡 The two figures conflict; either way he sees industry funding of its regulator as "a bad sign." ✅ Evidence check: in fiscal 2022 user fees were 46% of FDA's total budget and 66% of its human-drugs program budget (ASPE 2023), so "over half" fits drug review and "almost three quarters" overstates it. (User fees began with the 1992 Prescription Drug User Fee Act.)
- **Approval is not a guarantee of safety.** Later-approved drugs still caused cancers and blood clots; he cites early hormone replacement and the first birth-control pill (1960, which he says had ten times the hormones needed and calls "the first lifestyle drug"). The real trial is the public. ✅ / 🟡 (the "ten times" figure is his)

## Punchlines & Quotable Facts (Lecture 4)
- **"The same drugs that can heal can also harm, and too often that harm becomes visible only after the profits are banked."**
- **Last US homeopathy school closed in 1950**, from 20 in 1900.
- **Welch: about $287,000 from journals on a $17,000 FDA salary.**
- **Eight endorsing doctors for Sigmamycin; none existed.**
- **S.1552 wanted 5-year patents; the industry kept 17.**
- **Frances Kelsey and the 60-day rule:** one reviewer's stalling saved American babies from thalidomide.
- **1962: first time a drug had to be shown to work.**
- **"Approval is a snapshot in time."**
- **Cycle: tragedy, reform, industry resistance.**

## Key Corrections to Carry Forward
- 🔴 "FDA approval has always meant a drug works": efficacy was only required from 1962.
- 🔴 "Doctors can't be fooled by ads": the Sigmamycin fake-doctor pamphlet fooled them, and doctor-directed ads were unregulated until 1962.
- 🔴 "The 1962 law reined in drug prices": it did not touch patents or pricing; that is why the industry accepted it.
- 🔴 "Approved means safe long-term": approval is a snapshot; large-scale use reveals side effects later.
- 🔴 "Thalidomide hurt Americans like Europeans": the US largely escaped it because of Frances Kelsey.
- 🔴 "The Welch scandal showed a corrupt FDA": the FDA's own review blamed only Welch (Posner doubts it, but that was the official finding).

## Practical Takeaway
Posner's "smarter patient" list, as study points, not medical advice: (1) **ask if a drug is new** and how long it has been on the market and in how broad a population; older, well-studied drugs have a longer safety record; (2) **treat marketing claims with skepticism**, including direct-to-consumer TV ads (since 1997), which grew from Arthur Sackler's 1950s idea that the industry could "create consumer demand"; (3) **report unusual side effects** to your doctor, since safety profiles are built after approval; (4) never assume regulators caught everything.

## Questions to Learn From (Socratic) — with Answer Options
**1. Given what Posner says the 1962 Kefauver-Harris Amendments required for the first time, which statement is correct?**
A. They cut drug patents from 17 years to 5
B. \* They required companies to show, with substantial evidence from adequate and well-controlled studies, that a drug works for its intended use
C. They banned all advertising of prescription drugs to doctors
*Why B: efficacy was the big new requirement; the patent cut was in S.1552 and was dropped, and ads were held to a "fair balance," not banned.*

**2. Given that the drug industry fought S.1552 but accepted the 1962 law, what best explains the difference?**
A. \* The 1962 law kept 17-year patents and unfettered pricing power, which the industry valued more than avoiding new safety costs
B. The 1962 law was weaker on safety than S.1552
C. Thalidomide convinced executives that regulation was good for business
*Why A: Posner says internal memos showed relief that patents were untouched; the industry traded higher trial costs for keeping pricing power.*

**3. Given the Sigmamycin fake-doctor pamphlet, what lesson does Posner draw?**
A. Pfizer's copywriter acted alone, so the system worked
B. Fixed-dose combinations were proven more effective than single antibiotics
C. \* Doctors are as vulnerable to misleading advertising as the public, which undercut the FTC exemption for ads aimed at doctors
*Why C: the exemption assumed doctors would see through false claims; eight fictitious endorsers persuaded real prescribers.*

**4. Given Frances Kelsey's role, how did she keep thalidomide off the US market under the law of the time?**
A. She proved in her own trials that thalidomide caused birth defects
B. \* She used the one lever the 60-day automatic-approval rule allowed, repeatedly finding the application incomplete and requesting more safety data
C. She invoked the FDA's power to demand proof of efficacy
*Why B: the efficacy requirement did not exist yet, and an unanswered application was approved after 60 days, so requesting more data was her only tool.*
