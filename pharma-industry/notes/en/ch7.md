
# Posner — The Pharmaceutical Industry, Ch.7: Price of Monopoly & Orphan Drugs
*How a well-meant 1983 law for rare diseases became the industry's most lucrative model: orphan-drug monopolies (AZT, Truvada, Botox, Acthar, Daraprim), seven-figure gene therapies, PBM rebates, and how to be a smarter patient.*

*Source: Gerald Posner, Peterson Academy course "The Pharmaceutical Industry," Lecture 7 (transcript). Seventh of eight lectures; follows Ch.6 on political capture and sets up Ch.8 on pandemic profits. Posner is an investigative journalist (author of "Pharma"), not a scientist: his historical facts are marked ✅ when clearly stated, his interpretations 🟡. Transcript reads as complete; it ends on his "smarter patient" checklist.*

## Why This Matters
Earlier lectures followed the mass-market model. This lecture shows the inversion. Since the Orphan Drug Act, the most profitable drugs can be ones that treat a few hundred or a few thousand people at $300,000 to $4 million each. Posner's thesis: **profit comes from scarcity, not volume**, and the system that allows it is a mix of patent law, orphan exclusivity, off-label promotion and opaque middlemen (PBMs). For a patient: list price, formulary and pharmacy routing are shaped by forces unrelated to how well a drug works.

## Opening Aside — Profiting From Side Effects
- Posner says a 1960s company viewed a drug with many side effects as a "gateway drug": not addictive, but a source of follow-on drugs to treat its side effects. 🟡 (company and drug not named)
- Purdue Pharma's attempt to avoid being a "one-hit wonder" was a drug for **opioid-induced constipation**. ✅ His point: side-effect feedback becomes a new product line.

## The Market Inversion — From Mass Market to Micro Markets
- Old model: 20 million prescriptions at about $40. New model: sometimes 400 to 500 patients at "the highest price they can charge." ✅
- He frames this as a well-intentioned law with **unintended consequences**, not a planned scheme. 🟡 (his framing)

## The Orphan Drug Act (1983)
- Federal law giving incentives to develop drugs for rare diseases. Drafters debated the cutoff: first **100,000** patients, then expanded to **200,000 or fewer**. ✅ 🟢 Evidence check: the 1983 Act defined a rare disease only as one so infrequent that costs could not be recovered; a 1984 amendment set the "fewer than 200,000 persons in the US" threshold, and a disease above that still qualifies if costs cannot be recovered (21 U.S.C. 360bb).
- Why it was needed: large companies (Pfizer, Merck) had no reason to target small, mostly hereditary and genetic disease populations. ✅
- From the 1980s to the 2000s, orphan drugs became the **fastest-growing and most lucrative sector**, priced at **$300,000 to $3 million a year**. ✅

### The eight inducements

| Incentive | What it gives the company |
| --- | --- |
| Research grants | Taxpayer grants for early research, up to $500,000 a year |
| 50% R&D tax credit | Much higher than the normal R&D credit (✅ Evidence check: the 2017 tax law cut it to **25%** of qualified clinical-testing costs: RAPS 2017) |
| Deduction of the rest | Remaining 50% deducted as a business expense; net effect about **70% of R&D paid by the federal government** (🟡 his calculation, based on the pre-2018 50% credit) |
| Co-sponsorship | Simplified academic and NIH co-sponsorship |
| Fee waiver | FDA processing fees waived |
| Expedited review | Fast track without paying for it; the usual gauntlet of three or more rigorous trials "dramatically abbreviated" |
| Trial grants | Competitive FDA grants for clinical trials |
| **7-year exclusivity** | The prize: the "platinum mini-me patent" |

- Why seven years beat a 17 to 20 year patent: an ordinary patent still allows **me-too drugs** (his antibiotic example: Terramycin and Aureomycin competing with tetracycline). Orphan exclusivity means **no one else may sell a drug for that condition**, so competitors stop researching it. 🟡 Evidence check: legally it is narrower. For 7 years the FDA will not approve **the same drug for the same use**; a different drug for the same disease can still be approved, and even the same drug can be if it is clinically superior or the holder cannot supply enough (21 CFR 316.31).

### The loopholes
- **Grandfathered size:** if a drug gets orphan status while the disease is under 200,000 patients, it stays protected even if the population grows to millions (decided with AZT). ✅
- **Indication stacking:** splitting diseases into subtypes (for example a pediatric version) to win new exclusivity periods. ✅
- **Drug repurposing:** reusing an approved drug for a new orphan indication to extend protection. ✅
- **No "graduation" rule:** a drug that became a mass-market blockbuster can still collect orphan credits and exclusivity for each new rare indication. ✅
- **Price justification:** the drug is cheaper than five to seven years of hospital care; European payers require this calculation. ✅

## Case Studies — The Blueprint

### AZT — the template
- Samuel Broder led an NIH team looking for a drug to slow HIV replication; they focused on retroviral drugs and found **AZT**, patented but unused on Burroughs Wellcome's shelf. Burroughs could not even reconstitute it, so the NIH made it. ✅ (🟢 Court records show Burroughs Wellcome sent AZT, coded "Compound S", to Broder's NIH lab in February 1985, where Mitsuya showed it was active against HIV: *Burroughs Wellcome v. Barr*, 1994.)
- First human trial July 1985; approved in **20 months**, the fastest ever for an antiviral (normal could be eight to nine years). Activists still found it too slow. ✅ (approved 19 March 1987 after a record 20 months: POZ 2017)
- NIH spent **$50 million**; Burroughs Wellcome priced it at **$10,000 a patient**, then $8,000 after protests (🟡 reports put the launch price at about $8,000 a year: POZ 2017). **35% of AIDS patients were uninsured.** ✅
- A generic maker offered a copy at a couple of hundred dollars; federal court sided with Burroughs under orphan exclusivity. Posner says the fight over the patent reached the Supreme Court, where Burroughs' lawyers called the NIH "a pair of hands" and won. 🟡 Evidence check: the decisive ruling came from the **Federal Circuit** appeals court in November 1994, not the Supreme Court. Generic makers Barr and Novopharm argued that NIH scientists Broder and Mitsuya should be named co-inventors; the court largely sided with Burroughs Wellcome (*Burroughs Wellcome v. Barr*, 40 F.3d 1223). This was a patent case, not an orphan-exclusivity case.
- Wellcome's stock rose about 500%; AZT was about a third of revenue (around $450 million a year), passing $2 billion a year by 1994. Subsidy for the uninsured: about $300 million a year, then $400 million. 🟡 (figures move confusingly in the transcript)

### Truvada — thirty years later, same pattern
- Approved 2004 to treat HIV. CDC research on prevention from 2006; Gilead sent free doses in 2008; results published 2010 (JAMA and NEJM); in 2015 the US government patented the prophylactic (PrEP) use. ✅ (the 2010 iPrEx trial in NEJM: Grant 2010)
- Public money: Gates Foundation $17 million, NIH paid for human trials, a San Francisco researcher used $50 million in federal grants. ✅
- Gilead sued for the patent and **won**. 🟡 Evidence check: it was the US government that sued Gilead (2019), saying Truvada and Descovy sales for PrEP used CDC-held patents; in May 2023 a Delaware jury cleared Gilead and found the government's patent claims invalid (CNBC 2023). Posner calls it "one of the seminal battles in modern drug history": once you own a patent, no court will take it away, even from public-funded work. 🟡 (his interpretation)
- Cost about $6 a dose; price $1,800 a patient (🟡 most likely per month's supply; unverified); he states a **25,000% markup** 🟡 (the unit behind $1,800 is unclear, and the percentage does not follow cleanly from the two figures). Top-20 drug, about $3 billion a year, about $30 billion total until the patent expired in 2021. Gilead moved to a new formulation, **Descovy**. ✅

### Botox — orphan drug to cultural brand
- An ophthalmologist's drug, **Oculinum**, approved for two orphan eye-spasm indications (Posner says 1990; ✅ it was licensed on 29 December 1989 for strabismus and blepharospasm, and Allergan acquired the product in 1991: Scott 2023); Allergan bought the company for **$8 million** (as stated; unverified) and the rights in 1991. Botox = **botulinum toxin**. ✅
- Doctors noticed fewer wrinkles; dermatologists used it **off-label**. Allergan did not know about the cosmetic effect when it bought the drug, per Posner. ✅
- Cervical dystonia approval about ten years later. **2001 to 2010:** a covert campaign promoting it for headaches and pain. ✅
- **Black box warning** after two children died in cerebral palsy trials. It barely dented sales because patients never see the vial: the injector does. ✅ ("the black box warning with the least effect" is his judgment 🟡)
- Tactics: sales reps pushing off-label headache and pain use; workshops teaching doctors how to **disguise off-label injections for insurance coverage**; paid weekend getaways and dinners ("the Sackler playbook"); an Allergan-funded patient information outlet. ✅
- **2010:** guilty plea to one criminal misdemeanor (misbranding through off-label promotion); **$375 million** criminal fine plus **$225 million** civil settlement = **$600 million** (✅ US Department of Justice, 1 September 2010: the $375 million included $25 million in forfeited assets; the off-label uses were headache, pain, spasticity and juvenile cerebral palsy, and Allergan ran workshops on billing for off-label uses), including False Claims Act exposure for Medicare, Medicaid and VA claims; a Corporate Integrity Agreement. ✅ Posner: fines are "the cost of doing business." 🟡
- Moat: fewer than a dozen staff know the exact fermentation recipe; about a dozen more orphan designations are being tested, each with new credits and seven-year exclusivity. ✅
- Rough revenue split he gives: 70% cosmetic, 20% migraine, 10% overactive bladder; about 20% of company revenue, over $3 billion a year. 🟡 (his rough breakdown)

### Acthar gel — the price is the business model
- Approved **1952** for **West syndrome**, a rare, often fatal infant epileptic disorder (🟡 as stated; we could not confirm that the 1952 approval named West syndrome); made from slaughtered pigs' pituitary glands; discovered by **Armour**, the meatpacker. ✅
- Sanofi-Aventis (1990s): $40 a vial for an estimated 700 to 900 patients a year; losing money. Questcor (2001): $1,650, still losing money. ✅ (the $40-a-vial starting price matches the FTC case record: NAAG 2017)
- **2007:** new CEO **Don Bailey**, with no drug-industry background, raised it to **$23,000 a vial**, reformulated it as a gel and got **orphan status** again (seven more years). Pushed for nephrotic syndrome, rheumatoid arthritis, lupus. ✅
- Stock went from $0.60 to $50; insiders sold over $100 million in two years. **Government programs paid more than $2 billion (2011 to 2016).** ✅
- **60% of the sales spike went to Medicare seniors**, largely off-label for rheumatoid arthritis, where Posner says there is no clinical evidence it helps. Dr. Dennis Bourdette (Oregon Health & Science University): "a mystery to me why someone would be prescribing the drug." ✅ (🟢 A study led from Oregon Health & Science University of US Medicare prescribers found little evidence for Acthar over ordinary corticosteroids and that 88% of frequent prescribers received payments from its maker: Hartung 2018.)
- **Killing the cheap rival:** Canadian chemical cousin **Synacthen** sold for $33 a vial. Mallinckrodt bought Questcor, then bought Synacthen's maker and took it off the market. 🟡 Evidence check: it was **Questcor** that bought the US rights to Synacthen from Novartis (2012, outbidding three rivals with $135 million in guaranteed payments), before Mallinckrodt acquired Questcor; in 2017 Mallinckrodt paid $100 million to settle FTC and state antitrust charges over the deal (NAAG 2017).

### Daraprim — Shkreli and the "bad actor" story
- 2015: Martin Shkreli bought Daraprim (rare parasitic infections in people with weakened immune systems) for **$55 million** and raised it from **$13.50 to $750 a pill**, about 5,000%. Government cost for a month's supply went from **$608 to $35,556.48**. ✅ (🟢 court reporting gives the old price as $17.50 a tablet; the monthly government figures are as stated and unverified)
- Industry response: a **$60 million image campaign**, internal line **"more lab coat, less hoodie"**: blame the outsider, not the model. 🟡 (as stated; unverified)
- Shkreli went to prison for **securities fraud, not drug pricing**, and the company **kept the $750 price** after he left. ✅ (In January 2022 a federal court also banned him from the drug industry for life and ordered him to repay $64.6 million for blocking generic competition: Jurist 2022.)

## Why It Keeps Happening — Posner's Diagnosis
- No price regulation for orphan drugs; exclusivity extensions; broad eligibility; inadequate FDA oversight; no transparency on incentives and costs. 🟡 (his list)
- **Direct-to-consumer ads** (1997 FDA change): first ad was Claritin ("It's time... Ask your doctor"). A year later the industry spent $1 billion a year on TV; in 2025 about $7 to $8 billion on TV and $10 billion overall. 🟢 (Checked trend: US consumer advertising for prescription drugs grew from $1.3 billion in 1997 to $6 billion in 2016: Schwartz 2019. The 2025 figures are as stated, and "Claritin was first" is unverified.)
- **Reforms he lists:** tighter orphan eligibility, stricter price controls (he expects the "socialized medicine" fight), cost and pricing transparency, and letting equivalent generics end the seven-year exclusivity. He says none has traction in Congress. 🟡

## Ultra-Expensive Drugs and the Future
- New wave: gene therapies, enzyme replacement, biologics and cancer drugs for genetic subtypes, often **single-dose**. Regulators lag, patients can't shop around, and small patient groups resist regulation. Prices are "justified as innovation" while research relies on public funding. 🟡

| Drug | Use (as stated) | Price | Patient population |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Lenmeldy | MLD, a progressive nervous system disorder | $4.25 million | Under 4,000 |
| Kebilidi | Rare genetic brain disease | $3.95 million | Under 5,000 |
| Hemgenix | Hemophilia B | $3.5 million | Under 8,000 |
| Elevidys | Rare form of muscular dystrophy | $3.2 million | Under 13,000 |

- Novartis (drug not named) considered $5 million for a pediatric therapy, priced it at $2.1 million, and pioneered an **installment plan** for insurers ("layaway"). ✅
- **The 10 most expensive drugs in the world are all orphan drugs**, per Posner. 🟡 (🟢 broadly consistent: a November 2025 list of the 10 most expensive US drugs is dominated by one-time gene therapies for rare diseases, led by Lenmeldy at $4.25 million, with Hemgenix $3.5 million, Elevidys $3.2 million and Zolgensma $2.125 million: Drugs.com. Kebilidi's price is as stated.)
- Future: gene editing and cell therapies, micro-indications for genomic subtypes, and the "Holy Grail" of a drug tailored to one person's DNA, priced high and repeated "a few billion times." Prices that exceed the cost of a home. 🟡

## PBMs — The Invisible Middlemen
- **Pharmacy benefit managers** administer drug benefits for private insurers, Medicare Part D, employer plans and Medicaid managed care. ✅
- Origin: **PCS** in Scottsdale, Arizona, first a claims processor (like payroll for medical claims). After the HMO Act, it became the first firm to process prescriptions, build **formularies** and reimburse pharmacies; it was bought by **McKesson**, which wanted to influence coverage. ✅ (dates in the transcript are loose 🟡)
- PBMs decide which drugs are covered, what pharmacies can dispense, copays and which manufacturer gains share. Community pharmacists "hate PBMs." ✅
- **Rebates** from manufacturers for formulary placement are secret; **spread pricing** adds another billing tier. ✅
- The perverse incentive: PBMs prefer **higher list prices and bigger rebates**, not lower net prices; manufacturers inflate list prices to fund rebates; **patients pay cost-sharing on the inflated list price**. ✅
- 🟢 Evidence check: a 2024 FTC staff report found the three largest PBMs processed nearly 80% of US prescriptions in 2023, that some manufacturer rebates were tied to keeping cheaper generics and biosimilars off formularies, and that pharmacies affiliated with the big three took nearly 70% of specialty-drug revenue (FTC 2024).
- Posner: PBMs are an American invention that doesn't exist in France, Germany or England, and the industry will eventually "throw PBMs under the bus." 🟡

## Punchlines & Quotable Facts (Lecture 7)
- **"The profit here comes from scarcity, not volume."** The orphan-drug inversion in one line.
- **Seven years of exclusivity = the "platinum mini-me patent."** No competing drug for that condition at all.
- **About 70% of orphan R&D was effectively paid by the federal government** (50% credit plus deduction).
- **NIH "a pair of hands."** Burroughs Wellcome's description of the agency that made AZT.
- **Acthar: $40, then $1,650, then $23,000 a vial for the same pig-pituitary product.**
- **Daraprim: $13.50 to $750 a pill, and the price stayed after Shkreli went to prison.**
- **Every one of the 10 most expensive drugs is an orphan drug** (his claim).
- **PBMs profit from high list prices, and patients pay on the list price.**

## Key Corrections to Carry Forward
- 🔴 "Orphan drugs are a small niche": per Posner they became the fastest-growing, most lucrative sector.
- 🔴 "Orphan status ends when a disease becomes common": not under the Act; AZT kept protection as HIV passed millions.
- 🔴 "A new price means a new drug": Acthar and Daraprim show the same product repriced by new owners.
- 🔴 "Black box warnings reach patients": for injected drugs like Botox, the patient never sees the vial.
- 🔴 "Shkreli went to prison for drug pricing": it was securities fraud, and the price stayed.
- 🔴 "The list price is what you pay": rebates and PBMs distort it; ask for the out-of-pocket cost.
- 🔴 "Most expensive = most effective": Posner says none of these ultra-expensive therapies are cures; they treat symptoms or slow progression.

## Practical Takeaway
Posner's "smarter patient" list, as study points (he stresses he is not a doctor and it is not medical advice): (1) ask whether a prescribed drug is an orphan drug; your doctor should know; (2) unless it's a specific genetic illness, ask about non-orphan or generic alternatives; (3) understand that PBMs shape which drugs you are offered; (4) be wary of being routed to a specialty pharmacy without a clear reason (such as compounding); (5) ask for the actual out-of-pocket cost, not the list price; (6) remember the most expensive therapy is not automatically the most effective.

## Questions to Learn From (Socratic) — with Answer Options
**1. Given how the Orphan Drug Act's seven-year exclusivity works, why did companies prize it over a longer ordinary patent?**
A. Because it lasted longer than a 17 to 20 year patent
B. \* Because it blocked any competing drug for that condition, including me-too drugs, so rivals stopped researching it
C. Because it required no FDA approval at all
*Why B: in Posner's account, an ordinary patent still allows competitors like Terramycin and Aureomycin next to tetracycline, while orphan exclusivity shuts out competition for that indication. (Legally it blocks the same drug for the same use; see the evidence check above.)*

**2. Given the AZT story, what happened when the HIV population grew far past 200,000?**
A. \* AZT kept its orphan protection, because status granted while the disease was under the cutoff stays even if the population grows
B. Burroughs Wellcome had to repay its tax credits
C. Generics were immediately allowed in
*Why A: Congress had not anticipated the question, and the decision was that protection survives growth to millions.*

**3. Given the Acthar case, which mechanism drove most of the revenue jump?**
A. A new active molecule that outperformed existing therapies
B. Large new clinical evidence for rheumatoid arthritis
C. \* A price increase on essentially the same product, renewed orphan status for a gel reformulation, off-label Medicare use and removal of the cheap rival Synacthen
*Why C: the product came from the same pig pituitary source; the changes were price, exclusivity, prescribing patterns and competition.*

**4. Given Posner's PBM explanation, why might a drug with a higher list price be favored on a formulary?**
A. Because a higher list price signals better quality
B. \* Because higher list prices support bigger confidential rebates to the PBM, while patients' cost-sharing is based on the inflated list price
C. Because federal law requires formularies to favor expensive drugs
*Why B: PBMs prefer larger rebates over lower net prices, and the rebate pool grows with the list price.*
