
# Posner — The Pharmaceutical Industry, Ch.7: Price of Monopoly & Orphan Drugs
*How a well-meant 1983 law for rare diseases became the industry's most lucrative model: orphan-drug monopolies (AZT, Truvada, Botox, Acthar, Daraprim), seven-figure gene therapies, PBM rebates, and how to be a smarter patient.*

*Source: Gerald Posner, Peterson Academy course "The Pharmaceutical Industry," Lecture 7 (transcript). Seventh of eight lectures; follows Ch.6 on political capture and sets up Ch.8 on pandemic profits. Posner is an investigative journalist (author of "Pharma"), not a scientist: his historical facts are marked ✅ when clearly stated, his interpretations 🟡. Transcript reads as complete; it ends on his "smarter patient" checklist.*

**In plain words:** in 1983 the US passed a law to encourage drugs for rare ("orphan") diseases, with tax breaks, grants and seven years with no competition. It worked, but it also became the industry's most profitable model: a drug for a few thousand people can be priced at hundreds of thousands or millions of dollars. Posner shows this with AZT, Truvada, Botox, Acthar and Daraprim, then explains the middlemen (PBMs) who quietly decide which drugs your insurance covers and why high list prices can suit them. His facts are marked ✅, his opinions 🟡, with a 2026 source check where the record differs.

**Words in this note:**
- **PBM**: pharmacy benefit manager, a middleman that handles drug prices for insurers
- **PBMs**: pharmacy benefit managers, middlemen that handle drug prices for insurers
- **NIH**: U.S. National Institutes of Health

## Why This Matters
Earlier lectures followed the mass-market model (sell a lot of a cheap drug). This lecture shows the reverse. Since the Orphan Drug Act, the most profitable drugs can be ones that treat a few hundred or a few thousand people at $300,000 to $4 million each. Posner's main argument: **profit comes from scarcity, not volume**. The system that allows it is a mix of patent law, orphan exclusivity (a period with no competition), off-label promotion (selling a drug for uses it was not approved for) and hidden middlemen called PBMs. What it means for a patient: the list price, the formulary (the insurer's list of covered drugs) and which pharmacy you are sent to are shaped by forces that have nothing to do with how well a drug works.

## Opening Aside — Profiting From Side Effects
- Posner says a 1960s company saw a drug with many side effects as a "gateway drug": not addictive, but a source of further drugs to treat its side effects. 🟡 (company and drug not named)
- Purdue Pharma's attempt to avoid being a "one-hit wonder" was a drug for **constipation caused by opioids**. ✅ His point: side effects become a new product line.

## The Market Inversion — From Mass Market to Micro Markets
- The old model: 20 million prescriptions at about $40. The new model: sometimes 400 to 500 patients at "the highest price they can charge." ✅
- He presents this as a well-meant law with **unintended consequences**, not a planned scheme. 🟡 (his framing)

## The Orphan Drug Act (1983)
- A federal law giving rewards for developing drugs for rare diseases. The drafters argued about the cutoff: first **100,000** patients, then widened to **200,000 or fewer**. ✅ 🟢 Evidence check: the 1983 Act defined a rare disease only as one so uncommon that the costs of a drug could not be earned back. A 1984 change set the limit at "fewer than 200,000 persons in the US," and a disease above that still qualifies if costs cannot be earned back (21 U.S.C. 360bb).
- Why it was needed: big companies (Pfizer, Merck) had no reason to aim at small groups of patients, mostly with inherited and genetic diseases. ✅
- From the 1980s to the 2000s, orphan drugs became the **fastest-growing and most profitable sector**, priced at **$300,000 to $3 million a year**. ✅

### The eight inducements

| Incentive | What it gives the company |
| --- | --- |
| Research grants | Taxpayer grants for early research, up to $500,000 a year |
| 50% R&D tax credit | Much higher than the normal research tax credit (✅ Evidence check: the 2017 tax law cut it to **25%** of qualifying clinical-testing costs: RAPS 2017) |
| Deduction of the rest | The other 50% deducted as a business expense; net effect about **70% of R&D paid by the federal government** (🟡 his calculation, based on the 50% credit before 2018) |
| Co-sponsorship | Simpler joint sponsorship with universities and the NIH (National Institutes of Health) |
| Fee waiver | FDA processing fees waived |
| Expedited review | Fast track without paying for it; the usual hurdle of three or more strict trials "dramatically abbreviated" |
| Trial grants | Competitive FDA grants for medical trials |
| **7-year exclusivity** | The prize: the "platinum mini-me patent" |

- Why seven years beats a 17 to 20 year patent: an ordinary patent still allows **me-too drugs** (his antibiotic example: Terramycin and Aureomycin competing with tetracycline). Orphan exclusivity means **no one else may sell a drug for that condition**, so competitors stop researching it. 🟡 Evidence check: in law it is narrower. For 7 years the FDA will not approve **the same drug for the same use**. A different drug for the same disease can still be approved, and even the same drug can be if it is clearly better or the holder cannot supply enough (21 CFR 316.31).

### The loopholes
- **Size frozen at the start:** if a drug gets orphan status while the disease has under 200,000 patients, it stays protected even if the number grows to millions (decided with AZT). ✅
- **Stacking indications:** splitting diseases into subtypes (for example a children's version) to win new periods of exclusivity. ✅
- **Repurposing drugs:** reusing an approved drug for a new rare-disease use to extend protection. ✅
- **No "graduation" rule:** a drug that became a mass-market best-seller can still collect orphan tax credits and exclusivity for each new rare use. ✅
- **Justifying the price:** the drug costs less than five to seven years of hospital care; European payers require this calculation. ✅

## Case Studies — The Blueprint

### AZT — the template
- Samuel Broder led an NIH team looking for a drug to slow HIV from copying itself. They focused on retroviral drugs (drugs against the virus family HIV belongs to) and found **AZT**, patented but unused on Burroughs Wellcome's shelf. Burroughs could not even remake it, so the NIH made it. ✅ (🟢 Court records show Burroughs Wellcome sent AZT, coded "Compound S", to Broder's NIH lab in February 1985, where Mitsuya showed it worked against HIV: *Burroughs Wellcome v. Barr*, 1994.)
- The first trial in people was in July 1985; it was approved in **20 months**, the fastest ever for an antiviral (the normal time could be eight to nine years). Activists still found it too slow. ✅ (approved 19 March 1987 after a record 20 months: POZ 2017)
- The NIH spent **$50 million**; Burroughs Wellcome priced it at **$10,000 a patient**, then $8,000 after protests (🟡 reports put the launch price at about $8,000 a year: POZ 2017). **35% of AIDS patients had no insurance.** ✅
- A generic maker offered a copy for a couple of hundred dollars; a federal court sided with Burroughs under orphan exclusivity. Posner says the patent fight reached the Supreme Court, where Burroughs' lawyers called the NIH "a pair of hands" and won. 🟡 Evidence check: the deciding ruling came from the **Federal Circuit** appeals court in November 1994, not the Supreme Court. The generic makers Barr and Novopharm argued that NIH scientists Broder and Mitsuya should be named co-inventors; the court mostly sided with Burroughs Wellcome (*Burroughs Wellcome v. Barr*, 40 F.3d 1223). This was a patent case, not an orphan-exclusivity case.
- Wellcome's stock rose about 500%; AZT was about a third of its revenue (around $450 million a year), passing $2 billion a year by 1994. Help for patients without insurance: about $300 million a year, then $400 million. 🟡 (the figures shift confusingly in the transcript)

### Truvada — thirty years later, same pattern
- Approved in 2004 to treat HIV. CDC research on using it to prevent HIV began in 2006; Gilead sent free doses in 2008; results were published in 2010 (JAMA and NEJM); in 2015 the US government patented its use for prevention (PrEP, pre-exposure prophylaxis). ✅ (the 2010 iPrEx trial in NEJM: Grant 2010)
- Public money: the Gates Foundation $17 million, the NIH paid for trials in people, and a San Francisco researcher used $50 million in federal grants. ✅
- Gilead sued for the patent and **won**. 🟡 Evidence check: it was the US government that sued Gilead (2019), saying sales of Truvada and Descovy for PrEP used patents held by the CDC. In May 2023 a Delaware jury cleared Gilead and found the government's patent claims invalid (CNBC 2023). Posner calls it "one of the seminal battles in modern drug history": once you own a patent, no court will take it away, even for work paid for by the public. 🟡 (his interpretation)
- It cost about $6 a dose to make; the price was $1,800 a patient (🟡 most likely per month's supply; unverified); he gives a **25,000% markup** 🟡 (what the $1,800 covers is unclear, and the percentage does not follow neatly from the two figures). A top-20 drug, about $3 billion a year, about $30 billion in total until the patent ran out in 2021. Gilead moved patients to a new version, **Descovy**. ✅

### Botox — orphan drug to cultural brand
- An eye doctor's drug, **Oculinum**, approved for two rare eye-spasm conditions (Posner says 1990; ✅ it was licensed on 29 December 1989 for strabismus and blepharospasm, and Allergan acquired the product in 1991: Scott 2023). Allergan bought the company for **$8 million** (as stated; unverified) and the rights in 1991. Botox = **botulinum toxin**, a nerve poison used in tiny doses. ✅
- Doctors noticed fewer wrinkles; skin doctors used it **off-label**. According to Posner, Allergan did not know about the cosmetic effect when it bought the drug. ✅
- Approval for cervical dystonia (painful neck spasms) came about ten years later. **2001 to 2010:** a hidden campaign promoting it for headaches and pain. ✅
- A **black box warning** (the FDA's strongest warning) after two children died in trials for cerebral palsy. It hardly dented sales, because patients never see the vial: the person giving the injection does. ✅ ("the black box warning with the least effect" is his judgment 🟡)
- Tactics: sales reps pushing off-label use for headache and pain; workshops teaching doctors how to **disguise off-label injections so insurance would pay**; paid weekend trips and dinners ("the Sackler playbook"); a patient information outlet paid for by Allergan. ✅
- **2010:** a guilty plea to one minor crime (misbranding by promoting off-label uses); a **$375 million** criminal fine plus a **$225 million** civil settlement = **$600 million** (✅ US Department of Justice, 1 September 2010: the $375 million included $25 million in forfeited assets; the off-label uses were headache, pain, spasticity and juvenile cerebral palsy, and Allergan ran workshops on billing for off-label uses). This included liability under the False Claims Act for Medicare, Medicaid and VA (veterans') claims, and a Corporate Integrity Agreement (a compliance deal with the government). ✅ Posner: fines are "the cost of doing business." 🟡
- The moat: fewer than a dozen staff know the exact recipe for growing it; about a dozen more orphan uses are being tested, each with new tax credits and seven-year exclusivity. ✅
- The rough revenue split he gives: 70% cosmetic, 20% migraine, 10% overactive bladder; about 20% of the company's revenue, over $3 billion a year. 🟡 (his rough breakdown)

### Acthar gel — the price is the business model
- Approved in **1952** for **West syndrome**, a rare, often fatal form of epilepsy in babies (🟡 as stated; we could not confirm that the 1952 approval named West syndrome). It is made from the pituitary glands of slaughtered pigs and was discovered by **Armour**, the meatpacker. ✅
- Sanofi-Aventis (1990s): $40 a vial for an estimated 700 to 900 patients a year; losing money. Questcor (2001): $1,650, still losing money. ✅ (the $40-a-vial starting price matches the FTC case record: NAAG 2017)
- **2007:** a new chief executive, **Don Bailey**, with no background in the drug industry, raised it to **$23,000 a vial**, reformulated it as a gel and got **orphan status** again (seven more years). He pushed it for nephrotic syndrome (a kidney disorder), rheumatoid arthritis and lupus. ✅
- The stock went from $0.60 to $50; insiders sold over $100 million in two years. **Government programs paid more than $2 billion (2011 to 2016).** ✅
- **60% of the sales jump came from Medicare seniors**, mostly off-label for rheumatoid arthritis, where Posner says there is no medical evidence it helps. Dr. Dennis Bourdette (Oregon Health & Science University): "a mystery to me why someone would be prescribing the drug." ✅ (🟢 A study led from Oregon Health & Science University of US Medicare prescribers found little evidence for Acthar over ordinary corticosteroids, and that 88% of frequent prescribers received payments from its maker: Hartung 2018.)
- **Killing the cheap rival:** a Canadian chemical cousin, **Synacthen**, sold for $33 a vial. Mallinckrodt bought Questcor, then bought Synacthen's maker and took it off the market. 🟡 Evidence check: it was **Questcor** that bought the US rights to Synacthen from Novartis (2012, outbidding three rivals with $135 million in guaranteed payments), before Mallinckrodt bought Questcor. In 2017 Mallinckrodt paid $100 million to settle FTC and state charges of blocking competition over the deal (NAAG 2017).

### Daraprim — Shkreli and the "bad actor" story
- 2015: Martin Shkreli bought Daraprim (for rare parasite infections in people with weak immune systems) for **$55 million** and raised it from **$13.50 to $750 a pill**, about 5,000%. The government's cost for a month's supply went from **$608 to $35,556.48**. ✅ (🟢 court reporting gives the old price as $17.50 a tablet; the monthly government figures are as stated and unverified)
- The industry's response: a **$60 million image campaign**, with the internal line **"more lab coat, less hoodie"**: blame the outsider, not the business model. 🟡 (as stated; unverified)
- Shkreli went to prison for **securities fraud (cheating investors), not for drug pricing**, and the company **kept the $750 price** after he left. ✅ (In January 2022 a federal court also banned him from the drug industry for life and ordered him to repay $64.6 million for blocking generic competition: Jurist 2022.)

## Why It Keeps Happening — Posner's Diagnosis
- No price rules for orphan drugs; extra periods of exclusivity; broad eligibility; weak FDA oversight; no openness about rewards and costs. 🟡 (his list)
- **Drug ads aimed straight at the public** (an FDA change in 1997): the first ad was for Claritin ("It's time... Ask your doctor"). A year later the industry spent $1 billion a year on TV; in 2025 about $7 to $8 billion on TV and $10 billion overall. 🟢 (Checked trend: US consumer advertising for prescription drugs grew from $1.3 billion in 1997 to $6 billion in 2016: Schwartz 2019. The 2025 figures are as stated, and "Claritin was first" is unverified.)
- **Reforms he lists:** stricter rules on what counts as an orphan drug, tighter price controls (he expects the "socialized medicine" fight), openness about costs and pricing, and letting equal generics end the seven-year exclusivity. He says none has support in Congress. 🟡

## Ultra-Expensive Drugs and the Future
- The new wave: gene therapies (fixing or replacing a faulty gene), enzyme replacement, biologics (medicines made from living cells) and cancer drugs for genetic subtypes, often given as a **single dose**. Regulators lag behind, patients cannot shop around, and small patient groups resist regulation. Prices are "justified as innovation," while the research relies on public money. 🟡

| Drug | Use (as stated) | Price | Patient population |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Lenmeldy | MLD, a nervous system disorder that gets steadily worse | $4.25 million | Under 4,000 |
| Kebilidi | Rare genetic brain disease | $3.95 million | Under 5,000 |
| Hemgenix | Hemophilia B (a bleeding disorder) | $3.5 million | Under 8,000 |
| Elevidys | Rare form of muscular dystrophy (muscle-wasting disease) | $3.2 million | Under 13,000 |

- Novartis (drug not named) considered $5 million for a children's therapy, priced it at $2.1 million, and pioneered an **installment plan** for insurers ("layaway"). ✅
- **The 10 most expensive drugs in the world are all orphan drugs**, according to Posner. 🟡 (🟢 broadly consistent: a November 2025 list of the 10 most expensive US drugs is dominated by one-time gene therapies for rare diseases, led by Lenmeldy at $4.25 million, with Hemgenix $3.5 million, Elevidys $3.2 million and Zolgensma $2.125 million: Drugs.com. Kebilidi's price is as stated.)
- The future: gene editing and cell therapies, ever-smaller uses for genetic subtypes, and the "Holy Grail" of a drug made for one person's DNA, priced high and repeated "a few billion times." Prices higher than the cost of a home. 🟡

## PBMs — The Invisible Middlemen
- **Pharmacy benefit managers** run drug benefits for private insurers, Medicare Part D, employer plans and Medicaid managed care. ✅
- Where they came from: **PCS** in Scottsdale, Arizona, first a claims processor (like payroll, but for medical claims). After the HMO Act, it became the first firm to process prescriptions, build **formularies** and pay pharmacies back; it was bought by **McKesson**, which wanted a say in what was covered. ✅ (dates in the transcript are loose 🟡)
- PBMs decide which drugs are covered, what pharmacies can hand out, the copays (your share at the counter) and which drug maker gains market share. Local pharmacists "hate PBMs." ✅
- **Rebates** (money paid back by drug makers) for a good place on the formulary are secret; **spread pricing** (charging the insurer more than the PBM pays the pharmacy) adds another layer of billing. ✅
- The upside-down incentive: PBMs prefer **higher list prices and bigger rebates**, not lower real prices. Drug makers raise list prices to pay for rebates, and **patients pay their share on the inflated list price**. ✅
- 🟢 Evidence check: a 2024 FTC staff report found that the three largest PBMs handled nearly 80% of US prescriptions in 2023, that some drug-maker rebates were tied to keeping cheaper generics and biosimilars (near-copies of biologic drugs) off formularies, and that pharmacies linked to the big three took nearly 70% of revenue from specialty drugs (FTC 2024).
- Posner: PBMs are an American invention that does not exist in France, Germany or England, and the industry will eventually "throw PBMs under the bus." 🟡

## Punchlines & Quotable Facts (Lecture 7)
- **"The profit here comes from scarcity, not volume."** The orphan-drug reversal in one line.
- **Seven years of exclusivity = the "platinum mini-me patent."** No competing drug for that condition at all.
- **About 70% of orphan R&D was in effect paid by the federal government** (50% credit plus deduction).
- **NIH "a pair of hands."** Burroughs Wellcome's description of the agency that made AZT.
- **Acthar: $40, then $1,650, then $23,000 a vial for the same pig-pituitary product.**
- **Daraprim: $13.50 to $750 a pill, and the price stayed after Shkreli went to prison.**
- **Every one of the 10 most expensive drugs is an orphan drug** (his claim).
- **PBMs profit from high list prices, and patients pay on the list price.**

## Key Corrections to Carry Forward
- 🔴 "Orphan drugs are a small niche": according to Posner they became the fastest-growing, most profitable sector.
- 🔴 "Orphan status ends when a disease becomes common": not under the Act; AZT kept its protection as HIV passed millions.
- 🔴 "A new price means a new drug": Acthar and Daraprim show the same product re-priced by new owners.
- 🔴 "Black box warnings reach patients": for injected drugs like Botox, the patient never sees the vial.
- 🔴 "Shkreli went to prison for drug pricing": it was securities fraud, and the price stayed.
- 🔴 "The list price is what you pay": rebates and PBMs distort it; ask what you will actually pay.
- 🔴 "Most expensive = most effective": Posner says none of these ultra-expensive therapies are cures; they treat symptoms or slow the disease.

## Practical Takeaway
Posner's "smarter patient" list, as study points (he stresses he is not a doctor and this is not medical advice): (1) ask whether a prescribed drug is an orphan drug; your doctor should know; (2) unless it is for a specific genetic illness, ask about non-orphan or generic alternatives; (3) understand that PBMs shape which drugs you are offered; (4) be wary of being sent to a specialty pharmacy without a clear reason (such as compounding, mixing a drug specially); (5) ask for what you will actually pay out of pocket, not the list price; (6) remember the most expensive therapy is not automatically the most effective.

**1. Given how the Orphan Drug Act's seven-year exclusivity works, why did companies prize it over a longer ordinary patent?**
A. Because it lasted longer than a 17 to 20 year patent
B. \* Because it blocked any competing drug for that condition, including me-too drugs, so rivals stopped researching it
C. Because it required no FDA approval at all
*Why B: in Posner's account, an ordinary patent still allows competitors such as Terramycin and Aureomycin next to tetracycline, while orphan exclusivity shuts out competition for that use. (In law it blocks the same drug for the same use; see the evidence check above.)*

**2. Given the AZT story, what happened when the HIV population grew far past 200,000?**
A. \* AZT kept its orphan protection, because status granted while the disease was under the cutoff stays even if the population grows
B. Burroughs Wellcome had to repay its tax credits
C. Generics were immediately allowed in
*Why A: Congress had not foreseen the question, and the decision was that protection survives growth to millions.*

**3. Given the Acthar case, which mechanism drove most of the revenue jump?**
A. A new active molecule that outperformed existing therapies
B. Large new clinical evidence for rheumatoid arthritis
C. \* A price increase on essentially the same product, renewed orphan status for a gel reformulation, off-label Medicare use and removal of the cheap rival Synacthen
*Why C: the product came from the same pig-pituitary source; what changed was the price, the exclusivity, how it was prescribed and the competition.*

**4. Given Posner's PBM explanation, why might a drug with a higher list price be favored on a formulary?**
A. Because a higher list price signals better quality
B. \* Because higher list prices support bigger confidential rebates to the PBM, while patients' cost-sharing is based on the inflated list price
C. Because federal law requires formularies to favor expensive drugs
*Why B: PBMs prefer bigger rebates to lower real prices, and the rebate pot grows with the list price.*
