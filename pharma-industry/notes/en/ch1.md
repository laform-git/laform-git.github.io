
# Posner — The Pharmaceutical Industry, Ch.1: Snake Oil Origins
*How the drug industry was born selling morphine, cocaine, heroin and barbiturates as miracle cures in an unregulated market, and why Posner says that origin is still its DNA.*

*Source: Gerald Posner, Peterson Academy course "The Pharmaceutical Industry" (introduced on screen as "Secrets of Big Pharma: How to Be a Smarter Patient"), Lecture 1 (transcript). First of eight lectures; covers roughly 1800 to the 1920s. Transcript complete: it closes with a preview of Lecture 2 and "Thank you very much."*

## Why This Matters
This is the origin story the rest of the course builds on. Posner's thesis is that the industry's start (addictive products, no prescriptions, no safety or efficacy proof, marketing ahead of science, early lobbying against regulation) is its "DNA" and a "template" that keeps repeating. Posner is an investigative journalist and lawyer (author of *Pharma*), not a scientist or physician, so his historical facts are marked ✅ when clearly stated and his interpretations 🟡. Several dates and attributions in the lecture are loose; a source audit (October 2026) adds inline evidence checks where the record differs, with references at the end.

## The Thesis — "Before Big Pharma, There Was Big Hype"
- The industry started in a **"marketplace of ignorance"**: no national standards, no prescription laws, no medical licensing, no safety requirements, no duty to disclose ingredients or harms. "Anyone could call themselves a doctor." ✅
- The winners sold **"dreams," not evidence**: exaggerated promises, no clinical studies required. 🟡 (his framing)
- The repeating cycle he says you will see all course long: a "miracle pill" is pushed hard to doctors, through ads and sponsored studies; the public adopts it enthusiastically; competitors chase "me-too" versions to get their own patent; extra benefits get floated; only later do side-effect reports arrive ("I wish we had known 5 or 10 years ago"). His current example: **GLP-1 weight-loss drugs** (talk of dementia, heart and "vigor" benefits). 🟡 (his pattern claim)
- "It's not a coincidence the industry grew up on addictive products": an addictive product creates a customer who keeps coming back, which cartels also understand. 🟡

## Why Quack Cures Sold — Medical Ignorance
Posner's background argument: when people do not know what makes them sick, fear plus a confident cure claim is easy to sell. ✅ (as argument)
- Germ theory was new and widely dismissed. He credits **Louis Pasteur** (spoiled meat and milk) and a **German bacteriologist (1882)**, unnamed in the lecture but this is **Robert Koch**, who showed tuberculosis comes from microorganisms, when many thought it was inherited. 🟡 His statement that Pasteur's work came "a few decades" before the mid-1800s drugs is chronologically loose.
- Disease blamed on people, not microbes: he says an 1882 Immigration Act was driven by cholera fears (attributed to Irish immigrants) 🟡 (unverified: we found no source tying the 1882 Act to cholera fears), and quotes the **1891 Immigration Act** excluding "all idiots, insane persons, paupers... and persons suffering from a loathsome or dangerous contagious disease." ✅ (quote as read)
- **Walter Reed (1900)** showed mosquitoes spread yellow fever. Posner calls him an "army general"; ✅ Evidence check: Reed was an Army surgeon (rank of major) who led the Army's Yellow Fever Board in Cuba (Pierce 2003).

## Morphine and the War Origins of the Companies
- **Morphine (1804):** a 21-year-old German pharmacy apprentice isolated the first pure alkaloid from the opium poppy and named it after **Morpheus**, Greek god of dreams. Its pain-relief value was quickly recognized. ✅
- Early quality morphine came from two German firms, **Bayer** and **Schering** (later "Schering & Plough"). 🟡 He places this in "the early 1800s". ✅ Evidence check: the first large-scale commercial morphine maker was **E. Merck** of Darmstadt, from 1827 (Merck corporate history); Bayer and Schering were founded decades later.
- **Mexican-American War (1846–48):** **87% of US troop fatalities** were from untreated disease (yellow fever, dysentery, cholera), not combat. ✅ Evidence check: 10,986 of 12,535 US war deaths (88%) were from infectious disease, mostly dysentery (Cirillo 2009). Morphine reaching the front was often adulterated by middlemen who diluted it, so soldiers got little relief. ✅
- That failure created the American market. Posner's roll call of founders:

| Company | Origin as Posner tells it |
| --- | --- |
| **Charles Pfizer & Co.** | German cousins in New York, one year after the war; small savings plus a loan (figures garbled in transcript 🟡); factory on Bartlett Street; first serious US maker of high-quality morphine. 🟡 Evidence check: founded 1849 in Williamsburg, Brooklyn, by Charles Pfizer and his cousin Charles Erhart with a $2,500 loan from Pfizer's father; its first product was santonin, a worm remedy, not morphine (Pfizer 2024) |
| **E. R. Squibb & Sons** | Edward Robinson Squibb saw bad morphine in the war (often thrown overboard); set up in Brooklyn. Posner calls him a "colonel" 🟡; he was a US Navy medical officer, and set up his Brooklyn lab in 1858 (Britannica) |
| **Wyeth** | John and Frank Wyeth, Philadelphia pharmacists, one year into the Civil War; a Union Army contract so lucrative they dropped the pharmacy for manufacturing |
| **Parke-Davis** | Samuel Duffield (Detroit) sold an ether-and-alcohol pain solution under a Union contract; bought by Hervey Parke (mining investor) and George Davis (26-year-old salesman) |
| **Eli Lilly** | Civil War veteran who founded the company just after; main products opium- and morphine-based |
| **Burroughs Wellcome** | Silas Burroughs and Henry Wellcome, American pharmacists who went to the UK; laxatives and opium-based creams |

- The **Civil War (1861)** was "good for the morphine business" because of record battlefield injuries. ✅ Posner's point: these were not passing firms but the ancestors of today's giants, launched on "an addictive product that has no restrictions on how it's dispensed." 🟡 (interpretation)

## Cocaine — The Second Blockbuster
- **1859:** a German doctoral student (**Albert Niemann**, Göttingen; thesis completed 1860) isolated the alkaloid from the coca plant and named it **cocaine** (coca + Latin-derived *-ina*, "from"). ✅
- Posner adds that the same chemist later developed mustard gas and calls him "the inventor of nerve gas." 🟢 Evidence check: in 1860 Niemann did describe sulfur mustard and its delayed blistering of skin (he died in 1861), but mustard gas is a blister agent, not a nerve agent (Pharmazeutische Zeitung 2011).
- Cocaine and morphine were "the first big blockbusters," sold openly by Merck, Bayer, Pfizer, Parke-Davis and Squibb: cocaine lozenges from dentists for toothache, pure cocaine inhalants for asthma, remedies for "just about every ailment." ✅
- **Merck's cocaine use:** from under 1 pound to **180,000 pounds** in two years. 🟡 (figure as stated; unverified, we found no source for it. Merck had made cocaine since 1862, and demand surged after Koller showed its use as a local anaesthetic in 1884: Merck corporate history; Grzybowski 2007)
- **Parke-Davis:** tried to patent a process to raise purity and shelf life; sold coca cheroots, coca-leaf cigarettes and a cocaine-alcohol syrup "marketed as true medicine." ✅ (one maker sold cocaine in 15 forms by 1885, including cigarettes and cheroots: Das 1993)
- Opiates stayed in the mix: Merck's morphine powder (advertised as the purest) and opium-laced cough lozenges; Squibb and Pfizer sold nearly a dozen opium tinctures. ✅

## Patent Medicines — The Real Snake Oil
- The big companies' main rival was the **patent medicine** trade: **over 50,000** remedies (his book *Pharma* says "nearly fifty thousand branded remedies, sold without prescription, disclosure, or regulation"). Despite the name they were **not patented**; they were "nostrums." ✅
- **Kopp's Baby Friend:** marketed to new parents to stop an infant's cough so the family could sleep; the company mailed families found through newspaper **birth announcements**. It turned out to be opium and morphine (transcript reads "[one]-third pure opium" 🟡), and children died from overdoses. 🟡 (as told; we could not find a source for this product's formula or the deaths)
- Also named: **Lydia Pinkham** (ingredients unknown, only the claim) and what Posner calls **Dr. Winslow's** opium-based cough syrup. ✅ The product was **Mrs. Winslow's Soothing Syrup**, sold to quiet teething infants; it contained alcohol and morphine and could cause coma, addiction or death in a baby (Christen 2000).
- Secret formulas were standard (he compares Coca-Cola's and Heinz's "57"). Legal fights between makers were over **bottle shapes, labels and brand names**, never ingredients or cures. ✅ Posner, a non-practicing lawyer, finds this telling. 🟡

## Bayer's Lab — Is Heroin a Safe Medicine?
Posner's favorite case study. Bayer's research team, as he dates it:

| Year (as stated) | Drug | What Bayer did |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1887 | Phenacetin ("like Tylenol") | **Held back**: animal tests showed effects at high doses. He equates it with acetaminophen 🟡 |
| ~1.5 years later | **Aspirin** | Sold over the counter; "almost a wonder drug"; stomach bleeding not yet known |
| 1900 | **Heroin** (from German *heroisch*, "heroic") | Sold over the counter; 10–15x stronger than morphine and faster 🟡 |
| 1903 | **Phenobarbital (Luminal)**, first barbiturate | Sold over the counter |

🟡 The dates are Posner's. Evidence check: Hoffmann made pure aspirin on 10 August 1897 and Bayer launched it in 1899 (Sneader 2000); Bayer began commercial heroin production in 1898 (Hosztafi 2001); heroin is about **2 to 3 times** as potent as morphine, not 10 to 15 (EUDA); the first barbiturate was **barbital (Veronal)**, found in 1903 and sold by Bayer and Merck from 1904, while **phenobarbital (Luminal)** was made in 1911 and sold by Bayer from 1912 (López-Muñoz 2005). We could not verify that Bayer held phenacetin back.

- **Heroin marketing (US sale from 1900):** a cough and cold remedy, including for children; "more effective than codeine" with "a tenth" of codeine's side effects; and, his favorite, **a cure for morphine addiction**. "The starkest example to me of marketing triumphant [over] science." ✅
- **Barbiturates:** marketed for insomnia, anxiety, "nerves" and depression; without prescription, a big US seller by 1908. Once Bayer launched, rivals copied it, the same pattern as cocaine and morphine. ✅
- Posner's framing of "secret history": not leaked secrets but facts the industry omits from its own PR story. 🟡

## Regulation Arrives Late — The 1906 Pure Food and Drug Act
- Pattern Posner wants carried through the course: **government reacts late**, after a public-health crisis builds (up through Valium, amphetamines and opioids). 🟡 (his thesis)
- **Harvey Washington Wiley** (Bureau of Chemistry), focused on food preservatives such as formaldehyde, ran the **"Poison Squad"**: volunteers ate meals with undisclosed preservatives to see who got sick. Posner says it stopped after one volunteer died 🟡 (unverified: the trials began in 1902, and the sources we checked report no death; NPR 2018). **The Jungle** (Upton Sinclair's novel, author not named) added public outrage. ✅
- Both patent-medicine makers and the "ethical" firms (Merck, Pfizer) lobbied against strong rules: "We're a new industry... Don't overregulate us." Posner calls this **the industry's first successful lobbying effort**, done without a trade group or Madison Avenue. 🟡
- **What passed:** same addictive products stayed on sale; no prescription requirement; no proof of safety or efficacy; nothing had to be removed. The one change, as Posner tells it: **ingredients had to be listed** (not amounts). 🟡 Evidence check: the Act banned false or misleading labels and required the presence **and amount** of eleven named dangerous ingredients (including alcohol, heroin and cocaine) to be stated; other ingredients did not have to be listed (FDA).
- **Vin Mariani:** Bordeaux wine with **7.6 mg cocaine per ounce**, sold for vigor and alertness, endorsed by **Pope Leo XIII** and "thousands of doctors." Listing cocaine on the label did not hurt sales. ✅ (the 7.6 mg figure is as stated and unverified)
- **First test case:** Wiley sued **Coca-Cola** (coca and caffeine not disclosed). Coca-Cola said the coca leaf had long been removed and it would list caffeine. The government lost after over a year of effort. ✅ (as told) 🟡 Evidence check: the government lost at trial and on appeal, but in 1916 the Supreme Court reversed and sent the case back, holding that caffeine counted as an added ingredient (*U.S. v. Forty Barrels and Twenty Kegs of Coca-Cola*, 241 U.S. 265).
- The "best line they've ever had": **"We're science and cures and medicine, so don't hurt that by overregulating."** Posner calls the weakening of rules **regulatory capture** and part of the DNA. 🟡

## The Harrison Act (1914) and the Cold-Turkey Industry
- The **Harrison Act** controlled opiates and cocaine; Posner says it "essentially bans" them 🟡 (his simplification; 🟢 it was a tax-and-registration law that tightly regulated their distribution and sale rather than an outright ban: Das 1993). It caught the industry off guard because it was focused on the 1906 disclosure fight. ✅
- Effect: the whole industry went "cold turkey." **Patent-medicine makers, mostly one-product firms, were wiped out**; the diversified "ethical" companies (Pfizer, Lilly, Burroughs Wellcome) emerged stronger. ✅
- The search for "less addictive" replacements gave **oxycodone (1916)** and **hydrocodone (1920)**, which Posner calls "me-too versions of addiction" designed to get around the law. 🟡 (interpretation)
- Until World War II and penicillin, the catalogue was thin: laxatives, malt, headache remedies.

## Insulin and the Size of the Industry
- **Insulin (1922, Canada):** two researchers licensed it to **Eli Lilly for $1** because a life-saving discovery "shouldn't make money." 🟡 Evidence check: Banting, Best and Collip assigned the patent to the **University of Toronto** for $1 (1 January 1923); the university then gave Lilly exclusive manufacturing rights for the US and Central and South America (University of Toronto Fisher Library). Posner says Lilly held control for about 40 years via adjustments, dosages and **evergreening**, though a US patent lasts 17 years. 🟡 He also says Lilly "didn't have the patent" but exclusive distribution; the two statements conflict. Today's US insulin price debate (e.g. a $35 cap) concerns a 1922 drug "given away for a dollar." ✅
- **Moody's 1909:** its first ranking of US industries did not list pharmaceuticals; drugs were a subset of chemicals until **1929**. "Just 100 years ago... mom-and-pop" makers with a few addictive products. 🟡 (as stated; we found no source for the Moody's dates)
- Posner: "I'm a capitalist," but pharma sits "at the intersection of public health," which makes maximizing profit uncomfortable. 🟡

## The Business Model He Says Was Locked In by the 1920s
1. Sell potent, often addictive, psychoactive substances as miracle cures.
2. Avoid disclosing ingredients or harms whenever possible.
3. Exploit regulatory gaps: read the new law, then work around it.
4. Advertise heavily (newspapers, billboards; a visiting British writer around 1900 was struck by miles of patent-drug billboards).
5. Treat symptoms and dependence, not causes.
6. When a drug proves harmful, sell a "better" successor rather than an alternative (later: Valium to Xanax).

🟡 This list is Posner's synthesis. He adds that lab scientists are dedicated "nerds of science" (Valium's inventors got a few thousand dollars' bonus); the exaggeration happens in marketing and sales.

## Punchlines & Quotable Facts (Lecture 1)
- **"Before Big Pharma, there was big hype."** The industry's start was marketing, not evidence.
- **87% of US troop deaths in the Mexican-American War were from disease.** The gap that created American morphine makers.
- **Pfizer, Squibb, Wyeth, Parke-Davis, Lilly, Burroughs Wellcome** all began on opium, morphine or war contracts.
- **Heroin was sold as a cure for morphine addiction**, and as a children's cough remedy.
- **Kopp's Baby Friend found customers through birth announcements.**
- **The 1906 Act mainly required honest labels**, with dangerous ingredients such as cocaine declared; Vin Mariani's cocaine label did not dent sales.
- **"Don't overregulate, you'll stop innovation"**: the industry's best line, per Posner.
- **Insulin's inventors sold the patent to the University of Toronto for $1** (1923).
- **Moody's did not list pharma as an industry until 1929.**
- **Only the US and New Zealand allow direct-to-consumer drug ads** (US since 1997).

## Key Corrections to Carry Forward
- 🔴 "The drug industry began as science": per Posner it began on morphine, cocaine, heroin and barbiturates sold without prescription.
- 🔴 "Patent medicines were patented": they were not; they were secret-formula nostrums.
- 🔴 "The 1906 Pure Food and Drug Act made drugs safe": it banned misleading labels and required dangerous ingredients to be declared, with no safety, efficacy or prescription rules.
- 🔴 "Heroin was always a street drug": Bayer sold it over the counter, including for children's coughs.
- 🔴 "Disclosure alone changes behavior": Vin Mariani listed cocaine and sales held.
- 🔴 "A new formulation means a safer drug": morphine to heroin to oxycodone shows replacement often repackages the same problem.

## Practical Takeaway
Posner's "smarter patient" checklist, framed as study points, not medical advice: (1) question the marketing story and the motive behind promotion; (2) read labels, ingredients and warnings, knowing inserts were long sent to pharmacists rather than patients, then printed as four-foot fold-outs in tiny type; (3) ask what evidence supports a claimed benefit, especially for a brand-new drug; (4) ask whether a drug treats the cause or manages symptoms (symptom relief can be fine, but know which it is); (5) be wary of quick emotional fixes, since mental-health diagnosis is observational and he believes drugs such as Ritalin are overprescribed 🟡; (6) "don't become cynical": penicillin and other drugs genuinely changed history.

*Why A: he calls heroin-for-morphine-addiction "the starkest example" of marketing winning over science; the one drug Bayer held back was phenacetin, not heroin.*

*Why C: Posner calls the patent makers "one-hit wonders" wiped out by the cold-turkey shift; the diversified firms emerged stronger.*

**4. Given the insulin story, what point is Posner making about the industry?**
A. That insulin was invented by Eli Lilly's own scientists
