
# Posner — The Pharmaceutical Industry, Ch.1: Snake Oil Origins
*How the drug industry was born selling morphine, cocaine, heroin and barbiturates as miracle cures in an unregulated market, and why Posner says that origin is still its DNA.*

*Source: Gerald Posner, Peterson Academy course "The Pharmaceutical Industry" (introduced on screen as "Secrets of Big Pharma: How to Be a Smarter Patient"), Lecture 1 (transcript). First of eight lectures; covers roughly 1800 to the 1920s. Transcript complete: it closes with a preview of Lecture 2 and "Thank you very much."*

**In plain words:** the drug industry did not start as science. In the 1800s, companies that are still famous today (Pfizer, Squibb, Lilly, Bayer, Merck) made their first money selling morphine, cocaine, heroin and sleeping drugs, with no prescriptions, no safety tests and no rules about what went in the bottle. Laws came late and were weak, and the companies fought them. Posner calls this start the industry's "DNA": a habit of selling hope first and checking the science later. His historical facts are marked ✅, his opinions 🟡, and a 2026 source check corrects several of his dates and figures.

**Words in this note:**
- **DNA**: the genetic code inside each cell
- **GLP-1**: a gut hormone that lowers appetite and blood sugar; also a class of drugs (such as semaglutide)
- **FDA**: U.S. Food and Drug Administration
- **TRT**: testosterone replacement therapy
- **DHEA**: a hormone made by the adrenal glands that the body turns into sex hormones
- **ApoB**: apolipoprotein B; counts all the cholesterol particles that can clog arteries

## Why This Matters
This is the origin story the rest of the course builds on. Posner's main argument is that the way the industry started is its "DNA" and a "template" (a pattern) that keeps repeating. That start was: addictive products, no prescriptions, no proof that drugs were safe or worked ("efficacy"), marketing ahead of science, and early lobbying against rules. Posner is an investigative journalist and lawyer (author of *Pharma*), not a scientist or doctor. So his historical facts are marked ✅ when clearly stated, and his interpretations 🟡. Several dates and credits in the lecture are loose. A source check (October 2026) adds evidence checks in the text wherever the record differs, with references at the end.

## The Thesis — "Before Big Pharma, There Was Big Hype"
- The industry started in a **"marketplace of ignorance"**: no national standards, no prescription laws, no licences for doctors, no safety rules, and no duty to tell buyers the ingredients or the harms. "Anyone could call themselves a doctor." ✅
- The winners sold **"dreams," not evidence**: big promises, with no medical studies required. 🟡 (his framing)
- He says you will see the same cycle all course long. A "miracle pill" is pushed hard to doctors, through ads and studies the company pays for. The public takes it up eagerly. Rival companies chase "me-too" copies (near-copies of a drug, made to get their own patent). Extra benefits get floated. Only later do reports of side effects arrive ("I wish we had known 5 or 10 years ago"). His current example: **GLP-1 weight-loss drugs** (talk of benefits for dementia, the heart and "vigor"). 🟡 (his pattern claim)
- "It's not a coincidence the industry grew up on addictive products." An addictive product makes a customer who keeps coming back, which drug cartels also understand. 🟡

## Why Quack Cures Sold — Medical Ignorance
Posner's background argument: when people do not know what makes them sick, fear plus a confident promise of a cure is easy to sell. ✅ (as argument)
- Germ theory (the idea that tiny living things cause disease) was new, and many people dismissed it. He credits **Louis Pasteur** (spoiled meat and milk) and a **German bacteriologist (1882)**. Posner does not name him, but this is **Robert Koch**, who showed that tuberculosis comes from germs, at a time when many thought it was inherited. 🟡 His statement that Pasteur's work came "a few decades" before the mid-1800s drugs gets the timeline loose.
- Disease was blamed on people, not germs. He says an 1882 Immigration Act was driven by fears of cholera (blamed on Irish immigrants) 🟡 (unverified: we found no source tying the 1882 Act to cholera fears). He quotes the **1891 Immigration Act**, which kept out "all idiots, insane persons, paupers... and persons suffering from a loathsome or dangerous contagious disease." ✅ (quote as read)
- **Walter Reed (1900)** showed that mosquitoes spread yellow fever. Posner calls him an "army general." ✅ Evidence check: Reed was an Army surgeon (rank of major) who led the Army's Yellow Fever Board in Cuba (Pierce 2003).

## Morphine and the War Origins of the Companies
- **Morphine (1804):** a 21-year-old German pharmacy apprentice pulled the first pure alkaloid (a strong plant chemical) out of the opium poppy. He named it after **Morpheus**, the Greek god of dreams. Its power to relieve pain was quickly recognized. ✅
- Early good-quality morphine came from two German firms, **Bayer** and **Schering** (later "Schering & Plough"). 🟡 He places this in "the early 1800s". ✅ Evidence check: the first large-scale commercial maker of morphine was **E. Merck** of Darmstadt, from 1827 (Merck corporate history); Bayer and Schering were founded decades later.
- **Mexican-American War (1846–48):** **87% of US troop fatalities** came from untreated disease (yellow fever, dysentery, cholera), not from fighting. ✅ Evidence check: 10,986 of 12,535 US war deaths (88%) were from infectious disease, mostly dysentery, a severe bowel infection (Cirillo 2009). The morphine that reached the front was often watered down by middlemen, so soldiers got little relief. ✅
- That failure created the American market. Posner's list of the founders:

| Company | Origin as Posner tells it |
| --- | --- |
| **Charles Pfizer & Co.** | German cousins in New York, one year after the war; small savings plus a loan (figures garbled in the transcript 🟡); a factory on Bartlett Street; the first serious US maker of high-quality morphine. 🟡 Evidence check: founded 1849 in Williamsburg, Brooklyn, by Charles Pfizer and his cousin Charles Erhart with a $2,500 loan from Pfizer's father; its first product was santonin, a worm remedy, not morphine (Pfizer 2024) |
| **E. R. Squibb & Sons** | Edward Robinson Squibb saw bad morphine in the war (often thrown overboard) and set up in Brooklyn. Posner calls him a "colonel" 🟡; he was a US Navy medical officer, and set up his Brooklyn lab in 1858 (Britannica) |
| **Wyeth** | John and Frank Wyeth, Philadelphia pharmacists, one year into the Civil War; a Union Army contract paid so well they left the pharmacy for manufacturing |
| **Parke-Davis** | Samuel Duffield (Detroit) sold a pain solution of ether and alcohol under a Union contract; bought by Hervey Parke (a mining investor) and George Davis (a 26-year-old salesman) |
| **Eli Lilly** | A Civil War veteran who founded the company just after; its main products were made from opium and morphine |
| **Burroughs Wellcome** | Silas Burroughs and Henry Wellcome, American pharmacists who went to the UK; laxatives and creams made with opium |

- The **Civil War (1861)** was "good for the morphine business" because of record numbers of battlefield injuries. ✅ Posner's point: these were not short-lived firms but the ancestors of today's giants. They were launched on "an addictive product that has no restrictions on how it's dispensed." 🟡 (interpretation)

## Cocaine — The Second Blockbuster
- **1859:** a German doctoral student (**Albert Niemann**, Göttingen; thesis finished 1860) pulled the alkaloid out of the coca plant and named it **cocaine** (coca + the Latin-derived ending *-ina*, "from"). ✅
- Posner adds that the same chemist later developed mustard gas, and calls him "the inventor of nerve gas." 🟢 Evidence check: in 1860 Niemann did describe sulfur mustard and how it blisters skin after a delay (he died in 1861), but mustard gas is a blister agent, not a nerve agent (Pharmazeutische Zeitung 2011).
- Cocaine and morphine were "the first big blockbusters" (best-selling drugs), sold openly by Merck, Bayer, Pfizer, Parke-Davis and Squibb. There were cocaine lozenges from dentists for toothache, pure cocaine to breathe in for asthma, and remedies for "just about every ailment." ✅
- **Merck's use of cocaine:** from under 1 pound to **180,000 pounds** in two years. 🟡 (figure as stated; unverified, we found no source for it. Merck had made cocaine since 1862, and demand surged after Koller showed its use as a local anaesthetic in 1884: Merck corporate history; Grzybowski 2007)
- **Parke-Davis:** tried to patent a process to make it purer and keep longer. It sold coca cheroots (small cigars), coca-leaf cigarettes and a cocaine-and-alcohol syrup "marketed as true medicine." ✅ (one maker sold cocaine in 15 forms by 1885, including cigarettes and cheroots: Das 1993)
- Opium drugs stayed in the mix: Merck's morphine powder (advertised as the purest) and cough lozenges laced with opium. Squibb and Pfizer sold nearly a dozen opium tinctures (drugs dissolved in alcohol). ✅

## Patent Medicines — The Real Snake Oil
- The big companies' main rival was the **patent medicine** trade: **over 50,000** remedies. (His book *Pharma* says "nearly fifty thousand branded remedies, sold without prescription, disclosure, or regulation.") Despite the name, they were **not patented**. They were "nostrums," secret home-made-style remedies. ✅
- **Kopp's Baby Friend:** sold to new parents to stop a baby's cough so the family could sleep. The company mailed families it found through **birth announcements** in newspapers. It turned out to be opium and morphine (the transcript reads "[one]-third pure opium" 🟡), and children died from overdoses. 🟡 (as told; we could not find a source for this product's formula or the deaths)
- Also named: **Lydia Pinkham** (ingredients unknown, only the promise) and what Posner calls **Dr. Winslow's** opium cough syrup. ✅ The product was really **Mrs. Winslow's Soothing Syrup**, sold to quiet teething babies. It contained alcohol and morphine and could cause coma, addiction or death in a baby (Christen 2000).
- Secret formulas were normal (he compares Coca-Cola's recipe and Heinz's "57"). Court fights between makers were over **bottle shapes, labels and brand names**, never over ingredients or cures. ✅ Posner, a lawyer who no longer practices, finds this telling. 🟡

## Bayer's Lab — Is Heroin a Safe Medicine?
Posner's favorite example. Bayer's research team, with his dates:

| Year (as stated) | Drug | What Bayer did |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1887 | Phenacetin ("like Tylenol") | **Held back**: animal tests showed effects at high doses. He treats it as the same as acetaminophen 🟡 |
| ~1.5 years later | **Aspirin** | Sold over the counter (no prescription); "almost a wonder drug"; stomach bleeding not yet known |
| 1900 | **Heroin** (from German *heroisch*, "heroic") | Sold over the counter; 10–15x stronger than morphine and faster 🟡 |
| 1903 | **Phenobarbital (Luminal)**, the first barbiturate (a sleeping and calming drug) | Sold over the counter |

🟡 The dates are Posner's. Evidence check: Hoffmann made pure aspirin on 10 August 1897 and Bayer launched it in 1899 (Sneader 2000). Bayer began making heroin for sale in 1898 (Hosztafi 2001). Heroin is about **2 to 3 times** as strong as morphine, not 10 to 15 (EUDA). The first barbiturate was **barbital (Veronal)**, found in 1903 and sold by Bayer and Merck from 1904; **phenobarbital (Luminal)** was made in 1911 and sold by Bayer from 1912 (López-Muñoz 2005). We could not confirm that Bayer held phenacetin back.

- **How heroin was sold (US sale from 1900):** as a cough and cold remedy, including for children; as "more effective than codeine" with "a tenth" of codeine's side effects; and, his favorite, as **a cure for morphine addiction**. "The starkest example to me of marketing triumphant [over] science." ✅
- **Barbiturates:** sold for sleeplessness, anxiety, "nerves" and depression; with no prescription needed, a big US seller by 1908. Once Bayer launched them, rivals copied, the same pattern as with cocaine and morphine. ✅
- What Posner means by "secret history": not leaked secrets, but facts the industry leaves out of its own public story. 🟡

## Regulation Arrives Late — The 1906 Pure Food and Drug Act
- A pattern Posner wants you to follow through the course: **government reacts late**, only after a public-health crisis has built up (all the way to Valium, amphetamines and opioids). 🟡 (his thesis)
- **Harvey Washington Wiley** (Bureau of Chemistry) was worried about food preservatives such as formaldehyde. He ran the **"Poison Squad"**: volunteers ate meals with hidden preservatives to see who got sick. Posner says it stopped after one volunteer died 🟡 (unverified: the trials began in 1902, and the sources we checked report no death; NPR 2018). **The Jungle** (Upton Sinclair's novel about the meat trade; Posner does not name the author) added public anger. ✅
- Both the patent-medicine makers and the "ethical" firms (the established drug makers, such as Merck and Pfizer) lobbied against strong rules: "We're a new industry... Don't overregulate us." Posner calls this **the industry's first successful lobbying effort**, done without a trade group or big advertising agencies ("Madison Avenue"). 🟡
- **What passed:** the same addictive products stayed on sale; no prescription was needed; no proof of safety or efficacy was required; nothing had to be taken off the market. As Posner tells it, the one change was that **ingredients had to be listed** (not amounts). 🟡 Evidence check: the Act banned false or misleading labels. It required that eleven named dangerous ingredients (including alcohol, heroin and cocaine) be stated on the label, **with their amount**; other ingredients did not have to be listed (FDA).
- **Vin Mariani:** a Bordeaux wine with **7.6 mg cocaine per ounce**, sold for energy and alertness and endorsed by **Pope Leo XIII** and "thousands of doctors." Listing cocaine on the label did not hurt sales. ✅ (the 7.6 mg figure is as stated and unverified)
- **First test case:** Wiley sued **Coca-Cola** for not declaring coca and caffeine. Coca-Cola said the coca leaf had long been removed and that it would list caffeine. The government lost after more than a year of effort. ✅ (as told) 🟡 Evidence check: the government lost at trial and on appeal, but in 1916 the Supreme Court overturned that and sent the case back, ruling that caffeine counted as an added ingredient (*U.S. v. Forty Barrels and Twenty Kegs of Coca-Cola*, 241 U.S. 265).
- The "best line they've ever had": **"We're science and cures and medicine, so don't hurt that by overregulating."** Posner calls this weakening of rules **regulatory capture** (when an industry ends up steering the agency meant to watch it), and part of the DNA. 🟡

## The Harrison Act (1914) and the Cold-Turkey Industry
- The **Harrison Act** controlled opium drugs and cocaine. Posner says it "essentially bans" them 🟡 (his simplification; 🟢 it was a tax-and-registration law that tightly controlled how they were distributed and sold, rather than an outright ban: Das 1993). It caught the industry off guard, because the industry had been busy with the 1906 labelling fight. ✅
- The result: the whole industry went "cold turkey" (stopped suddenly). **Patent-medicine makers, mostly firms with a single product, were wiped out.** The broader "ethical" companies (Pfizer, Lilly, Burroughs Wellcome) came out stronger. ✅
- The search for "less addictive" replacements gave us **oxycodone (1916)** and **hydrocodone (1920)**. Posner calls these "me-too versions of addiction," designed to get around the law. 🟡 (interpretation)
- Until World War II and penicillin, the product list was thin: laxatives, malt, headache remedies.

## Insulin and the Size of the Industry
- **Insulin (1922, Canada):** two researchers licensed it to **Eli Lilly for $1**, because a life-saving discovery "shouldn't make money." 🟡 Evidence check: Banting, Best and Collip handed the patent to the **University of Toronto** for $1 (1 January 1923). The university then gave Lilly the sole right to manufacture it for the US and Central and South America (University of Toronto Fisher Library). Posner says Lilly kept control for about 40 years through adjustments, new doses and **evergreening** (small changes that extend a patent), even though a US patent lasts 17 years. 🟡 He also says Lilly "didn't have the patent," only the sole right to distribute it; the two statements do not fit together. Today's US debate over insulin prices (for example a $35 cap) is about a 1922 drug that was "given away for a dollar." ✅
- **Moody's 1909:** its first ranking of US industries did not list drugs at all; drugs were counted as part of chemicals until **1929**. "Just 100 years ago..." the makers were small family businesses ("mom-and-pop") with a few addictive products. 🟡 (as stated; we found no source for the Moody's dates)
- Posner: "I'm a capitalist," but drug-making sits "at the intersection of public health," which makes chasing maximum profit uncomfortable. 🟡

## The Business Model He Says Was Locked In by the 1920s
1. Sell strong, often addictive, mind-altering substances as miracle cures.
2. Avoid telling buyers the ingredients or the harms whenever possible.
3. Use gaps in the rules: read the new law, then work around it.
4. Advertise heavily (newspapers, billboards; a British writer visiting around 1900 was struck by miles of billboards for patent drugs).
5. Treat symptoms and dependence, not causes.
6. When a drug proves harmful, sell a "better" next version rather than a different approach (later: Valium to Xanax).

🟡 This list is Posner's own summary. He adds that lab scientists are dedicated "nerds of science" (Valium's inventors got a bonus of a few thousand dollars); the exaggeration happens in marketing and sales.

## Punchlines & Quotable Facts (Lecture 1)
- **"Before Big Pharma, there was big hype."** The industry started with marketing, not evidence.
- **87% of US troop deaths in the Mexican-American War were from disease.** That gap created the American morphine makers.
- **Pfizer, Squibb, Wyeth, Parke-Davis, Lilly and Burroughs Wellcome** all began on opium, morphine or war contracts.
- **Heroin was sold as a cure for morphine addiction**, and as a cough remedy for children.
- **Kopp's Baby Friend found its customers through birth announcements.**
- **The 1906 Act mainly required honest labels**, with dangerous ingredients such as cocaine declared; Vin Mariani's cocaine label did not dent its sales.
- **"Don't overregulate, you'll stop innovation"**: the industry's best line, according to Posner.
- **Insulin's inventors sold the patent to the University of Toronto for $1** (1923).
- **Moody's did not list drugs as an industry until 1929.**
- **Only the US and New Zealand allow drug ads aimed straight at the public** (US since 1997).

## Key Corrections to Carry Forward
- 🔴 "The drug industry began as science": according to Posner, it began on morphine, cocaine, heroin and barbiturates sold without prescription.
- 🔴 "Patent medicines were patented": they were not; they were secret-formula remedies.
- 🔴 "The 1906 Pure Food and Drug Act made drugs safe": it banned misleading labels and required dangerous ingredients to be declared, with no rules on safety, efficacy or prescriptions.
- 🔴 "Heroin was always a street drug": Bayer sold it over the counter, including for children's coughs.
- 🔴 "Just telling people changes what they do": Vin Mariani listed cocaine and its sales held.
- 🔴 "A new version means a safer drug": morphine to heroin to oxycodone shows that a replacement often repackages the same problem.

## Practical Takeaway
Posner's "smarter patient" checklist, as study points, not medical advice: (1) question the marketing story and the motive behind the promotion; (2) read labels, ingredients and warnings, knowing that package inserts were long sent to pharmacists rather than patients, and were later printed as four-foot fold-outs in tiny type; (3) ask what evidence supports a claimed benefit, especially for a brand-new drug; (4) ask whether a drug treats the cause or only the symptoms (relieving symptoms can be fine, but know which it is); (5) be wary of quick emotional fixes, since mental-health diagnosis is based on observation, and he believes drugs such as Ritalin are prescribed too often 🟡; (6) "don't become cynical": penicillin and other drugs truly changed history.
