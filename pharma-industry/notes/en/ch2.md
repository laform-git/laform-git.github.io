
# Posner — The Pharmaceutical Industry, Ch.2: Post-War Drug Boom
*How World War II's government-funded Penicillin Project turned small drug makers into global empires, and how antibiotic profits, me-too patents, combination drugs, marketing to doctors and the turn to chronic-disease drugs built the modern industry.*

*Source: Gerald Posner, Peterson Academy course "The Pharmaceutical Industry," Lecture 2 (transcript). Second of eight lectures; follows Ch.1 Snake Oil Origins and sets up Ch.3 Selling Sickness. Transcript complete: it ends on a preview of the next lecture (key opinion leaders), consistent with Posner's continuous-talk style.*

## Why This Matters
This is the lecture where real science arrives, and Posner's frame is "science meets profit." The Penicillin Project shows what the industry can do when government, universities and companies work together without patents; everything after it shows what the same companies did once they could patent. Nearly every structural feature of today's industry is born here: patent games (me-too drugs), marketing aimed at doctors, the off-label loophole, revolving doors with regulators, and the pivot from cures to lifelong prescriptions. Posner is an investigative journalist (author of *Pharma*), not a scientist: his historical facts are marked ✅ when clearly stated, his opinions and interpretations 🟡.

## The Penicillin Hunters — From Mold Juice to Oxford
- **1928, London** (St Mary's Hospital): a Scottish bacteriologist (named later in the lecture as Alexander Fleming), a former WWI military-hospital surgeon who had seen men die of infected cuts, leaves staph-covered petri dishes during a warm spell. On return, a greenish mold had grown and the staph around it had largely disappeared. ✅
- He traces it to the *Penicillium* family but **cannot isolate the pure active compound** (the same extraction step that gave morphine and cocaine in Lecture 1). His 1929 paper calls it "mold juice," names it **penicillin**, downplays its potential, and is largely ignored. ✅
- **1936, Oxford:** Howard Florey (Rhodes scholar), Norman Heatley and Ernst Chain (29, from Berlin, his Jewish family having fled the Nazis in 1933) take it up and succeed in isolating it. Human studies begin only in **1940**. ✅ 🟡 Evidence check: Florey assembled the team in **1939**; mouse tests ran in May 1939, the results appeared in *The Lancet* in August 1940, and the first patient was treated in **February 1941** (Gaynes 2017).
- Britain is at war and the government has no money to spare; Oxford tests first on **terminal cancer patients** (about 300 mg IV) because there were no clinical-trial rules yet. The cancer still killed them, but **no side effects** appeared. ✅
- **The policeman case:** a man in his 30s, cut on the face by a rose thorn, had lost an eye and had abscesses on his arm and chest. Within a day of penicillin his fever broke; in three days he was up; by five days he seemed nearly healed. Then the supply ran out, the infection returned, and he died. The proof was complete: the drug worked, there just was not enough of it. ✅ (He was an Oxford policeman treated in February 1941 who died a few weeks later when the supply ran out: Gaynes 2017.)

## Government as Drug Incubator — The Penicillin Project
- Two of the Oxford researchers appeal to the US before America enters the war. Roosevelt launches a secret program that Posner calls the **second largest secret program of WWII after the Manhattan Project**. ✅ (his ranking 🟡)
- A lab in **Peoria, Illinois** hosts the British researchers; **twelve companies volunteer**, including Pfizer, Merck and Squibb, alongside academics (Posner names a "Rockefeller Group"). ✅
- Samples of mold were gathered from US diplomatic missions worldwide; the most potent strain came from a **moldy cantaloupe at a farmers' market a couple of miles from the lab**. ✅
- **Pfizer's deep-tank fermentation breakthrough** made mass production possible. The transcript links it to citric acid ("this citric acid, leveraged") without explaining how. ✅ Evidence check: the link is real. Pfizer had made citric acid by deep-tank mold fermentation since 1919 and applied that know-how to penicillin; its converted Brooklyn plant opened on 1 March 1944 (ACS landmark, 2008).
- The **government built the fermentation tanks and plants at taxpayer expense**, for Pfizer and the others. Output went from small (1941 to 1942) to enormous (1943) to hundreds of millions of units by 1945. ✅ (Pfizer's Brooklyn plant alone made more in one month of 1944 than in all of 1943: ACS 2008. The "hundreds of millions of units" figure is as stated; we could not check it.) 🟡 That the government paid for every company's plant is as stated and unverified; historians do describe the program as rooted in government stewardship, industry cooperation and open sharing of data (Quinn 2013).
- The decisive condition: **"Nobody gets to patent penicillin."** With a dozen makers of the same product, the price fell and kept falling. Companies sold huge volumes but made little money, and hated it. ✅ 🟢 Evidence check: the drug itself was not patented, but **process patents** were filed, by Merck and by USDA scientist Andrew Moyer (Gaynes 2017; see Ch.3).

| Partner | Contribution in the Penicillin Project |
| --- | --- |
| Government (US) | All the money, secrecy, the Peoria lab, the fermentation plants; the no-patent rule |
| Academia (Oxford, US labs) | Isolation of the drug, early human testing, strain research ("unsung heroes") |
| Industry (12 companies) | Fermentation know-how (Pfizer), mass manufacturing, distribution |

🟡 Posner's COVID aside: he expected governments to repeat this template for vaccines, but "they forgot about the 'nobody owns a patent' part." His view, previewed for Ch.8.

## The Postwar Surge — How America Took the World Market
- New antibiotics: **streptomycin** (1943, first effective TB treatment), **Aureomycin** (1948, first broad-spectrum antibiotic), **tetracycline** (1955, "very versatile"). Broad-spectrum drugs suited doctors who did not know what bug a patient had. ✅
- **1945 to 1955: pharmaceutical revenues up 600%**, fueled by antibiotics. ✅ Later he restates this as *Pfizer's* revenues rising 600%, with **80% from tetracycline alone** 🟡 (the two framings conflict; he also says "profits" in the same breath).
- **Germany's collapse was America's opening.** Before the war, German firms such as Schering and Bayer filled about **one in two prescriptions worldwide**. Allied bombing destroyed their plants. ✅ (His list of Bayer discoveries includes acetaminophen alongside aspirin, heroin and barbiturates 🟡, worth checking.)
- **By 1950: 80% of world drug sales came from US companies**, mostly the top 10, and **95% of the profits**. 🟡 (as stated; we found no source for these shares)
- **2005:** all top-10 drug companies traced their origins to the penicillin program. 🟡 (as stated and unverified; several 2005 leaders were European firms, so "all" looks overstated)
- Profits were reinvested aggressively: foreign subsidiaries (UK, France, South America), new labs, buying out small competitors before they could grow, and **vertical integration** (discover, patent, manufacture, distribute, cutting out middlemen). ✅

## Patent Games — Me-Too Drugs and Combination Antibiotics
- Penicillin could not be patented, but **every new antibiotic variant could**: streptomycin, Terramycin, Aureomycin and the rest. ✅
- **Me-too drugs:** change a molecule or two of a competitor's drug, patent it, market it as better. No proof of efficacy was required. ✅
- **The 1938 Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act** only required companies to disclose if a drug was really dangerous (could kill); no full side-effect list, **no efficacy requirement**. ✅ 🟡 Evidence check: the Act went further on safety than this: makers had to show FDA that a new drug was safe before sale, and labels needed adequate directions for safe use; efficacy was not required until 1962 (FDA Part II).
- Pfizer and others sued for patent infringement; courts that "didn't understand the science" ruled that **a changed chemical structure is patentable**. Result: a "Wild West" of dozens of variants each, all promising broader spectrum, fewer side effects, better cures. ✅
- **Fixed-dose combinations:** Sigmamycin, marketed by Arthur Sackler, was the first; companies eventually sold **hundreds** of two-antibiotic combinations on the logic "two are better than one." Years later (Congress fights, next lectures) they were found almost never better than a single antibiotic, with **multiplied side-effect risk**. ✅

## The Dark Side — Overuse and Resistance
- 1950s doctors dispensed antibiotics "like candy" for sniffles, sore throats and colds. Overprescribing was part of the profit, so companies had no incentive to curb it. ✅
- **Late-1940s Japan:** a study saw resistant bacteria in long-term penicillin users; Western doctors dismissed it as "something peculiar to Japan." 🟡 (as stated; we could not identify the study)
- **1945:** Fleming himself, visiting America, warned companies that microbes would evolve resistance. ✅ He said so publicly in his Nobel lecture of 11 December 1945: an underdosed patient could "educate" microbes "to resist penicillin" (Fleming 1945). Posner's paraphrase of the reply: "Thanks for discovering it. See you later." ✅ (the reply is his dramatization 🟡)
- Some legislatures discussed cutting back; nothing was done. Superbugs are not yet a crisis in this era, "but it will" be. ✅

## From Cures to Chronic — Miltown and the Business-Model Pivot
- **Lobotomy → Thorazine → Miltown:** the lobotomy's inventor won a Nobel Prize; **Thorazine** (French origin) was sold as "lobotomy light" but left people like zombies; **Miltown**, a first-generation anxiolytic and predecessor of Librium and Valium, became a 1950s sensation (Hollywood pharmacy lines, TV talk). ✅ Posner says Miltown "replaced" Thorazine and frames them under depression 🟡 (sequence and categories loosely stated). Evidence check: chlorpromazine (Thorazine) was made at Rhône-Poulenc in December 1950, used in psychiatry from 1952 and in the US from 1954, as an **antipsychotic**, not an antidepressant (López-Muñoz 2005); Miltown, a tranquilizer, was launched by Carter Products in 1955 (Herzberg 2011). They treated different conditions, so one did not replace the other. The lobotomy's inventor, Egas Moniz, won the 1949 Nobel Prize. ✅
- The marketing insight: antibiotics are taken for 5 to 7 days and sales depend on how sick people are; **Miltown is taken every day**. Drugs for lifelong conditions mean steady refills. ✅
- Target "dream" areas from the late '50s and early '60s: **blood pressure, diabetes, depression**. ✅
- 🟡 Posner's thesis: the profit motive creates an incentive to treat symptoms and maintain chronic conditions rather than find causes and cures. Industry would say treating is its job; he is "not necessarily sure that's true."

| Model | Use pattern | Revenue profile (as Posner frames it) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Antibiotic (cure) | Days, when sick | Volatile, tied to epidemics and flu seasons |
| Chronic drug (Miltown, BP, diabetes) | Daily, for years | Reliable refills, "the long game" |

## Where the Money Went — The Late-1950s Spending Snapshot
Posner calls these "rough numbers" averaged from companies that reported them. 🟡

| Category | Share of spending |
| --- | --- |
| Academic research grants | About 15% |
| In-house R&D | About 30% |
| Regulatory compliance | About 5% |
| Marketing and advertising | About 50% |

- He says the marketing share "doesn't change much over time," and that today the top 10 companies still spend more on marketing and promotion than on R&D; **adding share buybacks**, the total far exceeds R&D. 🟡 (his argument against the "we need profits for R&D" defence; he concedes development is now expensive, about a billion dollars per drug, with failures; 🟢 a 2020 analysis put the median at $985 million including failures: Wouters 2020). 🟡 Evidence check: US medical marketing totalled $29.9 billion in 2016, most of it aimed at doctors (Schwartz 2019); whether marketing exceeds R&D depends on how each is counted, so this is contested.
- **Doctors as the gatekeeper market:** marketing went to doctors, not patients (pamphlets, hardcover books for Valium, medical-journal ads, funded studies likely to be favorable). Doctors usually do not know or weigh what a drug costs the patient, so high prices do not deter prescribing. ✅
- **Academic symbiosis:** Merck and others allied with Harvard, Yale, Johns Hopkins; researchers liked their names on discoveries. ✅

## Political Capture — The Seeds
- **The 1938 off-label provision:** companies won the right for doctors to prescribe a drug for anything they see fit. With a few dozen drug classes then, that seemed manageable; today, with thousands, Posner calls it **"one of the greatest loopholes,"** worth billions in sales for unapproved uses. ✅ (characterization 🟡) 🟡 Evidence check: off-label prescribing is not a provision of the 1938 Act; it exists because the FDA regulates drugs, not the practice of medicine, while companies may not promote unapproved uses (Wittich 2012).
- **Revolving door:** the FDA's chief of antibiotics approval was invited by Arthur Sackler and others to join their antibiotics magazine, and nobody saw a conflict. Later, the FDA official who approved OxyContin's label went to work for Purdue Pharma. ✅
- **The Sacklers in the 1950s:** three psychiatrist brothers; Posner says two lost security clearances at a state mental institution over Communist Party membership 🟡 (his claim; unverified here); Arthur builds foreign subsidiaries before having a product. ✅
- Posner: pharma "buys compliance on both sides of the aisle." 🟡 Opinion.
- Industry context: per Moody's, the industry did not exist as a category in 1909; by 1929 it was listed with essentially one product, insulin 🟡 (as stated; unverified).

## Punchlines & Quotable Facts (Lecture 2)
- **"Nobody gets to patent penicillin."** The no-patent rule drove prices toward zero.
- **A moldy cantaloupe from a Peoria farmers' market** gave the most potent strain.
- **The policeman lived five good days, then died when the penicillin ran out.** Proof by scarcity.
- **1950: 80% of world drug sales and 95% of profits were American.** Germany's bombed plants made it possible.
- **All top-10 drug companies in 2005 traced back to the penicillin program.**
- **1945 to 1955: revenues up 600% on antibiotics alone.**
- **Change a molecule, get a patent.** The courts' ruling that birthed me-too drugs.
- **Marketing about 50%, R&D about 30%.** Late-1950s rough split.
- **Antibiotics last a week; Miltown is every day.** The pivot to chronic drugs.
- **Off-label prescribing: a loophole still worth billions** (Posner dates it to 1938; it rests on the FDA not regulating medical practice).

## Key Corrections to Carry Forward
- 🔴 "Big Pharma built itself": the US government paid for the Penicillin Project, its research and its fermentation plants; the industry's head start was taxpayer-funded.
- 🔴 "A new patented version means a better drug": me-too variants and fixed-dose combinations were patented and sold without efficacy proof, and combinations were almost never better.
- 🔴 "Most drug spending is R&D": in Posner's late-1950s snapshot marketing was about half, R&D about a third; he argues marketing plus buybacks still exceed R&D.
- 🔴 "FDA approval covers every use": off-label prescribing lets doctors use drugs for uses never clinically approved, although companies may not promote them.
- 🔴 "Resistance was a surprise": Fleming warned in 1945 and a late-1940s Japanese study saw it; both were dismissed.
- 🔴 "Penicillin was a lone eureka": it took 1928 chance, a 1929 paper, a 1939 to 1941 Oxford team, and a wartime government program to become a drug.

## Practical Takeaway
Study-level takeaways, not medical directives. Posner's "smarter patient" questions:
1. **Who paid for the original research?** (He now says this matters less than he used to think.)
2. **Why is this drug being prescribed to me?** Is it a trend drug (Miltown, Valium, GLP-1s) or chosen for my case? "There's no free lunch in pharmaceuticals."
3. **Is this treating an infection or a convenience?** Ask before taking antibiotics for a cold.
4. **Are there non-drug options that work as well?** Lifestyle is "less profitable." He grants GLP-1 drugs can be life-changing for the morbidly obese, but questions using them to lose 20 pounds for a reunion.

## Questions to Learn From (Socratic) — with Answer Options
**1. Given how the Penicillin Project was run, why did the price of penicillin keep falling after the war?**
A. Pfizer cut prices voluntarily to win goodwill
B. \* The government barred anyone from patenting it, so a dozen manufacturers competed on the same product
C. Penicillin turned out to be less effective than sulfa drugs
*Why B: Posner presents it as a textbook free market: no patent, many makers, high demand, falling price, and companies hated the thin margins.*

**2. Given the 1950s legal environment Posner describes, what made "me-too" antibiotics both possible and profitable?**
A. \* Courts held that a changed chemical structure is patentable, and the 1938 Act required no proof of efficacy, so slight variants could be patented and marketed as better
B. The FDA required each variant to beat existing drugs in head-to-head trials
C. Penicillin's patent expired, opening the field
*Why A: the combination of easy patents and no efficacy requirement let companies sell variants on promises alone.*

**3. Given Posner's account of Miltown, what was the key business lesson the industry drew?**
A. Psychiatric drugs are too risky to market widely
B. Antibiotics are more profitable because people get sick often
C. \* Drugs taken daily for lifelong conditions produce steadier, larger revenue than short-course cures, so the industry shifted toward chronic treatments
*Why C: antibiotics last days and depend on illness waves; Miltown users took it every day, pointing companies toward blood pressure, diabetes and depression.*

**4. Given the late-1950s spending snapshot, which statement best reflects Posner's argument?**
A. R&D was the largest category, so high prices are justified by research
B. \* Marketing was about half of spending and R&D about a third, so the "we need profits for R&D" defence overstates research's share
C. Regulatory compliance consumed most of the budget
*Why B: his rough numbers put marketing near 50% and R&D near 30%, and he says marketing plus buybacks still exceed R&D today.*
