
# Posner — The Pharmaceutical Industry, Ch.2: Post-War Drug Boom
*How World War II's government-funded Penicillin Project turned small drug makers into global empires, and how antibiotic profits, me-too patents, combination drugs, marketing to doctors and the turn to chronic-disease drugs built the modern industry.*

*Source: Gerald Posner, Peterson Academy course "The Pharmaceutical Industry," Lecture 2 (transcript). Second of eight lectures; follows Ch.1 Snake Oil Origins and sets up Ch.3 Selling Sickness. Transcript complete: it ends on a preview of the next lecture (key opinion leaders), consistent with Posner's continuous-talk style.*

**In plain words:** penicillin was found by chance in 1928, but it only became a real medicine in World War II, when the US government paid for a secret program and banned anyone from patenting it. With no patent, many companies made it and the price fell. After the war, the same companies learned to make money another way: patenting small variations of drugs, selling to doctors, and moving toward pills people take every day for life. Posner's facts are marked ✅ and his opinions 🟡, with a 2026 source check where the record differs.

**Words in this note:**
- **USDA**: U.S. Department of Agriculture
- **FDA**: U.S. Food and Drug Administration
- **GLP-1**: a gut hormone that lowers appetite and blood sugar; also a class of drugs (such as semaglutide)
- **TRT**: testosterone replacement therapy
- **DHEA**: a hormone made by the adrenal glands that the body turns into sex hormones
- **ApoB**: apolipoprotein B; counts all the cholesterol particles that can clog arteries

## Why This Matters
This is the lecture where real science arrives, and Posner's theme is "science meets profit." The Penicillin Project shows what the industry can do when government, universities and companies work together without patents. Everything after it shows what the same companies did once they could patent. Nearly every basic feature of today's industry is born here: patent games (me-too drugs, small changes to an existing drug), marketing aimed at doctors, the off-label loophole (using a drug for something it was never approved for), a revolving door with regulators, and the shift from cures to prescriptions for life. Posner is an investigative journalist (author of *Pharma*), not a scientist: his historical facts are marked ✅ when clearly stated, his opinions and interpretations 🟡.

## The Penicillin Hunters — From Mold Juice to Oxford
- **1928, London** (St Mary's Hospital): a Scottish bacteriologist (a germ scientist; named later in the lecture as Alexander Fleming) had been a surgeon in a WWI military hospital and had seen men die of infected cuts. During a warm spell he left petri dishes (small lab dishes) covered in staph bacteria. When he came back, a greenish mold had grown, and the staph around it had mostly disappeared. ✅
- He traced it to the *Penicillium* family of molds but **could not isolate the pure active compound** (the same extraction step that gave us morphine and cocaine in Lecture 1). His 1929 paper called it "mold juice," named it **penicillin**, played down what it could do, and was largely ignored. ✅
- **1936, Oxford:** Howard Florey (a Rhodes scholar), Norman Heatley and Ernst Chain (29, from Berlin; his Jewish family had fled the Nazis in 1933) took it up and managed to isolate it. Tests on people began only in **1940**. ✅ 🟡 Evidence check: Florey put the team together in **1939**. Tests on mice ran in May 1939, the results appeared in *The Lancet* in August 1940, and the first patient was treated in **February 1941** (Gaynes 2017).
- Britain was at war and the government had no money to spare. Oxford tested first on **cancer patients who were dying** (about 300 mg into a vein, "IV"), because there were no rules for medical trials yet. The cancer still killed them, but **no side effects** appeared. ✅
- **The policeman case:** a man in his 30s, cut on the face by a rose thorn, had lost an eye and had abscesses (pockets of pus) on his arm and chest. Within a day of penicillin his fever broke; in three days he was up; by five days he seemed nearly healed. Then the supply ran out, the infection came back, and he died. The proof was complete: the drug worked, there just was not enough of it. ✅ (He was an Oxford policeman treated in February 1941 who died a few weeks later when the supply ran out: Gaynes 2017.)

## Government as Drug Incubator — The Penicillin Project
- Two of the Oxford researchers asked the US for help before America entered the war. Roosevelt launched a secret program that Posner calls the **second largest secret program of WWII after the Manhattan Project** (the atomic bomb). ✅ (his ranking 🟡)
- A lab in **Peoria, Illinois** hosted the British researchers. **Twelve companies volunteered**, including Pfizer, Merck and Squibb, alongside university scientists (Posner names a "Rockefeller Group"). ✅
- Mold samples were gathered from US embassies around the world. The strongest strain came from a **moldy cantaloupe at a farmers' market a couple of miles from the lab**. ✅
- **Pfizer's breakthrough with deep-tank fermentation** (growing the mold in huge tanks) made mass production possible. The transcript links it to citric acid ("this citric acid, leveraged") without explaining how. ✅ Evidence check: the link is real. Pfizer had made citric acid by growing mold in deep tanks since 1919, and used that know-how for penicillin; its converted Brooklyn plant opened on 1 March 1944 (ACS landmark, 2008).
- The **government built the fermentation tanks and plants with taxpayers' money**, for Pfizer and the others. Output went from small (1941 to 1942) to enormous (1943) to hundreds of millions of units by 1945. ✅ (Pfizer's Brooklyn plant alone made more in one month of 1944 than in all of 1943: ACS 2008. The "hundreds of millions of units" figure is as stated; we could not check it.) 🟡 That the government paid for every company's plant is as stated and unverified. Historians do describe the program as built on government leadership, cooperation between companies and open sharing of data (Quinn 2013).
- The deciding rule: **"Nobody gets to patent penicillin."** With a dozen makers of the same product, the price fell and kept falling. Companies sold huge amounts but made little money, and hated it. ✅ 🟢 Evidence check: the drug itself was not patented, but **process patents** (patents on ways of making it) were filed, by Merck and by USDA scientist Andrew Moyer (Gaynes 2017; see Ch.3).

| Partner | Contribution in the Penicillin Project |
| --- | --- |
| Government (US) | All the money, the secrecy, the Peoria lab, the fermentation plants; the no-patent rule |
| Academia (Oxford, US labs) | Isolating the drug, early testing on people, research on mold strains ("unsung heroes") |
| Industry (12 companies) | Fermentation know-how (Pfizer), mass manufacturing, distribution |

🟡 Posner's COVID aside: he expected governments to repeat this model for vaccines, but "they forgot about the 'nobody owns a patent' part." His view, previewed for Ch.8.

## The Postwar Surge — How America Took the World Market
- New antibiotics: **streptomycin** (1943, the first treatment that worked against TB, tuberculosis), **Aureomycin** (1948, the first broad-spectrum antibiotic, meaning it kills many kinds of bacteria), **tetracycline** (1955, "very versatile"). Broad-spectrum drugs suited doctors who did not know which germ a patient had. ✅
- **1945 to 1955: drug-industry revenues up 600%**, driven by antibiotics. ✅ Later he restates this as *Pfizer's* revenues rising 600%, with **80% from tetracycline alone** 🟡 (the two versions conflict; he also says "profits" in the same breath).
- **Germany's collapse was America's chance.** Before the war, German firms such as Schering and Bayer filled about **one in two prescriptions worldwide**. Allied bombing destroyed their plants. ✅ (His list of Bayer discoveries includes acetaminophen alongside aspirin, heroin and barbiturates 🟡, worth checking.)
- **By 1950: 80% of world drug sales came from US companies**, mostly the top 10, along with **95% of the profits**. 🟡 (as stated; we found no source for these shares)
- **2005:** all top-10 drug companies traced their beginnings to the penicillin program. 🟡 (as stated and unverified; several 2005 leaders were European firms, so "all" looks overstated)
- Profits were put back to work aggressively: branches abroad (UK, France, South America), new labs, buying small competitors before they could grow, and **vertical integration** (doing every step themselves: discover, patent, manufacture, distribute, cutting out middlemen). ✅

## Patent Games — Me-Too Drugs and Combination Antibiotics
- Penicillin could not be patented, but **every new variant of an antibiotic could**: streptomycin, Terramycin, Aureomycin and the rest. ✅
- **Me-too drugs:** change a molecule or two of a competitor's drug, patent it, sell it as better. No proof that it worked ("efficacy") was required. ✅
- **The 1938 Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act** only required companies to say if a drug was really dangerous (could kill). There was no full list of side effects and **no requirement to prove the drug worked**. ✅ 🟡 Evidence check: the Act went further on safety than this. Makers had to show the FDA that a new drug was safe before selling it, and labels needed proper directions for safe use; proof that a drug worked was not required until 1962 (FDA Part II).
- Pfizer and others sued each other for copying patents. Courts that "didn't understand the science" ruled that **a changed chemical structure can be patented**. The result was a "Wild West" of dozens of variants each, all promising to kill more germs, cause fewer side effects and cure better. ✅
- **Fixed-dose combinations** (two drugs in one pill): Sigmamycin, marketed by Arthur Sackler, was the first. Companies eventually sold **hundreds** of two-antibiotic combinations on the logic that "two are better than one." Years later (the fights in Congress, next lectures) they were found almost never better than a single antibiotic, with **multiplied side-effect risk**. ✅

## The Dark Side — Overuse and Resistance
- 1950s doctors handed out antibiotics "like candy" for sniffles, sore throats and colds. Prescribing too much was part of the profit, so companies had no reason to hold it back. ✅
- **Late-1940s Japan:** a study saw resistant bacteria (germs the drug no longer kills) in people who had taken penicillin for a long time. Western doctors dismissed it as "something peculiar to Japan." 🟡 (as stated; we could not identify the study)
- **1945:** Fleming himself, visiting America, warned companies that germs would evolve resistance. ✅ He said so publicly in his Nobel lecture of 11 December 1945: a patient given too small a dose could "educate" microbes "to resist penicillin" (Fleming 1945). Posner's version of the reply: "Thanks for discovering it. See you later." ✅ (the reply is his dramatization 🟡)
- Some lawmakers talked about cutting back; nothing was done. Superbugs (germs that resist most drugs) are not yet a crisis in this era, "but it will" be. ✅

## From Cures to Chronic — Miltown and the Business-Model Pivot
- **Lobotomy → Thorazine → Miltown:** the inventor of the lobotomy (brain surgery to calm patients) won a Nobel Prize. **Thorazine** (from France) was sold as "lobotomy light" but left people like zombies. **Miltown**, an early anti-anxiety drug and the forerunner of Librium and Valium, became a 1950s sensation (queues at Hollywood pharmacies, talk on TV). ✅ Posner says Miltown "replaced" Thorazine and groups them under depression 🟡 (order and categories loosely stated). Evidence check: chlorpromazine (Thorazine) was made at Rhône-Poulenc in December 1950, used in psychiatry from 1952 and in the US from 1954, as an **antipsychotic** (a drug for severe mental illness such as psychosis), not an antidepressant (López-Muñoz 2005). Miltown, a tranquilizer (calming drug), was launched by Carter Products in 1955 (Herzberg 2011). They treated different conditions, so one did not replace the other. The lobotomy's inventor, Egas Moniz, won the 1949 Nobel Prize. ✅
- The business insight: antibiotics are taken for 5 to 7 days, and sales depend on how many people are sick. **Miltown is taken every day.** Drugs for lifelong conditions mean steady refills. ✅
- The "dream" target areas from the late '50s and early '60s: **blood pressure, diabetes, depression**. ✅
- 🟡 Posner's argument: chasing profit gives companies a reason to treat symptoms and keep long-term (chronic) conditions going, rather than find causes and cures. The industry would say treating is its job; he is "not necessarily sure that's true."

| Model | Use pattern | Revenue profile (as Posner frames it) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Antibiotic (cure) | Days, when sick | Up and down, tied to epidemics and flu seasons |
| Chronic drug (Miltown, BP, diabetes) | Daily, for years | Reliable refills, "the long game" |

## Where the Money Went — The Late-1950s Spending Snapshot
Posner calls these "rough numbers," averaged from companies that reported them. 🟡

| Category | Share of spending |
| --- | --- |
| Academic research grants | About 15% |
| In-house R&D | About 30% |
| Regulatory compliance | About 5% |
| Marketing and advertising | About 50% |

(R&D means research and development; regulatory compliance means meeting government rules.)

- He says the marketing share "doesn't change much over time," and that today the top 10 companies still spend more on marketing and promotion than on R&D. **Adding share buybacks** (companies buying back their own stock to raise its price), the total is far more than R&D. 🟡 (his argument against the "we need profits for R&D" defence. He grants that developing a drug is now expensive, about a billion dollars per drug, counting failures; 🟢 a 2020 analysis put the median at $985 million including failures: Wouters 2020). 🟡 Evidence check: US medical marketing totalled $29.9 billion in 2016, most of it aimed at doctors (Schwartz 2019). Whether marketing is bigger than R&D depends on how each is counted, so this is disputed.
- **Doctors as the gatekeepers:** marketing went to doctors, not patients (leaflets, hardcover books for Valium, ads in medical journals, paid-for studies likely to come out well). Doctors usually do not know or think about what a drug costs the patient, so high prices do not stop them prescribing. ✅
- **Partnership with universities:** Merck and others teamed up with Harvard, Yale and Johns Hopkins; researchers liked having their names on discoveries. ✅

## Political Capture — The Seeds
- **The 1938 off-label provision:** companies won the right for doctors to prescribe a drug for anything they see fit. With a few dozen kinds of drugs then, that seemed manageable. Today, with thousands, Posner calls it **"one of the greatest loopholes,"** worth billions in sales for uses that were never approved. ✅ (characterization 🟡) 🟡 Evidence check: off-label prescribing is not part of the 1938 Act. It exists because the FDA regulates drugs, not how doctors practise medicine; companies themselves may not advertise unapproved uses (Wittich 2012).
- **Revolving door** (officials moving between the regulator and the industry): Arthur Sackler and others invited the FDA's head of antibiotic approvals to join their antibiotics magazine, and nobody saw a conflict. Later, the FDA official who approved OxyContin's label went to work for Purdue Pharma. ✅
- **The Sacklers in the 1950s:** three brothers, all psychiatrists. Posner says two lost their security clearances at a state mental hospital over Communist Party membership 🟡 (his claim; unverified here). Arthur set up foreign branches before he even had a product. ✅
- Posner: the drug industry "buys compliance on both sides of the aisle" (from both political parties). 🟡 Opinion.
- Industry background: according to Moody's, the industry did not exist as a category in 1909; by 1929 it was listed with essentially one product, insulin 🟡 (as stated; unverified).

## Punchlines & Quotable Facts (Lecture 2)
- **"Nobody gets to patent penicillin."** The no-patent rule pushed prices toward zero.
- **A moldy cantaloupe from a Peoria farmers' market** gave the strongest strain.
- **The policeman lived five good days, then died when the penicillin ran out.** Proof by shortage.
- **1950: 80% of world drug sales and 95% of profits were American.** Germany's bombed plants made it possible.
- **All top-10 drug companies in 2005 traced back to the penicillin program.**
- **1945 to 1955: revenues up 600% on antibiotics alone.**
- **Change a molecule, get a patent.** The court ruling that gave birth to me-too drugs.
- **Marketing about 50%, R&D about 30%.** A rough late-1950s split.
- **Antibiotics last a week; Miltown is every day.** The turn to chronic drugs.
- **Off-label prescribing: a loophole still worth billions** (Posner dates it to 1938; it rests on the FDA not regulating medical practice).

## Key Corrections to Carry Forward
- 🔴 "Big Pharma built itself": the US government paid for the Penicillin Project, its research and its fermentation plants; the industry's head start was paid for by taxpayers.
- 🔴 "A new patented version means a better drug": me-too variants and two-in-one combinations were patented and sold without proof that they worked, and the combinations were almost never better.
- 🔴 "Most drug spending is R&D": in Posner's late-1950s snapshot, marketing was about half and R&D about a third; he argues that marketing plus buybacks still exceed R&D.
- 🔴 "FDA approval covers every use": off-label prescribing lets doctors use drugs for purposes never tested and approved, although companies may not advertise them.
- 🔴 "Resistance was a surprise": Fleming warned in 1945 and a late-1940s Japanese study saw it; both were brushed aside.
- 🔴 "Penicillin was a lone eureka": it took a lucky find in 1928, a 1929 paper, an Oxford team from 1939 to 1941, and a wartime government program to turn it into a drug.

## Practical Takeaway
Study points, not medical instructions. Posner's "smarter patient" questions:
1. **Who paid for the original research?** (He now says this matters less than he used to think.)
2. **Why is this drug being prescribed to me?** Is it a fashionable drug (Miltown, Valium, GLP-1s) or chosen for my case? "There's no free lunch in pharmaceuticals."
3. **Is this treating an infection or a convenience?** Ask before taking antibiotics for a cold.
4. **Are there non-drug options that work as well?** Lifestyle change is "less profitable." He grants that GLP-1 drugs can change the lives of people with severe obesity, but questions using them to lose 20 pounds for a reunion.

## Questions to Learn From (Socratic) — with Answer Options
**1. Given how the Penicillin Project was run, why did the price of penicillin keep falling after the war?**
A. Pfizer cut prices voluntarily to win goodwill
B. \* The government barred anyone from patenting it, so a dozen manufacturers competed on the same product
C. Penicillin turned out to be less effective than sulfa drugs
*Why B: Posner presents it as a textbook free market: no patent, many makers, high demand, falling price, and companies hated the thin profits.*

**2. Given the 1950s legal environment Posner describes, what made "me-too" antibiotics both possible and profitable?**
A. \* Courts held that a changed chemical structure is patentable, and the 1938 Act required no proof of efficacy, so slight variants could be patented and marketed as better
B. The FDA required each variant to beat existing drugs in head-to-head trials
C. Penicillin's patent expired, opening the field
*Why A: easy patents plus no need to prove the drug worked let companies sell variants on promises alone.*

**3. Given Posner's account of Miltown, what was the key business lesson the industry drew?**
A. Psychiatric drugs are too risky to market widely
B. Antibiotics are more profitable because people get sick often
C. \* Drugs taken daily for lifelong conditions produce steadier, larger revenue than short-course cures, so the industry shifted toward chronic treatments
*Why C: antibiotics last days and depend on waves of illness; Miltown users took it every day, which pointed companies toward blood pressure, diabetes and depression.*

**4. Given the late-1950s spending snapshot, which statement best reflects Posner's argument?**
A. R&D was the largest category, so high prices are justified by research
B. \* Marketing was about half of spending and R&D about a third, so the "we need profits for R&D" defence overstates research's share
C. Regulatory compliance consumed most of the budget
*Why B: his rough numbers put marketing near 50% and R&D near 30%, and he says marketing plus buybacks still exceed R&D today.*
