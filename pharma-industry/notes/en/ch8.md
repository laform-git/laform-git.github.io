
# Posner — The Pharmaceutical Industry, Ch.8: Pandemic Profits
*How Posner reads COVID-19 as pharma's biggest crisis-as-opportunity: public money and liability shields, private patents, secret contracts, vaccine windfalls, buybacks, the booster economy, and his closing rules for being a smarter patient.*

*Source: Gerald Posner, Peterson Academy course "The Pharmaceutical Industry," Lecture 8 (transcript). Final lecture of the eight-part course; it closes the series with COVID-19 as the case study for every pattern in Ch.1 to Ch.7, then a short audience Q&A on doctors, the FDA and the ProPublica payments database. Transcript complete: it ends naturally at the end of the Q&A.*

## Why This Matters
This is the capstone lecture. Posner takes the playbook he built across the course (crisis, public funding, private patent, weak pricing oversight, market expansion) and runs COVID-19 through it in real time. He is explicit that he is not discussing whether the vaccines worked or were needed; he is "following the money." The lecture is useful for two reasons: it shows how emergency conditions changed the rules (liability shields, purchase guarantees, conflict-of-interest exemptions), and it ends with his most practical material in the whole course, a short list of habits for being an informed, skeptical patient without rejecting care. Posner is an investigative journalist (author of *Pharma: Greed, Lies, and the Poisoning of America*), not a scientist: his historical and financial facts are marked ✅ when clearly stated, and his interpretations 🟡. Several dollar figures in this lecture are internally inconsistent and are flagged.

## The Setup — A Book About the Wrong Pandemic
- *Pharma* was published **March 10, 2020** (five years of research with his wife Trisha, hundreds of interviews, tens of thousands of pages of documents). The WHO declared COVID-19 a pandemic **the next day**, and his book tour collapsed. ✅
- The book's second-to-last chapter, "The Coming Pandemic," predicted a pandemic, but a **superbug** one: antibiotic resistance, because companies abandoned antibiotics (a 5-to-7-day course is a small market; they prefer chronic and orphan drugs). Karen Bush is quoted: "It's not a question of if… it's a question of when." COVID is not in the book. ✅
- Days later he told *The Intercept*: "Pharmaceutical companies view COVID-19 as a **once-in-a-lifetime business opportunity**." He then wrote a *New York Times* op-ed, "Big Pharma May Pose an Obstacle to Vaccine Development." ✅
- Early-2020 pushback: in the fear phase, people told him they did not care if pharma made "a trillion dollars" if it saved the planet. 🟡 His recollection of the public mood.
- **June 2020 FDA bar:** vaccines only had to be **50% more effective than placebo**. Posner says infectious-disease vaccines normally need at least 70%, and 50% is flu-shot territory. His reading: a low bar meant control rather than elimination, and likely recurring boosters. ✅ (the 50% threshold) / 🟡 (the 70% norm and his inference)

## Crisis as Catalyst — The Century-Long Pattern

| Crisis | Pharma response (as Posner frames it) |
| --- | --- |
| 19th-century epidemics | Patent medicines marketed to "cure" each outbreak (Ch.1) |
| World War II | Penicillin and the antibiotic boom (Ch.2) |
| Post-war anxiety | Tranquilizers; pharma both fed and exploited the "age of anxiety" (Ch.5) |
| Chronic pain, economic despair | Opioid surge |
| COVID-19 | "The single greatest crisis and opportunity" in modern pharma |

- **Reputation rehab:** not every company could make a vaccine, but all benefited. Pharma went from 1950s heroes (penicillin) to distrusted (opioids); a pandemic win could do "more for us in a short amount of time than a hundred PR companies can over a decade." 🟡 Interpretation.
- **mRNA as goldmine:** the vaccines were a "subsidized breakthrough" of decades of mRNA research, with manufacturing scale-up dwarfing penicillin's. He stresses he is not commenting on vaccine quality. 🟡

## Socializing Risk, Privatizing Reward
**What the public side carried** (NIH, BARDA, Operation Warp Speed): early R&D, clinical trials, subsidies, purchase guarantees, and **liability shields** (no lawsuits for serious side effects). ✅

**What pharma kept:** the intellectual property, which brings long-term pricing power and global distribution profits from publicly funded work. ✅

- **The penicillin model he wanted:** fund the companies generously, expedite everything, but make sure **nobody owns the patent**. Because no COVID drug existed yet, Posner says this was a rare clean opportunity, unlike AZT and Truvada (Ch.7), where NIH did the work and companies kept the patents. 🟡 His policy preference.
- **The lost clause:** the first emergency tranche was **about $8 billion, with $3 to 4 billion for vaccine research**, passed about **eight days** later under a Republican president and a Democratic House (Pelosi). The first draft contained a no-private-IP clause, and it was **gone from the final bill**. Pharma lobbyists argued that giving away the IP would give away years of proprietary mRNA know-how. ✅ (as he reports it)
- **The NIH factoid:** since the 1930s NIH has invested about **$900 billion** in research that companies used to patent brand-name drugs (as of 2020; "getting up to a trillion"). His analogy: taxes fund schools that benefit everyone, but that should not allow a company to charge **$23,000 a vial for Acthar gel**. ✅ / 🟡 (analogy)

## The Contracts — Secrecy, Leverage, Guaranteed Markets
- **Secret contracts:** you had to sue or apply to see them, and they came back heavily redacted. ✅
- **Guaranteed purchases** before anyone knew whether the product worked; **indemnification**; **exclusive supply rights**; **full protection of proprietary formulas**. ✅
- **Wealthy nations first:** first batches were sold where they paid; donations to poorer countries came later under pressure. "Global inequity is a feature, not a bug": booster access was stratified by GDP, not medical need. 🟡 Framing.
- **Conflicts of interest:** industry executives helped run Operation Warp Speed, brought in as **contractors**, so federal conflict-of-interest rules for employees did not apply. Advisors could **keep their holdings**, with only a requirement to assign some COVID-19 profits to NIH **after they died**. ✅ (as stated; the posthumous-gift detail is his reading of the terms 🟡)

Cost per dose is unknown; Pfizer does not break it out, and estimates run to "a few dollars." 🟡 Interpretation; the unknown cost is ✅.

## Windfalls, Stock Spikes and Buybacks
- **Stock moves:** Moderna up **30% in one day (May 2020)** on Phase 1 results; Pfizer and BioNTech's **November 2020** announcement of **90% effectiveness** lifted the whole market. Posner questions what "effective" meant at that point. No company ever announced bad news. ✅ / 🟡 (his inference that companies knew their statements moved stocks)
- **Moderna's position:** a company that had never brought a drug to market got billions in aid, its first product, an expedited review and no lawsuit risk. 🟡 Characterization.
- **Profits doubled:** the 20 biggest pharma companies went from **$83 billion (2020) to $188 billion (2022)**. The "rising tide" included respiratory drugs and test kits (made in China for under a dollar each, bought in huge numbers by governments). ✅
- **Margins:** top-20 average **19%**, rivaling oil and gas (about 17% in 2022). Vaccine makers: **BioNTech 54%, Moderna 51%, Pfizer 28%**. Bristol-Myers Squibb at 3% because these firms are now conglomerates; pure drug companies run 25 to 35%. ✅
- **Shareholders vs R&D:** payouts (dividends, buybacks, executive pay) ran close to R&D, and some exceeded it; **AbbVie spent nearly 50% more** on payouts than on R&D. ✅ (He also read "Pfizer $223 million, $30 million R&D" off a chart; units are probably billions 🟡.)

| Company | Stock buybacks | Executive bonuses |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Pfizer | $8.5 billion | $21 million |

| J&J | $7.5 billion | $29 million |

- **The post-COVID pivot:** COVID vaccine revenue falls each year from 2021 to 2025, but the projected **mRNA therapeutics market** keeps rising (Vision Research: **$45 billion by 2034**). Publicly funded COVID work spills into other mRNA products, and no company will break out how much. 🟡 Inference.

## The Booster Economy and Marketing
- **Crisis becomes continuity:** a one-time vaccine is profitable; a recurring one is far more so. The aim is to move from pandemic emergency to **"endemic management"**, with predictable annual revenue, premium pricing and new products for age and risk subgroups. His Walgreens pharmacist in Miami Beach now asks about COVID shots alongside flu, pneumonia and shingles. 🟡 Interpretation of strategy.
- **IP share of doses** ("vaccine knowledge lockdown"): J&J 70%, AstraZeneca 75%, Moderna 85%, Pfizer/BioNTech 90%. ✅ (as read from a chart)
- **Targeting women:** messaging framed vaccination as maternal caregiving ("protect your family"), leaning on anxiety and guilt, echoing earlier campaigns aimed at women (Ch.5). Social campaigns targeted **women aged 25 to 54**, the group the industry says makes household health decisions. 🟡 His reading; he says he does not know if the household claim is true.
- **mRNA as a badge:** expect "mRNA" to be marketed like "organic," as a cutting-edge label, whether or not a given product is good. 🟡
- **IP was the ultimate test:** pharma feared a penicillin replay (no leverage, price driven to near nothing). This time it had the leverage and held its ground. Annual vaccine markets were already normalized: 20 to 25 years ago companies nearly left vaccines over litigation, then got liability protection. 🟡 / ✅ (the earlier exit and protection are from his book)
- **Not a conspiracy claim:** he says outright that pharma did not create COVID; once it happened, they moved to make as much as possible from it. ✅

## The Smarter Patient — His Closing Rules
1. **Be system-aware:** watch behavior during a crisis; emergencies can reshape markets permanently.
2. **Question the urgency**, even when everyone is running for the exit.
3. **Separate innovation from pricing:** new to the market is not the same as better science.
4. **Understand the limits of regulation:** do not put all your faith in the FDA; in a crisis both parties rushed billions to pharma.
5. **Recognize marketing as a sales strategy** and **assume financial incentives shape behavior.**

Balance he insists on: **modern medicine saves lives**. Infections that killed our ancestors (a rosebush cut) no longer do. "Being a smarter patient does not mean rejecting care." If a doctor says you are very sick and need a drug, ask questions; do not refuse it on cynicism. Be skeptical, not distrustful: "informed participation."

## Q&A — Doctors, the FDA and ProPublica
- **FDA:** he gives approvals initial benefit of the doubt, then checks PubMed later to see whether trials were handled correctly. 🟡 Personal practice.
- **Doctors:** ProPublica (with *The New York Times*) built a **searchable database** of industry payments to physicians. Posner searched his own doctor and found about **$384** (a couple of dinners); he was ready to switch if it had been large. Most doctors average a few hundred dollars; a handful take millions, often labeled research. ✅
- Headlines spin it: "pharma-friendly outlets" led with the small average. Doctors and pharma are allied but not symmetric: pharma needs doctors; doctors could practice without pharma money. 🟡

## Key Corrections to Carry Forward
- 🔴 "Low price means no profiteering": about $20 a dose across billions of doses produced 50%+ margins; the price is set by calculation, not generosity.
- 🔴 "Taxpayers fund it, so taxpayers own it": public money carried the risk, but companies kept the IP and pricing power.
- 🔴 "Vaccine sales falling means the windfall is over": publicly funded mRNA know-how now feeds a projected $45B therapeutics market.
- 🔴 "Posner is anti-vaccine or a conspiracy theorist": he says neither; his subject is the money, not efficacy or origin.
- 🔴 "Skepticism means refusing treatment": he explicitly rejects that; ask questions, then accept needed care.
- 🔴 "Your doctor's industry payments are unknowable": the ProPublica database lets you look them up.

## Questions to Learn From (Socratic) — with Answer Options
**1. Given Posner's account of the first COVID emergency bill, what was the "penicillin model" he says was lost?**
A. Requiring vaccines to be 70% effective before approval
B. \* Public funding of development with no company owning the resulting patent, so prices could fall to near cost
C. Letting the military run all vaccine manufacturing
*Why B: with penicillin, government funded factories and research and nobody owned the patent; the COVID bill's first draft had a similar no-IP clause that was removed before passage.*

**2. Given that the vaccines were priced around $20 a dose, which reading matches Posner's argument?**
A. The low price proves pharma did not profit from COVID
B. Pfizer disclosed a production cost close to $20, so the margin was thin
C. \* The price was calculated to look like a bargain while still yielding margins over 50% across billions of doses, and the true cost per dose is undisclosed
*Why C: he cites BioNTech 54% and Moderna 51% margins and notes Pfizer never breaks out cost per dose.*

**3. Given his "booster economy" argument, why would a 50%-effective bar matter commercially?**
A. \* A vaccine that controls rather than eliminates a virus supports recurring boosters and predictable annual revenue
B. A lower bar meant the vaccines could not be sold to governments
C. It forced companies to give up their IP
*Why A: Posner links the 50% threshold to "endemic management," where annual shots, like the flu shot, become a steady market.*

**4. Given his closing advice, what does "smarter patient" mean for Posner?**
A. Refuse drugs from companies with high profit margins
B. \* Stay engaged and skeptical (ask why, check the ProPublica database, separate innovation from price) without rejecting care you genuinely need
C. Trust FDA approvals completely, because regulation removes the need for questions
*Why B: he insists "being a smarter patient does not mean rejecting care" and calls his stance "informed participation," not distrust.*
