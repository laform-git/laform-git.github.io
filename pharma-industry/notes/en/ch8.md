
# Posner — The Pharmaceutical Industry, Ch.8: Pandemic Profits
*How Posner reads COVID-19 as pharma's biggest crisis-as-opportunity: public money and liability shields, private patents, secret contracts, vaccine windfalls, buybacks, the booster economy, and his closing rules for being a smarter patient.*

*Source: Gerald Posner, Peterson Academy course "The Pharmaceutical Industry," Lecture 8 (transcript). Final lecture of the eight-part course; it closes the series with COVID-19 as the case study for every pattern in Ch.1 to Ch.7, then a short audience Q&A on doctors, the FDA and the ProPublica payments database. Transcript complete: it ends naturally at the end of the Q&A.*

**In plain words:** in this last lecture Posner "follows the money" in COVID-19. He does not discuss whether the vaccines worked. His point is that taxpayers paid most of the cost and carried the risk (research, trials, guaranteed purchases, protection from lawsuits), while the companies kept the patents and made record profits. He ends with simple habits for being a questioning patient without refusing care you need. His facts are marked ✅ and his opinions 🟡; several of his dollar figures disagree with each other and are flagged, with a 2026 source check.

**Words in this note:**
- **FDA**: U.S. Food and Drug Administration
- **WHO**: World Health Organization
- **mRNA**: messenger RNA, the copy of a gene the cell reads to build a protein
- **NIH**: U.S. National Institutes of Health
- **TRT**: testosterone replacement therapy
- **DHEA**: a hormone made by the adrenal glands that the body turns into sex hormones
- **ApoB**: apolipoprotein B; counts all the cholesterol particles that can clog arteries

## Why This Matters
This is the final lecture that pulls the course together. Posner takes the playbook he built across the course (a crisis, public money, a private patent, weak control of prices, a bigger market) and runs COVID-19 through it as it happened. He says clearly that he is not discussing whether the vaccines worked or were needed; he is "following the money." The lecture is useful for two reasons. It shows how emergency conditions changed the rules (protection from lawsuits, guaranteed purchases, exemptions from conflict-of-interest rules). And it ends with his most practical material in the whole course: a short list of habits for being an informed, questioning patient without rejecting care. Posner is an investigative journalist (author of *Pharma: Greed, Lies, and the Poisoning of America*), not a scientist: his historical and financial facts are marked ✅ when clearly stated, and his interpretations 🟡. Several dollar figures in this lecture contradict each other and are flagged.

## The Setup — A Book About the Wrong Pandemic
- *Pharma* was published on **March 10, 2020** (five years of research with his wife Trisha, hundreds of interviews, tens of thousands of pages of documents). The WHO (World Health Organization) declared COVID-19 a pandemic **the next day**, and his book tour fell apart. ✅
- The book's second-to-last chapter, "The Coming Pandemic," predicted a pandemic, but of a **superbug** (a germ that resists antibiotics). The reason: companies had walked away from antibiotics (a 5-to-7-day course is a small market; they prefer drugs for long-term illness and rare diseases). Karen Bush is quoted: "It's not a question of if… it's a question of when." COVID is not in the book. ✅
- Days later he told *The Intercept*: "Pharmaceutical companies view COVID-19 as a **once-in-a-lifetime business opportunity**." He then wrote a *New York Times* opinion piece, "Big Pharma May Pose an Obstacle to Vaccine Development." ✅ (🟡 order: the op-ed is dated 2 March 2020, so it came before the book's release and the Intercept interview: TWN reprint 2020)
- Pushback in early 2020: in the frightened early months, people told him they did not care if the drug industry made "a trillion dollars" if it saved the planet. 🟡 His memory of the public mood.
- **The FDA's bar in June 2020:** vaccines only had to be **50% more effective than placebo** (a dummy treatment). Posner says vaccines for infectious diseases normally need at least 70%, and 50% is the level of a flu shot. His reading: a low bar meant controlling the virus rather than wiping it out, and probably repeated boosters. ✅ (the 50% threshold) / 🟡 (the 70% norm and his conclusion). Evidence check: the FDA's June 2020 guidance asked for at least 50% efficacy against placebo (RAPS 2020). There is no general 70% rule for vaccines; WHO's target for COVID-19 vaccines set about 50% as the minimum and about 70% as the preferred efficacy (WHO TPP).

## Crisis as Catalyst — The Century-Long Pattern

| Crisis | Pharma response (as Posner frames it) |
| --- | --- |
| 19th-century epidemics | Patent medicines sold to "cure" each outbreak (Ch.1) |
| World War II | Penicillin and the antibiotic boom (Ch.2) |
| Post-war anxiety | Tranquilizers; the industry both fed and used the "age of anxiety" (Ch.5) |
| Chronic pain, economic despair | The opioid surge |
| COVID-19 | "The single greatest crisis and opportunity" in modern drug-making |

- **Repairing its reputation:** not every company could make a vaccine, but all of them benefited. The industry had gone from heroes in the 1950s (penicillin) to distrusted (opioids); a pandemic win could do "more for us in a short amount of time than a hundred PR companies can over a decade." 🟡 Interpretation.
- **mRNA as a goldmine:** the vaccines were a "subsidized breakthrough" built on decades of research into mRNA (a molecule that gives cells instructions to make a protein). The factory scale-up dwarfed penicillin's. He stresses he is not commenting on how good the vaccines were. 🟡

## Socializing Risk, Privatizing Reward
**What the public side carried** (NIH; BARDA, the government's emergency medicine agency; Operation Warp Speed): early research, medical trials, subsidies, guaranteed purchases, and **protection from lawsuits** (no suing over serious side effects). ✅

**What the companies kept:** the intellectual property (IP: patents and know-how), which brings long-term control of prices and worldwide profits from work paid for by the public. ✅

- **The penicillin model he wanted:** pay the companies generously and speed everything up, but make sure **nobody owns the patent**. Because no COVID drug existed yet, Posner says this was a rare clean chance, unlike AZT and Truvada (Ch.7), where the NIH did the work and companies kept the patents. 🟡 His policy preference.
- **The lost clause:** the first emergency payment was **about $8 billion, with $3 to 4 billion for vaccine research**, passed about **eight days** later under a Republican president and a Democratic House (Pelosi). The first draft contained a clause saying no private company could own the IP, and it was **gone from the final bill**. Drug-industry lobbyists argued that giving away the IP would give away years of private mRNA know-how. ✅ (as he reports it) 🟡 Evidence check: the law signed on 6 March 2020 contained no IP provisions, only that purchases follow "fair and reasonable pricing" rules and that HHS (the health department) "may" act under existing law to keep products affordable (CRS 2020). A 2 March House draft added that HHS "shall not take actions that delay the development" of such products (KEI 2020). We could not see the no-IP clause he describes in any draft we checked.
- **The NIH fact:** since the 1930s the NIH has put about **$900 billion** into research that companies used to patent brand-name drugs (as of 2020; "getting up to a trillion"; 🟡 figure as stated and unverified). His comparison: taxes pay for schools that benefit everyone, but that should not let a company charge **$23,000 a vial for Acthar gel**. ✅ / 🟡 (analogy)

## The Contracts — Secrecy, Leverage, Guaranteed Markets
- **Secret contracts:** you had to sue or apply to see them, and they came back with large parts blacked out. ✅
- **Guaranteed purchases** before anyone knew whether the product worked; **indemnification** (the government covering the companies' legal costs); **sole supply rights**; **full protection of private formulas**. ✅
- **Rich nations first:** the first batches went where they paid; donations to poorer countries came later, under pressure. "Global inequity is a feature, not a bug": access to boosters depended on a country's wealth (GDP), not on medical need. 🟡 Framing.
- **Conflicts of interest:** industry executives helped run Operation Warp Speed, brought in as **contractors**, so the federal conflict-of-interest rules for government employees did not apply. Advisors could **keep their shares**, with only a requirement to give some COVID-19 profits to the NIH **after they died**. ✅ (as stated; the after-death gift detail is his reading of the terms 🟡)

- **The pricing trick:** around **$20 a dose** looked like a bargain next to rare-disease drugs at $500,000 to $3 million. Posner: they could not charge $1,000 ("the planet will be burning down their headquarters"), so they set the price at a calculated maximum across billions of doses. The cost to make each dose is unknown; Pfizer does not report it separately, and estimates run to "a few dollars." 🟡 Interpretation; the unknown cost is ✅.

## Windfalls, Stock Spikes and Buybacks
- **Stock moves:** Moderna rose **30% in one day (May 2020)** on Phase 1 results (the first, small trial in people); Pfizer and BioNTech's **November 2020** announcement of **90% effectiveness** lifted the whole stock market. Posner questions what "effective" meant at that point. No company ever announced bad news. ✅ / 🟡 (his conclusion that companies knew their statements moved stocks)
- **Moderna's position:** a company that had never brought a drug to market got billions in aid, its first product, a fast-tracked review and no risk of lawsuits. 🟡 Characterization.
- **Profits doubled:** the 20 biggest drug companies went from **$83 billion (2020) to $188 billion (2022)** (🟡 as stated; source not identified). The "rising tide" included breathing drugs and test kits (made in China for under a dollar each, bought in huge numbers by governments). ✅
- **Profit margins** (the share of sales kept as profit): the top-20 average was **19%**, close to oil and gas (about 17% in 2022). Vaccine makers: **BioNTech 54%, Moderna 51%, Pfizer 28%** (🟡 as stated; source and year not identified). Bristol-Myers Squibb was at 3%, because these firms are now conglomerates (groups of many businesses); pure drug companies run at 25 to 35%. ✅
- **Shareholders vs. research:** payouts (dividends, buybacks, executive pay) came close to research spending, and some were bigger; **AbbVie spent nearly 50% more** on payouts than on research. ✅ (He also read "Pfizer $223 million, $30 million R&D" off a chart; the units are probably billions 🟡.)

| Company | Stock buybacks | Executive bonuses |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Pfizer | $8.5 billion | $21 million |
| Moderna | $3 billion | $18 million |
| J&J | $7.5 billion | $29 million |

(Stock buybacks are when a company buys back its own shares, which tends to raise their price.)

- **The turn after COVID:** COVID vaccine revenue falls each year from 2021 to 2025, but the expected **market for mRNA treatments** keeps rising (Vision Research: **$45 billion by 2034**). COVID work paid for by the public flows into other mRNA products, and no company will say how much. 🟡 Inference.

## The Booster Economy and Marketing
- **Crisis becomes routine:** a one-time vaccine is profitable; a repeated one is far more so. The aim is to move from a pandemic emergency to **"endemic management"** (living with the virus long term), with predictable yearly revenue, premium prices and new products for age and risk groups. His Walgreens pharmacist in Miami Beach now asks about COVID shots alongside flu, pneumonia and shingles. 🟡 His reading of the strategy.
- **Share of doses protected by IP** ("vaccine knowledge lockdown"): J&J 70%, AstraZeneca 75%, Moderna 85%, Pfizer/BioNTech 90%. ✅ (as read from a chart)
- **Aiming at women:** messages framed vaccination as a mother's caregiving ("protect your family"), leaning on anxiety and guilt, echoing earlier campaigns aimed at women (Ch.5). Social-media campaigns targeted **women aged 25 to 54**, the group the industry says makes health decisions for the household. 🟡 His reading; he says he does not know if the household claim is true.
- **mRNA as a badge:** expect "mRNA" to be marketed like "organic," as a cutting-edge label, whether or not a given product is good. 🟡
- **IP was the final test:** the industry feared a repeat of penicillin (no leverage, prices pushed close to nothing). This time it had the leverage and held its ground. Yearly vaccine markets were already normal: 20 to 25 years ago companies nearly left vaccines because of lawsuits, then got legal protection. 🟡 / ✅ (the earlier exit and protection are from his book) Evidence check: the timing is earlier: the National Childhood Vaccine Injury Act of **1986** responded to lawsuits that "threatened to cause vaccine shortages," and its compensation program began in 1988 (HRSA).
- **Not a conspiracy claim:** he says plainly that the drug industry did not create COVID; once it happened, they moved to make as much from it as possible. ✅

## The Smarter Patient — His Closing Rules
1. **Be aware of the system:** watch how companies behave during a crisis; emergencies can reshape markets for good.
2. **Question the urgency**, even when everyone is running for the exit.
3. **Separate innovation from pricing:** new on the market is not the same as better science.
4. **Know the limits of regulation:** do not put all your faith in the FDA; in a crisis both parties rushed billions to the drug industry.
5. **See marketing as a sales strategy**, and **assume money shapes behavior.**

The balance he insists on: **modern medicine saves lives**. Infections that killed our ancestors (a cut from a rosebush) no longer do. "Being a smarter patient does not mean rejecting care." If a doctor says you are very sick and need a drug, ask questions; do not refuse it out of cynicism. Be questioning, not distrustful: "informed participation."

## Q&A — Doctors, the FDA and ProPublica
- **FDA:** he gives an approval the benefit of the doubt at first, then checks PubMed (the free database of medical studies) later to see whether the trials were done properly. 🟡 Personal practice.
- **Doctors:** ProPublica (with *The New York Times*) built a **searchable database** of payments from the industry to doctors. Posner searched his own doctor and found about **$384** (a couple of dinners); he was ready to switch if it had been large. Most doctors average a few hundred dollars; a handful take millions, often labelled research. ✅ (The official source is the federal **Open Payments** database, which drug and device makers must report to and which anyone can search at openpaymentsdata.cms.gov; ProPublica's tool was built on such data: CMS.)
- Headlines spin it: "pharma-friendly outlets" led with the small average. Doctors and drug companies are allies, but not equal ones: the industry needs doctors; doctors could practise without drug-company money. 🟡

## Punchlines & Quotable Facts (Lecture 8)
- **"Pharmaceutical companies view COVID-19 as a once-in-a-lifetime business opportunity."** Said days after the WHO declaration.
- **The no-patent clause was in the first draft and gone from the final bill.** The penicillin model, lost in about eight days.
- **NIH: about $900 billion since the 1930s** in research that companies used for brand-name patents.
- **The 20 biggest drug companies: $83B profit (2020) to $188B (2022).** Profits doubled.
- **Margins: BioNTech 54%, Moderna 51%, Pfizer 28%;** the industry 19% vs. oil and gas about 17%.
- **Pfizer: $8.5B in buybacks and $21M in executive bonuses.** AbbVie spent nearly 50% more on payouts than on research.
- **About $20 a dose looked cheap by design.** The real cost per dose is not disclosed.
- **"Being a smarter patient does not mean rejecting care."** Questioning, not distrust.

## Key Corrections to Carry Forward
- 🔴 "A low price means no profiteering": about $20 a dose across billions of doses produced margins over 50%; the price was set by calculation, not generosity.
- 🔴 "Taxpayers fund it, so taxpayers own it": public money carried the risk, but companies kept the IP and the control of prices.
- 🔴 "Falling vaccine sales mean the windfall is over": mRNA know-how paid for by the public now feeds an expected $45B market for treatments.
- 🔴 "Posner is anti-vaccine or a conspiracy theorist": he says he is neither; his subject is the money, not whether the vaccines worked or where the virus came from.
- 🔴 "Being questioning means refusing treatment": he clearly rejects that; ask questions, then accept care you need.
- 🔴 "Your doctor's payments from the industry can't be known": the ProPublica database and the federal Open Payments site let you look them up.

## Practical Takeaway
Ask why a drug is urgent, who paid for it and who profits; look up your doctor's industry payments on Open Payments; and accept the care you genuinely need.

## Questions to Learn From (Socratic) — with Answer Options
**1. Given Posner's account of the first COVID emergency bill, what was the "penicillin model" he says was lost?**
A. Requiring vaccines to be 70% effective before approval
B. \* Public funding of development with no company owning the resulting patent, so prices could fall to near cost
C. Letting the military run all vaccine manufacturing
*Why B: with penicillin, the government paid for factories and research and nobody owned the patent; the COVID bill's first draft had a similar no-IP clause that was removed before it passed.*

**2. Given that the vaccines were priced around $20 a dose, which reading matches Posner's argument?**
A. The low price proves pharma did not profit from COVID
B. Pfizer disclosed a production cost close to $20, so the margin was thin
C. \* The price was calculated to look like a bargain while still yielding margins over 50% across billions of doses, and the true cost per dose is undisclosed
*Why C: he cites margins of 54% for BioNTech and 51% for Moderna and notes that Pfizer never reports the cost per dose.*

**3. Given his "booster economy" argument, why would a 50%-effective bar matter commercially?**
A. \* A vaccine that controls rather than eliminates a virus supports recurring boosters and predictable annual revenue
B. A lower bar meant the vaccines could not be sold to governments
C. It forced companies to give up their IP
*Why A: Posner links the 50% threshold to "endemic management," where yearly shots, like the flu shot, become a steady market.*

**4. Given his closing advice, what does "smarter patient" mean for Posner?**
A. Refuse drugs from companies with high profit margins
B. \* Stay engaged and skeptical (ask why, check the ProPublica database, separate innovation from price) without rejecting care you genuinely need
C. Trust FDA approvals completely, because regulation removes the need for questions
*Why B: he insists "being a smarter patient does not mean rejecting care" and calls his stance "informed participation," not distrust.*
