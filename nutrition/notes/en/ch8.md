
# Lugavere — Introduction to Nutrition, Ch.8: Microbiome Mastery
*Frames the oral and gut microbiomes as co-evolved partners you feed (fiber diversity, resistant starch, fermented foods), protect (mouthwash, antibiotics, additives), and support (slow, relaxed eating), then closes with Lugavere's supplement picks.*

*Source: Max Lugavere, Peterson Academy course "Introduction to Nutrition," Lecture 8 (transcript). Final lecture of the 8-part series; it recaps threads from earlier chapters (nitrates in greens, fiber, supplemental HCl, vitamin D as a hormone) rather than introducing a wholly new framework.*

**In plain words:** the body relies on its microbes (bacteria and other tiny organisms) to turn food into health. Bacteria in the mouth turn nitrates from leafy greens into a step toward nitric oxide, which helps blood vessels, and bacteria in the colon turn fiber into helpful compounds such as butyrate. Feed them a wide variety of plants and fermented foods, add fiber slowly, and sit down and chew calmly so you digest well. Lugavere's supplement choices are his own and are checked against the evidence below.

**Words in this note:**
- **LDL**: low-density lipoprotein, the "bad" cholesterol carrier
- **ApoB**: apolipoprotein B; counts all the cholesterol particles that can clog arteries
- **NIH**: U.S. National Institutes of Health
- **DHEA**: a hormone made by the adrenal glands that the body turns into sex hormones
- **TRT**: testosterone replacement therapy

## Why This Matters
This lecture explains how food is turned into the body's working parts. Bacteria in the mouth turn nitrate from food into the building block for nitric oxide (which widens blood vessels). Bacteria in the colon (the large bowel) turn fiber into butyrate and other helpful products (postbiotics). And the state of your nervous system while you eat decides how much of the meal you actually take in. Most claims here rest on how things work plus studies that observe people, and Lugavere openly says the field is "still in the dark ages."

## The Core Frame — A Co-Evolved Universe of Microbes
- The **microbiome** = the bacteria, viruses and fungi that live on and in us (the fungal part is the **mycobiome**). Microbes live all over the body, including places once thought to be germ-free (breast tissue, the pancreas). ✅
- Three microbiomes you can influence: **mouth, gut and skin**. For the health of the whole body he focuses on the mouth and gut; his rule for skin is: don't put on your skin what you wouldn't think of swallowing. ✅
- **Genes:** after the Human Genome Project, our own set of genes looked basic; the genes of the microbes we carry outnumber ours roughly **10 to 100 to 1**, so we hand jobs to them (turning nitrate into nitrite, fermenting fiber). ✅ As stated by Lugavere. 🟢 Evidence check: the first catalogue of gut genes found about 150 times more microbial genes than human genes (Qin 2010), so his range is reasonable; by number of cells, though, bacteria and human cells are roughly 1:1, not the old 10:1 (Sender 2016).
- The core rule: **anything you eat or take in changes your microbiome**, and we cannot yet say whether each change is good or bad. So: choose whole foods, and be careful with man-made compounds outside the natural structure of food (the food matrix). ✅

## Why the Colon Is Where It Happens

| Segment | Lecture description | Microbial role |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Stomach | Volatile, hydrochloric acid, unfriendly | Few bacteria |
| Small intestine | Active absorption of proteins, sugars, fats; "where the host gets fed" | Not the main microbial site |
| Large intestine (colon) | "The Miami of the GI tract" — mild, bacteria-friendly | Bulk of the gut microbiome |

(GI tract means the gastrointestinal tract, the whole food passage from mouth to bottom.)

- The inside of the gut (the lumen) is technically *outside* the body until food is absorbed, and laid flat it would cover roughly the floor of a room, far more than the skin (🟢 measured at about 32 m², roughly half a badminton court, not the tennis court of older textbooks; Helander 2014). So the gut is the **largest surface where we meet the outside world**, and the immune system concentrates its watch there. ✅
- Lugavere's picture: the gut microbiome is a **boot camp or training camp for the immune system**. Autoimmune diseases (where the immune system attacks the body) and allergies are described as possibly rooted in an unbalanced gut (dysbiosis), plus genes that make people prone to them. 🟡 Put as "thought to be," not established.
- What we take from it: vitamins and **short-chain fatty acids such as butyrate**, which calm inflammation. ✅

## The Oral Microbiome — The Nitrate-to-Nitric-Oxide Link
- Leafy greens (arugula, kale) and beets are rich in **nitrate**. Humans **lack the enzymes to turn nitrate into nitrite**; bacteria in the mouth do it, and nitrite feeds the **nitric oxide pathway**. ✅
- **Antiseptic (germ-killing) mouthwash:** frequent users show a higher risk of high blood pressure (one study: **double**) and about **50% higher type 2 diabetes risk** (nitric oxide also plays a part in how insulin works). 🟡 Lugavere says plainly that few studies exist and "more research needs to be done"; these are links seen in groups of people, not proof of cause. Studies found: in a group of overweight adults in Puerto Rico, using mouthwash twice a day or more was linked to 85% higher risk of high blood pressure (Joshipura 2020) and 55% higher risk of pre-diabetes or diabetes (Joshipura 2017).
- His own practice: no antiseptic mouthwash unless there is a risk of infection (he says no to Listerine at the dentist), and he avoids fluoride toothpaste after talking with an expert on nitric oxide. 🟡 A personal choice and reasoning from one conversation with an expert, not advice backed by data in the lecture. Evidence check: fluoride toothpaste clearly reduces tooth decay in randomized trials (Walsh 2019); dropping it trades a proven benefit for a theoretical one. For healthy teeth he stresses: avoid sticky foods that bacteria ferment easily and that cause decay (cariogenic foods), floss regularly, then brush.
- Artificial sweeteners: some evidence that they change the gut bacteria, though whether for better or worse is unknown; an effect in the mouth is believable, but "the jury is still out." 🟡 (Saccharin and other sweeteners changed gut bacteria and made mice handle sugar worse, with similar changes in some healthy volunteers; Suez 2014.)

## Gut Playbook — Feed, Protect, Pace
- **Many different plant fibers:** "what you feed, you breed." A big kale salad every day favours the bacteria that love kale; think of a city that does well with "a plurality of voices." ✅
- **Fermented foods:** fermented dairy (like yogurt and kefir), raw sauerkraut, pickles, kimchi, natto (fermented soybeans), even dark chocolate. They seem to increase the variety of gut bacteria and help people tolerate fiber. ✅ (In a 10-week randomized trial, a diet high in fermented foods increased gut bacteria variety and lowered signs of inflammation, while a high-fiber diet did not increase variety; Wastyk 2021.)
- **Avoid unless medically needed:** broad-spectrum antibiotics (which kill many kinds of bacteria at once). He says doctors sometimes prescribe them because patients expect a drug. ✅
- **Buy organic when you eat the peel or skin** (apples, greens, berries) to cut exposure to weed killers and pesticides; he clearly says he is not strict about organic and eats non-organic berries at restaurants. Glyphosate (a common weed killer) may affect pathways in gut bacteria; the long-term effect is unknown. 🟡
- **Man-made emulsifiers** (additives that keep oil and water mixed): polysorbate 80 and carboxymethylcellulose, found in nut milks and ice creams. Early research, mostly in animals, suggests they inflame the bowel. 🟡 "The dose makes the poison." A small randomized feeding trial in healthy people found carboxymethylcellulose reduced the variety of gut bacteria and the short-chain fatty acids they make (Chassaing 2022).
- **Increase fiber slowly.** Bloating and gas after a sudden jump mean the bacteria have not been built up yet, not that fiber is the enemy. ✅

## The Fiber Map

| Type | What it does | Lecture examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Soluble | Fermented by gut bacteria into butyrate, vitamins, postbiotics (e.g., urolithin A from pomegranate polyphenols) | Oats, psyllium husk, chia, legumes |
| Insoluble | "Roughage": adds bulk, keeps things moving, helps slough dead epithelial cells; harder to digest | Brown rice, grain fiber |
| Resistant starch | Starch that escapes small-intestine digestion and is fermented in the colon; not listed as fiber on labels | Green bananas, unripe mango, cooked-and-cooled potato, rice, pasta |
| Viscous (gel-forming) | A second axis of classification; improves blood sugar and lowers LDL | Psyllium husk, oats |

(Soluble fiber dissolves in water; insoluble fiber does not. Epithelial cells line the gut. LDL is the so-called "bad" cholesterol.)

- Foods contain a mix of soluble and insoluble fiber, so sorting foods into one group is "a bit moot." ✅
- **The cook-and-cool trick:** cooking a potato makes its starch digestible; cooling it to room temperature or in the fridge creates **retrograded resistant starch** (starch that has re-formed so it resists digestion), which can lower the rise in blood sugar and feed gut bacteria. White potato is the best source; it works less well in sweet potato; it also works for rice (he has no concern about sushi rice) and pasta. ✅ (Mechanism as described; how much it lowers the blood-sugar rise is not measured in the lecture.)
- Too much resistant starch too fast causes bloating and gas ("a feeding frenzy"), so build up slowly. ✅

## Viscous Fiber and Cholesterol — The Bile Acid Mechanism
- Bile acids are **made from cholesterol** in the liver, stored in the gallbladder, released at the top of the small intestine to break up fat, and normally **taken back** near the end of the small intestine. ✅
- Psyllium and oats **trap bile acids**, so they leave the body; the liver then **pulls cholesterol out of the blood** to make new ones. ✅
- The number given: up to **~20% less total cholesterol** with regular psyllium at **about 10 g a day** (he admits it is a big daily dose). 🟡 The lecture's figure, "up to," for total cholesterol (not ApoB). Evidence check: a study pooling 28 trials (median about 10 g a day) found LDL cholesterol fell by about 0.33 mmol/L (about 13 mg/dL, roughly 10% at typical levels), with non-HDL cholesterol and apoB (a count of artery-clogging particles) also lower (Jovanovski 2018). So 20% is the top of the range, not the usual effect.
- **Oat beta-glucan** (the gel-forming fiber in oats) also adjusts the immune system; the cholesterol claim on oat packaging is "legit." ✅ (Pooled studies: oat beta-glucan lowers LDL, non-HDL cholesterol and apoB; Ho 2016.)
- Lugavere's caveat: he is not saying LDL is bad or that everyone should push it as low as possible.

## Digestion Starts When You Sit Down
- **The cephalic phase** (the "head" phase of digestion): seeing, smelling and looking forward to food sets off digestive juices; **about 20% of all stomach acid** is released before food reaches the stomach. ✅
- Eating on the go is blamed for bloating, diarrhoea and taking in fewer nutrients. The fix: a few deep breaths (through the nose), looking at and smelling the food, and switching on the calm **"rest and digest"** side of the nervous system (parasympathetic). The "fight or flight" side "expects a threat," not a meal. ✅ (Mechanism consistent with the lecture; no studies cited.)
- **Chew slowly:** sulforaphane is made when raw cabbage-family vegetables are chewed and their cell walls are broken; allicin forms when raw garlic is chewed; eating slowly also gives the brain time to notice fullness. ✅
- Lugavere's personal aid: **betaine HCl** (an acid supplement) or digestive enzymes, especially when eating on the go or for people taking acid-blocking medicines. 🟡 "Not a ton of evidence"; his own experience.

## Lugavere's Supplement Notes

| Supplement | What he says | Flag |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Magnesium | 50–60% of people don't get enough; best foods are dark leafy greens, almonds, dark chocolate; blood tests are poor because most is in bone; he takes **glycinate** daily; helps migraine prevention; helps activate vitamin D, so he'd try it before vitamin D if D is low (NIH ODS: 48% of Americans eat less than the average requirement, and 50–60% of body magnesium is in bone) | 🟡 personal choice |
| Vitamin D | A hormone regulating about **5% of the genome**; evidence for calming an overactive immune system (autoimmunity); **2,000–5,000 IU/day** a good starting place | 🟡 "hard to get too much" is his claim. Evidence check: the adult upper limit is 4,000 IU/day (NIH ODS), and the Endocrine Society advises against routine supplementation above the recommended intake for healthy adults under 75 (Demay 2024). The genome share is usually put at about 3% (Bouillon 2008). In the VITAL trial, 2,000 IU/day lowered new autoimmune disease by about 22% (Hahn 2022). |
| Fish oil | Most useful if you don't eat fish; evidence centers on fish oil over krill or algae; buy from a reputable maker, look for **IFOS** certification because the fats oxidize easily | 🟡 |
| Creatine monohydrate | Safe and cheap; pulls water into muscle; helps lifting; emerging brain and mental health evidence; check with a physician if you have a medical issue | ✅ best-evidenced form per lecture |
| Whey protein | Drinks one or two shakes a day; satiating; incremental protein increases shift body composition | 🟡 personal practice |

(IU means international units, a way of measuring vitamin D. IFOS is an independent testing programme for fish oil purity and freshness. Creatine is a natural compound that helps muscles make quick energy.)

He says again that he is "not a big believer in one-size-fits-all."

- **The large intestine is "the Miami of the GI tract."**
- **The gut microbiome = boot camp for your immune system.**
- **Human genes are outnumbered 10 to 100 to 1 by the genes of our microbes** (the lecture's claim).
- **Humans cannot turn nitrate into nitrite; bacteria in the mouth do it.** Wipe them out and the salad does you less good.

## Key Corrections to Carry Forward
- 🔴 "Bacteria are bad": the "germs cause disease" reflex is out of date for the microbiome; many microbes are partners that help us.
- 🔴 "Digestion starts with chewing": it starts in the head phase, when you sit down, see and smell the food.
- 🔴 "Bloating from fiber means fiber is the problem": it means the gut bacteria have not been built up yet; increase slowly.
- 🔴 "Fiber is fiber": soluble, insoluble, resistant starch and gel-forming fibers do different jobs.
- 🔴 "Cooking potatoes only makes them raise blood sugar more": cooling after cooking turns part of the starch back into resistant starch.
- 🔴 "More antiseptic mouthwash is better hygiene": frequent use is linked to higher risk of high blood pressure and diabetes (observational, 🟡), and may weaken the benefits of nitrate.

## Practical Takeaway
According to Lugavere: rotate many different plant fibers, include fermented foods, add fiber slowly, use cooked-and-cooled starches, and choose whole foods over additives. Sit down, breathe, and chew slowly at meals. Treat mouthwash, antibiotics and sweeteners as decisions to think about, not habits. Supplements are optional and personal; this note records his choices, not a prescription.

*Why C: the bacteria in the mouth carry the enzymes needed; the lecture links less nitric oxide to blood pressure and to how insulin works, while noting the human data are limited and only show links.*

**2. Psyllium husk and oats lower circulating cholesterol in Lugavere's explanation primarily because:**
A. \* They bind bile acids so they are excreted, prompting the liver to draw cholesterol from circulation to make new bile acids

*Why A: bile acids are made from cholesterol and are normally recycled; trapping them forces the liver to make new ones. The lecture's number (up to ~20% total cholesterol at ~10 g a day) refers to total cholesterol, not ApoB.*

*Why B: Lugavere says bloating from a sudden increase is "not meant to happen" and shows the gut bacteria have not been built up yet, so increasing slowly until it feels comfortable is the fix.*
