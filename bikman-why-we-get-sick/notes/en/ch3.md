
# Bikman — Why We Get Sick, Ch.3: Insulin Resistance — Beyond the Basics
*This lecture explains why insulin resistance is clinically under-detected, traces the tissue-by-tissue cascade from insulin resistance to type 2 diabetes, and challenges the standard LDL-centric model of heart disease and hypertension.*

*Source: Dr. Benjamin Bikman, Peterson Academy course "Why We Get Sick," Lecture 3 (transcript). Continuation of Ch.1–2 — this chapter moves from "what insulin resistance is" into "what diseases it actually causes," starting with type 2 diabetes and heart disease.*

## Why This Matters

Ch.1–2 built the mechanism and the threshold concept. This chapter answers **why we keep missing insulin resistance clinically** (we measure the wrong thing, historically and technically), then walks through exactly how insulin resistance becomes type 2 diabetes tissue by tissue, and mounts a detailed, evidence-based challenge to the standard LDL-causes-heart-disease model.

## Scope-Setting Claim — Why We Keep Missing It

- **Historical reason:** "Diabetes" literally means "to flow through" — named for excess urination (polyuria), and "mellitus" (honey-sweet) was added much later, in the 17th century, by Thomas Willis to describe the sweet taste of the urine (Karamanou 2016); ancient physicians had already noticed the sugar-laden urine. The disease has been defined by its *glucose* symptom since antiquity.
- **Scientific/technical reason:** glucose is trivially easy to measure (a drop of blood, a cheap meter, now continuous patches). Insulin is not — it took **Rosalyn Yalow's Nobel Prize-winning radioimmunoassay work** (with Solomon Berson, 1960; Nobel 1977) to make insulin measurement possible at all (Yalow 1960), and it still is not routine in standard clinical care.
- **The consequence, restated from Ch.1:** insulin can be elevated *decades* before glucose ever moves — but because we only historically look for and can easily measure glucose, insulin resistance goes undetected until it's already progressed to full diabetes.

## Punchlines & Quotable Facts (Lecture 3)

- **"There is no type 2 diabetes without insulin resistance."** — restated here even more forcefully as the foundation of the entire chapter.
- **"Type 1 and type 2 diabetes... they should not be in the same family."** — one is a disease of insulin excess, the other a disease of insulin deficiency; they only share the glucose symptom. 🔴 Framing; type 2 diabetes involves both insulin resistance and failing insulin secretion (Kahn 2003).
- **"Double diabetes"** — a real, growing phenomenon: type 1 diabetics who develop type 2-style insulin resistance from their own injected insulin, because "eat whatever, just cover it with insulin" advice drives chronically high injected-insulin doses. 🟡 The term is used in the literature (Kietsiriroje 2019); weight gain, family history and lifestyle contribute, and the share due to injected insulin itself is debated.
- **"Why is a fire truck at a burning house?"** — Bikman's central metaphor for questioning whether LDL causes atherosclerosis or is simply present responding to it (possible infection).
- **People with the lowest LDL had the highest risk of Alzheimer's disease, in the Shanghai data cited.** — stated as a direct inversion of the "lower cholesterol is always better" assumption. 🟡 Unverified — the specific Shanghai cohort could not be identified.
- **The Sydney Diet Heart Study and the Minnesota Coronary Experiment were both withheld from publication for decades** because replacing saturated fat with seed oils *increased* all-cause mortality — discovered only when researchers dug through literal boxes in a scientist's basement. 🟡 Evidence check: both trials were incompletely published; deliberate suppression is not proven. Sydney found higher all-cause mortality in the safflower-oil group (Ramsden 2013); Minnesota found no mortality benefit (Ramsden 2016) — see details below.
- **"You can eat as much salt as you want" — if you are insulin sensitive.** The national salt-quartile study found zero mortality effect in normal-weight (insulin sensitive) subjects, but a strong effect in overweight (insulin resistant) subjects at the identical salt intake. 🟢 Matches the NHANES I follow-up study (He 1999); observational.
- **Restricting salt can make a person MORE insulin resistant** — the reverse of the intuitive assumption that low salt is unconditionally protective. 🟡 A 7-day low-salt diet modestly raised HOMA-IR in healthy adults (Garg 2011); long-term effects are unknown.

## The Tissue Cascade — How Insulin Resistance Becomes Type 2 Diabetes

**Muscle (the biggest glucose consumer):**

- Skeletal muscle makes up roughly 40% of body mass and takes up **about 80%** of glucose under insulin-clamp conditions (a smaller share after an ordinary meal, when the liver also takes up much of it), via insulin opening GLUT4 doors (DeFronzo 2009).
- When muscle becomes insulin resistant, this is the single biggest loss of glucose-clearing capacity in the body — glucose has nowhere to go and accumulates in blood.

**Liver:**

- The liver runs two insulin-driven programs: **lipogenesis** (making new fat) and **glycogenesis** (storing glucose as glycogen for later)
- In a resistant liver, **lipogenesis stays fully intact** while **glycogenesis breaks** — insulin cannot tell the liver to store glucose anymore
- Worse: rather than just failing to store, the liver actively **breaks down existing glycogen and dumps glucose into the blood** — the opposite of what insulin is trying to signal. 🟢 This "selective" hepatic insulin resistance (fat-making stays on, glucose output is not switched off) is well described (Brown 2008); 🟡 failure to suppress glucose production overall, more than glycogen breakdown alone, is the main problem

**Alpha cells (pancreas, glucagon-producing — not the insulin-making beta cells):**

- Insulin normally suppresses the alpha cells' glucagon production (glucagon does the opposite of insulin — raises glucose)
- When alpha cells become insulin resistant, this suppression fails — glucagon rises unchecked and directly tells the liver to release even more glucose. 🟢 Strong evidence (DeFronzo 2009)
- **Net effect of all three dominoes falling:** uncontrolled hyperglycemia — full type 2 diabetes

## Heart Disease — A Serious Challenge to the Standard LDL Model

**Framing note:** Bikman is explicit that the precise mechanism causing arterial plaque is genuinely **theoretical, not settled** — this section presents his case against the conventional LDL-centric model using primary trial and cohort data, not just opinion. Treat this the way you'd treat any single scientist's synthesis: compelling data, but a minority position relative to mainstream cardiology guidance.

**Does LDL/cholesterol predict mortality? Four cited studies say largely no:**

- UCLA-led registry, ~137,000 hospitalizations for coronary disease: almost half had LDL under 100 mg/dL on admission, while 55% had low HDL (under 40 mg/dL) (Sachdeva 2009) — LDL at admission did not flag these patients. 🟡 A one-time snapshot at admission, not a study of LDL predicting risk over time
- Population study: lowest death rate occurred at LDL ~140 mg/dL — about 40% *above* the conventional "optimal" cutoff; as populations pushed LDL down further, death rate rose. For every 1 mmol/L (~30 mg/dL) drop in cholesterol, mortality risk rose ~15% in this data. 🟢 A Danish cohort of ~108,000 people found the lowest all-cause mortality at LDL ~140 mg/dL, with a U-shaped curve (Johannesen 2020); 🟡 the 15% figure could not be verified, and low cholesterol in such studies partly reflects illness and frailty (reverse causation).
- Shanghai cohort: lowest LDL associated with **highest** Alzheimer's risk
- **These are observational/correlational studies**, not randomized trials — Bikman himself flags this limitation. Both bodies of evidence exist; they are not fully reconciled here. ✅ Evidence check: randomized statin and PCSK9-inhibitor trials and Mendelian randomization studies show that lowering LDL reduces heart attacks and strokes in proportion to the LDL drop (Ference 2017).

**LDL pattern A vs. pattern B:**

- Pattern A: larger, more buoyant, less dense ("beach ball") — relatively benign
- Pattern B: smaller, denser ("golf ball") — penetrates the arterial wall more readily — confirmed pattern B raises heart attack risk **>3x** in cited data (🟢 Austin 1988)
- Insulin resistance directly shifts LDL toward pattern B, while also raising triglycerides and lowering HDL.
- **Reversibility, stated directly:** dietary changes that resolve insulin resistance have been shown to shift LDL pattern from B back to A — without necessarily changing total LDL-C. 🟢 Cutting carbohydrate reduced small, dense LDL even apart from weight loss (Krauss 2006).

**Saturated fat vs. seed oils — primary trial evidence, not just opinion:**

- Journal of the American College of Cardiology review (cardiologists themselves) found no clear benefit to reducing saturated fat, and noted saturated fat tends to shift LDL toward the benign pattern A (Astrup 2020). 🟡 Evidence check: a Cochrane review of 15 RCTs found that cutting saturated fat lowered combined cardiovascular events by 17%, with little or no effect on deaths (Hooper 2020).
- **Sydney Diet Heart Study** and the **Minnesota Coronary Experiment** (both 1960s randomized trials, both suppressed from publication for decades, recovered and reanalyzed within roughly the last 10 years): replacing saturated fat with polyunsaturated seed oils **lowered LDL but increased all-cause mortality** — 22% higher death risk per 30 mg/dL drop in serum (total) cholesterol in the Minnesota trial. These are real RCTs, not observational data — the strongest evidence tier in this chapter, and genuinely uncomfortable for the simple "lower LDL = better" model. 🟡 Evidence check: Sydney (1966–73) found higher all-cause mortality in the safflower-oil group (Ramsden 2013). Minnesota (1968–73) found no mortality benefit in the randomized comparison; the 22% figure is an association within the trial, not a randomized result, and only about a quarter of participants stayed on the diets for a year or more (Ramsden 2016). Larger meta-analyses find that cutting saturated fat modestly lowers cardiovascular events (Hooper 2020).
- **Mechanistic explanation offered:** refined seed oils (polyunsaturated, industrially processed, prone to rancidity/peroxidation) may make macrophages react differently to the LDL carrying them — the "rotten cargo" turning a normal macrophage into a foam cell, vs. a normal/benign interaction with saturated-fat-carrying LDL. Framed by Bikman as a plausible mechanism, not yet fully proven — flagged as interpretation. 🔴

**The infection/LDL-as-first-responder hypothesis:**

- Atherosclerotic plaques commonly contain infectious bacteria — a real, established finding. 🟡 Bacterial DNA is often found in plaques; whether it causes disease is unproven
- LDL has a documented ability to bind and neutralize bacteria/pathogens, clearing them via the liver — a genuine immune function, separate from its fat-transport role
- **Periodontal disease connection:** daily brushing/flossing (not mouthwash) associated with ~50% lower heart disease risk in a 2023 paper cited — consistent with an oral-infection-to-artery pathway. 🟡 The 2023 paper and the ~50% figure could not be verified; the AHA concludes that periodontal disease is associated with atherosclerotic disease independent of known confounders, but causation is unproven (Lockhart 2012)
- Low LDL is associated with higher infection/sepsis mortality and a **15x** higher cancer death risk in cited data — presented as suggestive that LDL's immune role may be underappreciated, not as a settled causal claim. 🔴 The 15x figure is unverified; low cholesterol before death from cancer or infection is widely attributed to the illness itself (reverse causation)
- **The "fire truck" reframe:** LDL's presence at a plaque may reflect it *responding* to infection/damage rather than *causing* the plaque — an alternative hypothesis, explicitly labeled theoretical by Bikman himself

## Hypertension — Three Distinct Insulin-Driven Mechanisms

**1. Sympathetic nervous system activation**

- Elevated insulin pushes the body toward fight-or-flight (sympathetic) dominance over rest-and-digest (parasympathetic) — narrowing blood vessels and increasing heart contraction force, both raising blood pressure. 🟢 Strong evidence for sympathetic activation in obesity-related hypertension (Hall 2015).

**2. Nitric oxide production (endothelial function)**

- In an insulin-sensitive vessel, insulin stimulates nitric oxide production → vessel relaxation → lower pressure (same mechanism nitroglycerin exploits during a heart attack)
- In an insulin-resistant vessel, this signal fails — less nitric oxide, narrower vessels, higher pressure. 🟢 Strong evidence (Muniyappa 2013)

**3. Kidney sodium handling**

- Normally kidneys regulate salt/water balance independently based on need
- Elevated insulin **forces the kidney to retain more sodium** (and the water that follows it) regardless of whether the body actually needs it — raising blood volume and pressure. 🟢 Strong evidence (DeFronzo 1975)

**The salt paradox study — genuinely counterintuitive, directly relevant:**

- National survey, salt intake in quartiles (up to 4x difference between lowest and highest groups)
- **Normal-weight (largely insulin-sensitive) subjects:** zero effect of salt intake on stroke, cardiovascular mortality, or all-cause mortality across all quartiles
- **Overweight (largely insulin-resistant) subjects:** same salt quartiles, but now every outcome correlated with mortality. This matches the NHANES I follow-up (19 years): higher sodium predicted stroke and cardiovascular death in overweight adults only (He 1999). 🟡 Observational, based on a single 24-hour diet recall
- **The paradox:** restricting salt intake can itself raise insulin (the body "panics" trying to conserve sodium via insulin's normal salt-retention role), meaning aggressive salt restriction in an insulin-sensitive person could theoretically worsen insulin resistance rather than help. Stated as an emerging finding, not yet a settled clinical recommendation — does not override standard sodium guidance for anyone with existing hypertension or kidney disease. 🟡 A 7-day low-salt diet modestly raised insulin resistance in healthy adults (Garg 2011). ✅ Cutting salt lowers blood pressure, and in a large RCT a salt substitute reduced stroke and death (Neal 2021).

## Key Corrections to Carry Forward

- Assuming type 1 and type 2 diabetes are variations of the same disease: they are near-opposite mechanisms (insulin deficiency vs. insulin excess/resistance) sharing only the glucose symptom.
- Assuming LDL causing atherosclerosis is settled science: it remains genuinely theoretical at the plaque-formation-mechanism level, per Bikman (✅ evidence check: randomized trials and genetic studies establish LDL as a cause of atherosclerotic disease, so this is a minority position — Ference 2017).
- Assuming salt restriction is universally protective: its cardiovascular effect appears to depend heavily on the person's insulin sensitivity status, not a fixed dose-response for everyone.
- Assuming saturated fat is worse than seed oils based on LDL-C alone: two suppressed 1960s RCTs found the opposite for all-cause mortality when seed oils replaced saturated fat, despite LDL dropping. 🟡 Only Sydney showed higher mortality; Minnesota showed no benefit (Ramsden 2013; Ramsden 2016), and pooled trials favor a modest benefit of cutting saturated fat (Hooper 2020).

## Practical Takeaway

## Questions to Learn From (Socratic) — with Answer Options

**1. Given the salt paradox study (no effect in insulin-sensitive people, strong effect in insulin-resistant people at the same intake) — what does this imply about applying generic sodium guidelines to any one individual?**

A. Sodium guidelines should be identical for everyone regardless of metabolic status
B. *The cardiovascular impact of a given salt intake likely depends on the person's underlying insulin sensitivity — meaning improving insulin sensitivity itself may matter more for salt-related risk than salt intake alone, though this does not override existing hypertension/kidney-specific sodium guidance
C. Salt should be eliminated entirely from everyone's diet as a precaution

*Why B: this is a nuance, not a license to ignore standard advice — particularly for anyone with diagnosed hypertension or kidney disease, where sodium restriction has its own established evidence base outside this specific insulin-resistance framing.*

**2. Given that insulin normally suppresses alpha-cell glucagon production, and this suppression fails with insulin resistance — why cannot the body just compensate by making even more insulin to re-suppress glucagon, the way it compensates elsewhere?**

A. It can, indefinitely, which is why type 2 diabetes never actually progresses to glucagon-driven hyperglycemia
B. *Once alpha cells themselves become insulin resistant (not just muscle or liver), no amount of additional insulin restores the suppression — the resistance is at the alpha cell's own signaling level, not a dose problem, which is why this domino falling marks a more advanced, harder-to-reverse stage than fat-tissue-only resistance
C. Glucagon does not actually respond to insulin at all, so this compensation was never possible

*Why B: this distinguishes a compensable problem (not enough insulin reaching a still-sensitive tissue) from a structural one (the tissue itself no longer responds regardless of insulin dose).*
