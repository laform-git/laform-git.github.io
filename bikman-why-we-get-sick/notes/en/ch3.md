
# Bikman — Why We Get Sick, Ch.3: Insulin Resistance — Beyond the Basics
*This lecture explains why insulin resistance is clinically under-detected, traces the tissue-by-tissue cascade from insulin resistance to type 2 diabetes, and challenges the standard LDL-centric model of heart disease and hypertension.*

*Source: Dr. Benjamin Bikman, Peterson Academy course "Why We Get Sick," Lecture 3 (transcript). Continuation of Ch.1–2 — this chapter moves from "what insulin resistance is" into "what diseases it actually causes," starting with type 2 diabetes and heart disease.*

**In plain words:** doctors usually test blood sugar, but insulin rises first, often years earlier, so insulin resistance is missed until it has become type 2 diabetes. This chapter shows how muscle, liver and pancreas become resistant one after another, how high insulin raises blood pressure in three ways, and why salt seems to matter more for people who are insulin resistant. It also presents Bikman's challenge to the standard view that LDL cholesterol causes heart disease, a minority view, with the mainstream evidence marked next to each claim.

## Why This Matters

Ch.1–2 built the mechanism and the idea of the fat threshold. This chapter answers **why doctors keep missing insulin resistance** (we measure the wrong thing, for historical and technical reasons). It then walks through exactly how insulin resistance becomes type 2 diabetes, one tissue at a time. Finally, it makes a detailed, evidence-based challenge to the standard model that LDL ("bad" cholesterol) causes heart disease.

## Scope-Setting Claim — Why We Keep Missing It

- **The historical reason:** "Diabetes" literally means "to flow through." It was named for passing too much urine (polyuria). "Mellitus" (honey-sweet) was added much later, in the 17th century, by Thomas Willis to describe the sweet taste of the urine (Karamanou 2016); ancient doctors had already noticed the sugar in it. Since ancient times the disease has been defined by its *sugar* symptom.
- **The scientific and technical reason:** blood sugar is very easy to measure (a drop of blood, a cheap meter, now sensor patches worn all day). Insulin is not. It took **Rosalyn Yalow's Nobel Prize-winning work on radioimmunoassay** (a lab method for measuring tiny amounts of a hormone), done with Solomon Berson in 1960 (Nobel 1977), to make measuring insulin possible at all (Yalow 1960). It is still not part of routine medical care.
- **The result, repeated from Ch.1:** insulin can be high *decades* before blood sugar ever moves. But because, historically, we only look for blood sugar and can easily measure it, insulin resistance goes unnoticed until it has already become full diabetes.

## Punchlines & Quotable Facts (Lecture 3)

- **"There is no type 2 diabetes without insulin resistance."** Repeated here even more strongly, as the foundation of the whole chapter.
- **"Type 1 and type 2 diabetes... they should not be in the same family."** One is a disease of too much insulin, the other a disease of too little insulin; they only share the high blood sugar. 🔴 A way of framing it; type 2 diabetes involves both insulin resistance and falling insulin production (Kahn 2003).
- **"Double diabetes":** a real and growing problem. People with type 1 diabetes develop type 2-style insulin resistance from their own injected insulin, because the advice "eat whatever you want, just cover it with insulin" leads to high injected doses all the time. 🟡 The term is used in medical writing (Kietsiriroje 2019); weight gain, family history and lifestyle also play a part, and how much is due to the injected insulin itself is debated.
- **"Why is a fire truck at a burning house?"** Bikman's central picture for asking whether LDL causes hardened, clogged arteries (atherosclerosis), or is simply there responding to the problem (possibly an infection).
- **In the Shanghai data cited, people with the lowest LDL had the highest risk of Alzheimer's disease.** Presented as a direct reversal of the idea that "lower cholesterol is always better." 🟡 Not verified; the specific Shanghai study could not be identified.
- **The Sydney Diet Heart Study and the Minnesota Coronary Experiment were both left unpublished for decades,** because replacing saturated fat with seed oils *increased* deaths from all causes. This came to light only when researchers dug through actual boxes in a scientist's basement. 🟡 Evidence check: both trials were only partly published; deliberate hiding is not proven. Sydney found more deaths from all causes in the safflower-oil group (Ramsden 2013); Minnesota found no benefit in deaths (Ramsden 2016). See the details below.
- **"You can eat as much salt as you want," if you are insulin sensitive.** The national study that split people into four salt groups found no effect on deaths in normal-weight (insulin-sensitive) people, but a strong effect in overweight (insulin-resistant) people eating the same amount of salt. 🟢 This matches the NHANES I follow-up study (He 1999); an observational study (one that watches people rather than testing a change).
- **Cutting salt can make a person MORE insulin resistant,** the reverse of the common belief that low salt always protects. 🟡 A 7-day low-salt diet slightly raised HOMA-IR (a score of insulin resistance) in healthy adults (Garg 2011); long-term effects are unknown.

## The Tissue Cascade — How Insulin Resistance Becomes Type 2 Diabetes

**Muscle (the biggest user of blood sugar):**

- Skeletal muscle makes up roughly 40% of body weight and takes up **about 80%** of blood sugar under insulin-clamp test conditions (a smaller share after an ordinary meal, when the liver also takes up much of it). It does this through insulin opening GLUT4 "doors" in the cell (DeFronzo 2009).
- When muscle becomes insulin resistant, the body loses its biggest single way of clearing sugar. The sugar has nowhere to go and builds up in the blood.

**Liver:**

- The liver runs two programs driven by insulin: **lipogenesis** (making new fat) and **glycogenesis** (storing sugar as glycogen, a starch-like reserve, for later).
- In a resistant liver, **fat-making keeps working fully** while **sugar storage breaks**. Insulin can no longer tell the liver to store sugar.
- Worse: rather than just failing to store it, the liver actively **breaks down its stored glycogen and dumps sugar into the blood**, the opposite of what insulin is telling it. 🟢 This "selective" insulin resistance of the liver (fat-making stays on, sugar output is not switched off) is well described (Brown 2008); 🟡 the main problem is the failure to hold back sugar production in general, more than glycogen breakdown alone.

**Alpha cells (in the pancreas; they make glucagon, not insulin, which is made by the beta cells):**

- Insulin normally holds back the alpha cells' production of glucagon (glucagon does the opposite of insulin: it raises blood sugar).
- When alpha cells become insulin resistant, this brake fails. Glucagon rises unchecked and directly tells the liver to release even more sugar. 🟢 Strong evidence (DeFronzo 2009).
- **When all three dominoes fall:** blood sugar runs out of control, which is full type 2 diabetes.

## Heart Disease — A Serious Challenge to the Standard LDL Model

**A note on framing:** Bikman says clearly that the exact cause of plaque (the fatty build-up in artery walls) is truly **theoretical, not settled.** In this section he makes his case against the standard LDL-centered model using data from original trials and long-term studies, not just opinion. Treat it as you would any single scientist's summary: interesting data, but a minority view compared with mainstream heart-medicine advice.

**Does LDL or cholesterol predict death? Four studies he cites say mostly no:**

- A UCLA-led registry of about 137,000 hospital stays for heart artery disease: almost half the patients had LDL under 100 mg/dL when admitted, while 55% had low HDL (under 40 mg/dL) (Sachdeva 2009). LDL at admission did not pick these patients out. 🟡 A one-time snapshot at admission, not a study of LDL predicting risk over time.
- A population study: the lowest death rate came at an LDL of about 140 mg/dL, about 40% *above* the usual "ideal" cutoff; as LDL went lower still, the death rate rose. For every 1 mmol/L (about 30 mg/dL) drop in cholesterol, the risk of death rose about 15% in these data. 🟢 A Danish study of about 108,000 people found the lowest death rate from all causes at an LDL of about 140 mg/dL, with a U-shaped curve (Johannesen 2020); 🟡 the 15% figure could not be verified, and low cholesterol in such studies partly reflects illness and frailty (reverse causation, where the illness lowers the cholesterol rather than the other way round).
- The Shanghai study: the lowest LDL went with the **highest** risk of Alzheimer's.
- **These are observational studies, showing links, not randomized trials** (where people are assigned by chance to a treatment or not). Bikman himself points out this weakness. Both bodies of evidence exist, and they are not fully reconciled here. ✅ Evidence check: randomized trials of statins and PCSK9 inhibitors (cholesterol-lowering medicines), and Mendelian randomization studies (which use natural genetic differences as a kind of experiment), show that lowering LDL reduces heart attacks and strokes in proportion to how much LDL drops (Ference 2017).

**LDL pattern A vs. pattern B:**

- Pattern A: larger, lighter, less dense particles ("beach balls"); relatively harmless.
- Pattern B: smaller, denser particles ("golf balls"); they get into the artery wall more easily. Confirmed pattern B raises heart attack risk **more than 3 times** in the data cited (🟢 Austin 1988).
- Insulin resistance directly shifts LDL toward pattern B, while also raising triglycerides (blood fats) and lowering HDL ("good" cholesterol).
- **It can be reversed, he says plainly:** diet changes that fix insulin resistance have been shown to shift LDL from pattern B back to A, without necessarily changing total LDL. 🟢 Cutting carbohydrate reduced small, dense LDL even apart from weight loss (Krauss 2006).

**Saturated fat vs. seed oils: original trial evidence, not just opinion**

- A review in the Journal of the American College of Cardiology (written by heart doctors themselves) found no clear benefit from cutting saturated fat, and noted that saturated fat tends to shift LDL toward the harmless pattern A (Astrup 2020). 🟡 Evidence check: a Cochrane review of 15 randomized trials found that cutting saturated fat lowered heart and blood vessel events combined by 17%, with little or no effect on deaths (Hooper 2020).
- **The Sydney Diet Heart Study** and the **Minnesota Coronary Experiment** were both randomized trials from the 1960s, both left unpublished for decades, then recovered and reanalyzed within roughly the last 10 years. Replacing saturated fat with polyunsaturated seed oils **lowered LDL but increased deaths from all causes**: 22% higher risk of death for each 30 mg/dL drop in blood (total) cholesterol in the Minnesota trial. These are real randomized trials, not observational data, the strongest kind of evidence in this chapter, and truly uncomfortable for the simple "lower LDL = better" model. 🟡 Evidence check: Sydney (1966–73) found more deaths from all causes in the safflower-oil group (Ramsden 2013). Minnesota (1968–73) found no benefit in deaths in the randomized comparison; the 22% figure is a link seen within the trial, not a randomized result, and only about a quarter of the participants stayed on the diets for a year or more (Ramsden 2016). Larger combined analyses find that cutting saturated fat slightly lowers heart and blood vessel events (Hooper 2020).
- **His proposed explanation:** refined seed oils (polyunsaturated, made in factories, prone to going rancid, or peroxidation) may change how macrophages (immune cells that clean up debris) react to the LDL carrying them. The "rotten cargo" turns a normal macrophage into a foam cell (a fat-stuffed cell that builds plaque), unlike the normal, harmless contact with LDL carrying saturated fat. Bikman presents this as a believable mechanism, not yet fully proven; flagged as interpretation. 🔴

**The idea that LDL is an infection "first responder":**

- Artery plaques often contain bacteria, a real, established finding. 🟡 Bacterial DNA is often found in plaques; whether it causes disease is unproven.
- LDL has a documented ability to latch onto and neutralize bacteria and other germs, clearing them through the liver. This is a real immune job, separate from its job of carrying fat.
- **The gum disease connection:** daily brushing and flossing (not mouthwash) was linked to about 50% lower heart disease risk in a 2023 paper he cited. This fits with infection traveling from the mouth to the arteries. 🟡 The 2023 paper and the 50% figure could not be verified; the American Heart Association (AHA) concludes that gum disease is linked to artery disease even after allowing for known shared factors, but that it is not proven to cause it (Lockhart 2012).
- Low LDL is linked to more deaths from infection and sepsis (blood poisoning), and to a **15 times** higher risk of cancer death in the data cited. He presents this as a hint that LDL's immune role may be underrated, not as a settled cause. 🔴 The 15-times figure is not verified; low cholesterol before death from cancer or infection is widely put down to the illness itself (reverse causation).
- **The "fire truck" reframing:** LDL being at a plaque may mean it is *responding* to infection or damage, not *causing* the plaque. An alternative hypothesis, which Bikman himself labels as theory.

## Hypertension — Three Distinct Insulin-Driven Mechanisms

High insulin can raise blood pressure (hypertension) in three separate ways.

**1. Switching on the "fight or flight" nervous system**

- High insulin pushes the body toward "fight or flight" (the sympathetic nervous system) and away from "rest and digest" (the parasympathetic nervous system). This narrows blood vessels and makes the heart pump harder, and both raise blood pressure. 🟢 Strong evidence that this nervous system is switched on in high blood pressure linked to obesity (Hall 2015).

**2. Nitric oxide (how the inner lining of the vessels works)**

- In a vessel that responds well to insulin, insulin makes it produce nitric oxide → the vessel relaxes → pressure falls (the same mechanism nitroglycerin uses during a heart attack).
- In an insulin-resistant vessel, this signal fails: less nitric oxide, narrower vessels, higher pressure. 🟢 Strong evidence (Muniyappa 2013).

**3. How the kidneys handle salt**

- Normally the kidneys balance salt and water on their own, according to what the body needs.
- High insulin **forces the kidneys to hold on to more sodium** (and the water that follows it), whether or not the body needs it. This raises blood volume and pressure. 🟢 Strong evidence (DeFronzo 1975).

**The salt paradox study: truly surprising and directly relevant**

- A national survey that split people into four groups by salt intake (up to 4 times more salt in the highest group than the lowest).
- **Normal-weight (mostly insulin-sensitive) people:** salt intake had no effect on stroke, heart and blood vessel deaths, or deaths from all causes, in any of the four groups.
- **Overweight (mostly insulin-resistant) people:** the same salt groups, but now every outcome went with the risk of death. This matches the NHANES I follow-up (19 years): more sodium predicted stroke and heart and blood vessel death in overweight adults only (He 1999). 🟡 Observational, based on a single 24-hour record of what people ate.
- **The paradox:** cutting salt can itself raise insulin (the body "panics" and tries to hold on to sodium through insulin's normal salt-keeping role). So strict salt restriction in an insulin-sensitive person could, in theory, worsen insulin resistance rather than help. Presented as a new finding, not yet settled medical advice, and it does not override standard sodium advice for anyone who already has high blood pressure or kidney disease. 🟡 A 7-day low-salt diet slightly raised insulin resistance in healthy adults (Garg 2011). ✅ Cutting salt lowers blood pressure, and in a large randomized trial a salt substitute reduced stroke and death (Neal 2021).

## Key Corrections to Carry Forward

- Thinking type 1 and type 2 diabetes are versions of the same disease: their mechanisms are nearly opposite (too little insulin vs. too much insulin and resistance to it); they share only the high blood sugar.
- Thinking it is settled science that LDL causes clogged arteries: according to Bikman, it is still truly theoretical at the level of how plaque forms (✅ evidence check: randomized trials and genetic studies establish LDL as a cause of artery disease, so this is a minority view; Ference 2017).
- Thinking cutting salt protects everyone: its effect on the heart and blood vessels seems to depend heavily on how insulin-sensitive the person is, not on a fixed effect of the amount for everyone.
- Thinking saturated fat is worse than seed oils because of LDL alone: two 1960s randomized trials left unpublished for years found the opposite for deaths from all causes when seed oils replaced saturated fat, even though LDL fell. 🟡 Only Sydney showed more deaths; Minnesota showed no benefit (Ramsden 2013; Ramsden 2016), and combined trials favor a small benefit from cutting saturated fat (Hooper 2020).

## Practical Takeaway

Since insulin rises long before blood sugar does, a normal blood sugar test alone does not rule out insulin resistance. And how much salt matters, and how much LDL tells you, may depend on how well the body responds to insulin.

## Questions to Learn From (Socratic) — with Answer Options

**1. Given the salt paradox study (no effect in insulin-sensitive people, a strong effect in insulin-resistant people at the same intake), what does this mean for applying general sodium advice to any one person?**

A. Sodium advice should be identical for everyone, whatever their metabolic state
B. *The effect of a given amount of salt on the heart and blood vessels probably depends on the person's underlying insulin sensitivity. So improving insulin sensitivity itself may matter more for salt-related risk than salt intake alone, though this does not override existing sodium advice for high blood pressure or kidney disease
C. Salt should be cut out of everyone's diet completely, just in case

*Why B: this is a fine point, not permission to ignore standard advice, especially for anyone diagnosed with high blood pressure or kidney disease, where cutting sodium has its own established evidence outside this insulin-resistance view.*

**2. Insulin normally holds back the alpha cells' glucagon, and this brake fails with insulin resistance. Why can the body not simply make even more insulin to hold glucagon back again, the way it makes up for resistance elsewhere?**

A. It can, forever, which is why type 2 diabetes never actually reaches high blood sugar driven by glucagon
B. *Once the alpha cells themselves become insulin resistant (not just muscle or liver), no amount of extra insulin brings the brake back. The resistance is in the alpha cell's own signaling, not a matter of dose. That is why this domino falling marks a later stage that is harder to reverse than resistance in fat tissue alone
C. Glucagon does not respond to insulin at all, so this was never possible

*Why B: this separates a problem the body can make up for (not enough insulin reaching a tissue that still responds) from a structural one (the tissue itself no longer responds, however much insulin there is).*
