
# Bikman — Why We Get Sick, Ch.5: Resisting Insulin Resistance (Solutions)
*This lecture delivers the diagnostic thresholds (fasting insulin, HOMA-IR, TG/HDL, Adipose Insulin Resistance Score) and ranks drugs, exercise, surgery, and diet by how well each actually addresses insulin resistance rather than just glucose.*

*Source: Dr. Benjamin Bikman, Peterson Academy course "Why We Get Sick," Lecture 5 (transcript). This is the "solutions" lecture referenced as an open thread across Ch.1–4 — diagnostic tests, then the four intervention levers: drugs, exercise, surgery, and diet.*

**In plain words:** this is the "what to do" chapter. It lists the tests that can show insulin resistance, from simple home checks (a tape measure, certain skin changes) to blood tests, and gives Bikman's number targets. It then ranks the diabetes drugs as he sees them, covers exercise and surgery, and argues that cutting carbohydrate is the most effective single step for people who are insulin resistant. Where large studies disagree with him, that is marked next to the claim.

**Words in this note:**
- **HOMA-IR**: a score calculated from fasting sugar and fasting insulin to estimate insulin resistance
- **TG**: triglycerides, the main fat in the blood
- **HDL**: high-density lipoprotein, the "good" cholesterol carrier
- **RCT**: randomized controlled trial, the strongest kind of study for testing a treatment
- **TZD**: thiazolidinediones, a class of diabetes drugs
- **OGTT**: oral glucose tolerance test: you drink sugar and blood sugar is checked over two hours
- **ADA**: American Diabetes Association
- **PCOS**: polycystic ovary syndrome, a hormone condition in women
- **GLP-1**: a gut hormone that lowers appetite and blood sugar; also a class of drugs (such as semaglutide)
- **SGLT2**: a pump in the kidney that takes sugar back into the blood; also a class of diabetes drugs that block it
- **SGLT1**: a pump that pulls sugar in from the gut
- **RCTs**: randomized controlled trials, the strongest kind of study for testing a treatment
- **ApoB**: apolipoprotein B; counts all the cholesterol particles that can clog arteries

## Punchlines & Quotable Facts (Lecture 5)

- **"This is the gospel of metabolism."** Bikman's own description of this chapter: insulin resistance clears up fast, though not for good unless the changes are kept up.
- **"Giving a type 2 diabetic insulin is like giving an alcoholic another glass of wine, hoping the wine will solve their alcoholism."** About treating diabetes driven by insulin resistance with sulfonylureas (pills that make the pancreas release more insulin) or with insulin. 🔴 The lecturer's opinion; in a 6-year randomized trial, insulin glargine (a long-acting insulin) did not raise heart and blood vessel events or cancer (ORIGIN 2012), and insulin is still a standard treatment when needed.
- **The randomized trial data on metformin itself: lifestyle change worked more than 2 times as well as the most-prescribed diabetes drug in the world.** 🟢 In the Diabetes Prevention Program, lifestyle change cut new cases of diabetes by 58%, against 31% for metformin, in adults with prediabetes (blood sugar higher than normal but not yet diabetes) (Knowler 2002).
- **"Not all calories are created equal."** Said after showing a study where people ate the same calories, but the mix of fat, protein and carbohydrate alone led to different results in the body. 🟡 Contested: in a tightly controlled research-ward study at equal calories, cutting fat led to more body-fat loss than cutting carbohydrate (Hall 2015).
- **58 randomized controlled trials compared low-carbohydrate with low-fat diets: 29 show significantly more weight loss on low-carb. Zero show significantly more weight loss on low-fat. Not one, ever, published.** 🟡 Evidence check: this is a count, not a meta-analysis (a formal, reviewed pooling of studies). Meta-analyses find a modest low-carb advantage of about 1 kg at a year or more (Tobias 2015), and the large DIETFITS trial found no difference at 12 months (Gardner 2018).
- **The oldest recorded low-carb vs. low-fat study: the Book of Daniel.** Daniel and his companions refused the king's meat and wine and ate vegetables and pulses (beans and lentils)... and Bikman notes they ended up "fairer and fatter" than expected. A wry ancient data point he cites for context, not as medical evidence.
- **TZD drugs can cause 20 lbs of fat gain in a month** while technically improving insulin sensitivity. The most paradoxical drug effect in the whole lecture. 🔴 Evidence check: the usual weight gain on TZDs is a few kilograms over months, partly from fluid retention (Yki-Järvinen 2004); 20 lbs in a month is not typical.

## Diagnostic Tests — Ranked Simplest to Most Rigorous

**The dynamic test (the most complex of the routine tests; the research gold standard is the hyperinsulinemic-euglycemic clamp, a lab test that holds blood sugar steady while giving insulin (DeFronzo 1979)): the Oral Glucose Tolerance Test (OGTT)**

- You drink a sugar solution, and blood is measured every 30 min for 2 hours.
- **Blood sugar alone:** should be back under 100 mg/dL by 2 hours. 🟡 This is Bikman's stricter target; standard criteria call a 2-hour value under 140 mg/dL normal (ADA 2026).
- **Insulin (if measured at the same time):** the peak should come at the **30-minute mark** specifically. If the peak comes later (60, 90 or more minutes), that is a warning sign of insulin resistance even if blood sugar looks fine. This reading is more sensitive than blood sugar alone, matching the "insulin moves before blood sugar" theme of all four earlier chapters. 🟢 A later insulin peak (60–120 min) predicted a much higher risk of type 2 diabetes over 10 years in Japanese Americans (Hayashi 2013).

**Static tests (one blood draw):**

- **Fasting insulin:** target **about 6 µIU/mL** (approximate; insulin follows a daily rhythm, so a somewhat higher single reading is not automatically bad news). 🔴 This is the lecturer's ideal target, not a cutoff for diagnosis: insulin tests are not standardized between labs (Wallace 2004), and the American Diabetes Association (ADA) criteria for diagnosis do not use insulin (ADA 2026).
- **C-peptide:** made together with insulin (one molecule of each made at the same time), but cleared from the blood far more slowly, so there is much more of it in the blood than insulin itself. It is not interchangeable with the insulin level. It is especially useful for people already injecting insulin, since their measured "insulin" would just reflect the syringe, not what their own body makes.
- **HOMA-IR:** (fasting insulin × fasting blood sugar) ÷ 405
    - Under 1.5 = insulin sensitive; 1.6–3.9 = insulin resistant; over 3.9 = likely in the type 2 diabetes range. 🟡 Contested: there is no universal cutoff; in one population study, cutoffs ranged from about 1.9 to 3.5 depending on the standard used (Gayoso-Diz 2013).
- **TG/HDL ratio** (triglycerides divided by "good" HDL cholesterol): Bikman gives **cutoffs by ethnic background**, stricter than the single cutoff of 3.0 or more from the McLaughlin study (McLaughlin 2003): Black populations about 1.0, white and Asian about 1.5, Hispanic and South Asian Indian about 2.0.
    - This comes from a different, more detailed source than McLaughlin's original study; treat it as a refinement, not a replacement, since it is less widely cited in medical writing than the cutoff of 3.0 or more. 🟡 Evidence check: these cutoffs by ethnic background could not be traced to a study that tested them. In overweight adults, a TG/HDL of 3.0 or more picked up insulin resistance with about 64% sensitivity (how many true cases it catches) and 68% specificity (how many people without it it correctly clears) (McLaughlin 2003), and in African Americans TG/HDL did no better than chance (Sumner 2005).
- **⭐ Bikman's favorite: the Adipose (fat tissue) Insulin Resistance Score:** insulin × free fatty acids (FFA, loose fat in the blood)
    - **The logic:** high insulin should hold back lipolysis (fat breakdown), so free fatty acids should be LOW when insulin is HIGH. If fat cells are insulin resistant (swollen, at their size limit), they leak fat *despite* high insulin. That produces an abnormal combination: high insulin AND high free fatty acids at the same time. 🟡 Adipo-IR (fasting insulin × free fatty acids) is a recognized research measure of insulin resistance in fat tissue (Søndergaard 2017), but it has no tested medical cutoffs; the targets of under 5 and under 8 depend on the units used and are the lecturer's.
    - **Targets by sex:** men under 5, women under 8 (women naturally have a faster turnover of free fatty acids, about 40% more fat burning on average, driven by estradiol's effect on fat breakdown, especially in hip and buttock fat).
    - **Why this one is special:** it catches insulin resistance in the fat cells specifically. It explains the PCOS finding from Ch.4, where whole-body HOMA-IR looked normal but this score caught the problem. It is the earliest-warning test on the whole list, matching the idea of fat tissue as the "first domino." 🔴 The lecturer's interpretation; not established.

**Tests at home (no blood draw needed):**

- **Acanthosis nigricans:** darker, thicker skin with a texture like tissue paper on the neck or in other skin folds (armpits, groin). Bikman says this alone comes close to "proof-positive" of insulin resistance. The change in texture can be felt on all skin tones, even when the darkening is harder to see. 🟢 A well-established skin sign of insulin resistance (Rendon 1989), though not proof on its own; it can also have other causes.
- **Skin tags:** small growths on a little stalk, different from moles; also a direct, visible sign. Both clear up quickly once insulin resistance is corrected, with no skin treatment needed, which makes them truly useful as live feedback. 🟡 Skin tags are linked to insulin resistance; the claim that both clear up quickly is not verified.
- **Waist-to-height ratio:** your waist measurement × 2 should be **less than your height.** Simple; all you need is a tape measure. 🟢 A ratio under 0.5 is a well-supported screening target (Ashwell 2012).

## The Three Primary Causes, Revisited as "Levers"

Of Ch.1's three primary causes (stress, inflammation and too much insulin), Bikman says clearly that **inflammation is the hardest lever to grab**. It is hard to confirm there is even a problem, and unclear what exactly to do about it once found. **Stress and too much insulin are the two levers you can actually pull**, and too much insulin gets the most attention because it is the single fix with the biggest payoff.

**Stress steps, with specific numbers:**

- Evening snacks: leave **about 3 hours** between the last carbohydrate-heavy food and bedtime, so blood sugar and insulin can come down before sleep. This directly prevents the "hot and bothered" bedtime effect of the fight-or-flight nervous system from Ch.3.
- Caffeine: a **6-hour gap** before bed is enough to clear caffeine and avoid its adrenaline-driven effects on blood sugar and heart rate. 🟡 Evidence check: 400 mg of caffeine taken 6 hours before bed still clearly disturbed sleep (Drake 2013); 6 hours is a minimum, not a guarantee.

## Drug Classes — Bikman's Explicit Ranking ("the gospel according to Ben")

**Metformin** (mixed)

- Truly improves insulin sensitivity (muscles take up more sugar, the liver puts out less).
- **But:** it works partly by slightly impairing the mitochondria (the cell's power plants). In someone who exercises regularly, it directly blunts what exercise does for both mitochondrial health AND insulin sensitivity. A real tension: the drug and the best lifestyle step partly cancel each other out. 🟢 In older adults, metformin blunted the gains in insulin sensitivity and in muscle mitochondrial function that exercise brings (Konopka 2019).
- Lifestyle change alone works more than 2 times better in randomized trial data. Bikman gives this as a reason to seriously question making this drug the default for an active person.

**GLP-1 agonists (semaglutide/Ozempic, liraglutide and others)** (mixed, depends on the dose)

- At **low doses that copy the body's own levels:** they truly reduce the liver's sugar output (restoring the signal to store glycogen) and slightly reduce appetite. A real, good way of improving insulin sensitivity.
- At the **high doses used for weight loss** (5–10 times higher, he says): appetite suppression turns into severe nausea, not true fullness. 🟡 Evidence check: weight-loss doses are only about 1.2–2 times the diabetes doses (semaglutide 2.4 mg vs. up to 2 mg; liraglutide 3.0 mg vs. 1.8 mg). Nausea is the most common side effect, but appetite falls even without it.
- **The fat cell paradox (his real concern):** high-dose GLP-1 agonists stimulate the **making of new fat cells (hyperplasia)** while also breaking down fat in existing cells. The net result while on the drug is smaller (shrinking) but MORE fat cells. This raises the personal fat threshold (good: more insulin sensitive) but permanently increases how much fat the body *can store*. 🔴 Evidence check: no human study has shown that GLP-1 drugs create new fat cells. Weight regain after stopping is well documented (two-thirds of the lost weight came back within a year of stopping semaglutide) (Wilding 2022), but it is explained by appetite returning, not by proven growth in fat cell numbers.
- **Stopping the drug:** the push to break down fat goes away, but the new fat cells do not disappear. That leaves more long-term fat-storage capacity than before starting, which Bikman describes as effectively locking someone into needing the drug forever. A structural concern about this specific "make new fat cells, then stop" pattern, not a claim that the drug brings no benefit at all.
- **Bikman's stated advice:** the lowest dose that works, for a limited time, together with real changes in diet. Not a blanket rejection of this drug class. 🟢 Benefit beyond weight: semaglutide cut major heart and blood vessel events by 20% in people with obesity and heart disease but no diabetes (Lincoff 2023).

**SGLT inhibitors** (Bikman's least favorite)

- They block the taking back of sugar in the intestines (SGLT-1) or the kidneys (SGLT-2). This lowers blood sugar and insulin by forcing sugar out of the body instead of absorbing it.
- **Side effects, with the mechanism:** unabsorbed sugar in the intestines draws water in → diarrhea; sugar-rich urine feeds bacteria → a much higher risk of urinary tract infections (UTIs). 🟡 Genital yeast infections are the most common side effect of the widely used kidney (SGLT2) drugs (Zinman 2015); the UTI risk is only slightly raised, and diarrhea comes mainly from blocking SGLT1 in the intestines.
- Bikman's blunt way of putting it: if the alternative to these side effects is simply eating less sugar, why take the drug route at all? ✅ Evidence check: SGLT2 inhibitors have benefits beyond blood sugar. Empagliflozin cut death from heart and blood vessel disease by 38% and hospital stays for heart failure by 35% in type 2 diabetes (Zinman 2015).

**Thiazolidinediones (TZDs, glitazones)** (a fascinating mechanism, a poor trade-off in real life)

- They directly turn swollen (insulin-resistant) fat cells toward the healthier, more-cells (insulin-sensitive) pattern. This truly targets the real mechanism (the personal fat threshold), not just blood sugar.
- **But:** they raise the threshold by adding fat cells, and patients commonly gain **about 20 lbs of fat in a single month.** That is the most common reason people stop the drug, and then the fat cells swell again and the resistance returns. 🔴 The figure of 20 lbs a month is not typical; see the evidence check above (Yki-Järvinen 2004).

**Sulfonylureas and injected insulin for type 2 diabetes** (the ones Bikman opposes most strongly)

- They directly raise insulin in the blood to force blood sugar down. That treats the sugar symptom by giving more of the actual root cause (too much insulin).
- **Documented results, not just theory:** the insulin dose keeps going up over 6 months in treated patients; body weight rises even though the same patients eat *fewer* calories (explained by insulin directly lowering the metabolic rate, the same mechanism as in Ch.2's Joslin and Benedict data).
- **About 90% higher cancer risk** in patients treated with insulin or sulfonylureas. 🟡 Evidence check: in a Canadian study, insulin users had 1.9 times and sulfonylurea users 1.3 times the cancer death rate of metformin users (Bowker 2006). This is observational and likely distorted by other factors (confounded), since sicker patients get insulin. In the ORIGIN randomized trial, 6 years of insulin glargine did not raise cancer (ORIGIN 2012).
- **Up to 3 times higher death rate from heart disease** in the people with diabetes needing the highest insulin doses, despite "good" blood sugar control. 🟡 Observational and not verified; in randomized data, insulin glargine had no effect either way on heart and blood vessel events (ORIGIN 2012).
- Bikman's position: these drugs can never fix the underlying metabolic problem. They manage the symptom without correcting it, and the data suggest they may actively worsen the diseases that insulin resistance itself drives (cancer, heart disease). 🔴 The lecturer's interpretation of observational data.

## Exercise — Brief But Real

- **"The best exercise is the one you will do."** Bikman says clearly that sticking with it matters more than finding the perfect plan. His examples ranged from CrossFit to a slow walk with friends, both fine depending on the person.
- **How it works:** working muscles open a route for taking up sugar that does not need insulin. During the activity itself, clearing sugar from the blood truly no longer depends on insulin, so both blood sugar and insulin drop in real time.

## Surgery — Two Very Different Categories

**Stomach and intestine (metabolic) surgery (gastric bypass, sleeve):**

- It works by forcing people to eat less or absorb less. In effect, a forced, severe cut in energy.
- **How GLP-1 was discovered, according to Bikman:** GLP-1's use as a treatment was *found* by studying why these surgeries improved metabolic health; the rerouted intestines were found to make more GLP-1 naturally. 🔴 Evidence check: this history is wrong. GLP-1 was discovered in the 1980s from the proglucagon gene, and the first GLP-1 drug (exenatide) came from research on Gila monster venom. The rise in GLP-1 after gastric bypass was described later and helps explain how the surgery works (Drucker 2017).

**Liposuction, with a specific study that extends the Ch.2 finding:**

- Removing up to **44%** of the belly fat under the skin produced **no improvement in any measure of metabolic health.** This supports the idea that the *size* of fat cells, not their number, is what really matters. 🟢 In 15 obese women, belly liposuction did not change insulin sensitivity, blood pressure, blood fats or inflammation markers 10–12 weeks later (Klein 2004); 🟡 the explanation about fat cell size is interpretation.
- The same logic of fat moving elsewhere as before: fat grows back in other places (arms, buttocks, hips) if eating habits do not change, because the underlying insulin and energy pressure to store fat somewhere has not been dealt with. 🟡 In the one randomized trial, fat came back mainly in the belly (Hernandez 2011).

## Diet — The Core Intervention

**How much each food type raises insulin, stated simply:**

- Pure fat: no effect on insulin, confirmed directly in Bikman's own lab.
- Pure protein: a modest effect, bigger in people with type 2 diabetes in particular.
- Pure carbohydrate: the main driver.

**Why low-fat diets tend to fail people with insulin resistance:**

- A low-fat diet is, by necessity, a **higher-carbohydrate** diet. That works directly against someone whose actual problem is handling carbohydrate and insulin.
- Studies from the 1980s already found that low-fat diets *worsened* metabolic markers in people with type 2 diabetes. 🟢 For example, in a research-ward study where people tried both diets in turn, a high-carbohydrate diet gave higher blood sugar, higher insulin needs and higher triglycerides than a diet high in monounsaturated fat (the kind in olive oil) (Garg 1988).
- **The American Diabetes Association's own 2019 nutrition consensus report** says that eating less carbohydrate overall has the most evidence for improving blood sugar (Evert 2019). A mainstream body reaching the same conclusion Bikman has argued for years. 🟢 The same report also accepts several eating patterns (for example Mediterranean, low-fat, vegetarian), not one diet for everyone.

**Direct comparisons in randomized trials he cites (several separate trials):**

- One trial: low-carb lowered both fasting blood sugar and fasting insulin several times more than low-fat (🟡 the specific trial is not verified).
- A three-way trial (low-fat with calorie limits vs. Mediterranean with calorie limits vs. low-carb without calorie limits): the low-carb group lost weight so dramatically that researchers had to raise their carbohydrate allowance 6 times (20g→120g a day) to slow it down for the study to stay valid, and it still beat the calorie-limited groups on every metabolic marker measured. 🟡 Evidence check: in this 2-year trial (DIRECT), the low-carb group started at 20 g a day for 2 months, with a planned gradual rise to at most 120 g a day. That was part of the design, not a fix in the middle of the study. Weight loss was 4.7 kg (low-carb), 4.4 kg (Mediterranean) and 2.9 kg (low-fat); low-carb improved the cholesterol ratio most, but in people with diabetes the Mediterranean diet gave better fasting blood sugar and insulin (Shai 2008).
- **Results split by insulin-sensitive vs. insulin-resistant people (in two separate studies):** insulin-sensitive people do well on *either* diet; insulin-resistant people show a **real, meaningful response only to low-carb**, not low-fat, even at the same calories. A key, personal finding: the "best diet" truly depends on how insulin resistant a person is at the start; there is no one-size-fits-all answer. 🟡 Evidence check: a small 16-week trial in 21 women found this pattern (Cornier 2005), but the large DIETFITS trial (609 adults) found that how much insulin people made at the start did not predict who did better on low-carb vs. low-fat (Gardner 2018).
- **Weight loss by insulin response:** people with a big insulin response to a sugar drink lost **more than 2 times the weight** on low-carb as on low-fat; people with a small insulin response did fine on either diet. 🟡 Not confirmed in DIETFITS (Gardner 2018).

**The count of studies, stated precisely:** across 58 published randomized trials comparing low-carb with low-fat, 48 leaned toward more weight loss on low-carb (29 of them statistically significant, that is, unlikely to be chance), 7 leaned toward low-fat (none significant), and 3 found no difference. **No randomized trial has ever found significantly more weight loss on a low-fat diet.** 🟡 Evidence check: this count is not a reviewed meta-analysis. Reviewed meta-analyses find a modest low-carb advantage of about 1 kg at a year or more (Tobias 2015), and a research-ward study found that cutting fat led to more body-fat loss than cutting carbohydrate at equal calories (Hall 2015).

## Key Corrections to Carry Forward

- Thinking GLP-1 agonists are a metabolic "free" win with no lasting downside: the "make new fat cells, then stop" pattern is a real long-term drawback with a mechanism behind it, not just a problem of eating more afterward. 🔴 Not shown in humans; see the evidence check above (Wilding 2022).
- Thinking a single TG/HDL cutoff of 3.0 applies to everyone: this chapter gives stricter cutoffs by ethnic background (🟡 not tested; see the evidence check above, Sumner 2005).
- Thinking the "best diet" is the same for everyone regardless of insulin status: repeated randomized trial data show insulin-resistant and insulin-sensitive people respond differently to the same diets (🟡 contradicted by the largest trial, Gardner 2018).

## Practical Takeaway

Insulin resistance can be spotted early, with an insulin test, the fat-tissue score or simply a tape measure, and the main fix is lowering the insulin load through diet and movement, with drugs used carefully as helpers rather than as the whole answer.

## Questions to Learn From (Socratic) — with Answer Options

**1. Given the "make new fat cells, then stop" mechanism of GLP-1 agonists: if someone used this kind of drug, what would reduce the long-term downside of extra fat-storage capacity described here?**

A. Using the highest possible dose for the most weight loss in the short term
B. *Using the lowest dose that works, for the shortest time needed, together with diet changes that tackle insulin resistance directly. This is exactly Bikman's stated advice, since it limits how many new fat cells are made while still getting some of the insulin-sensitizing benefit
C. There is no way to avoid the downside; the drug should never be used under any circumstances

*Why B: Bikman does not reject this drug class outright. He specifically recommends keeping the dose and the time on it low while using diet to tackle the root cause, rather than relying on the drug alone forever.*

**2. Insulin-resistant and insulin-sensitive people showed truly different responses to the same low-carb and low-fat diets in several randomized trials. What does this mean for general "best diet" advice?**

A. There is one best diet for everyone, and these studies just had flaws in their methods
B. *The right diet seems to depend on a person's underlying insulin resistance. Someone who is already insulin sensitive may do fine either way, while someone insulin resistant (like the type these trials describe) shows a real, specific advantage from cutting carbohydrate
C. Diet has no measurable effect on insulin resistance, whatever its mix of fat, protein and carbohydrate

*Why B: this is the direct, repeated finding of at least two separate studies cited. The same diet gives different real-world results depending on the starting insulin status, which is exactly why general diet advice needs a caution attached.*

**3. Metformin partly blunts both the mitochondrial benefits and the insulin-sensitizing benefits of exercise. What should someone who exercises regularly and is thinking about metformin take into account?**

A. Metformin and exercise fully complement each other, with no interaction to think about
B. *The two can partly work against each other at the level of mechanism described here. It is worth asking the prescribing doctor whether lifestyle change alone might be better for someone already exercising regularly, given the randomized trial data showing lifestyle change beats metformin by more than 2 times
C. Metformin should always come before exercise, since it is a more "precise" treatment

*Why B: this is a real, specific tension in the mechanism that Bikman raises.*
