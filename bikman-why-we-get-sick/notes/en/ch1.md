
# Bikman — Why We Get Sick, Ch.1: Metabolic Origins of Disease
*This lecture reframes insulin resistance as the common upstream driver of most chronic disease and explains the fat-cell biochemistry — hypertrophy, hypoxia, and ceramide formation — behind it.*

*Source: Dr. Benjamin Bikman, Peterson Academy course "Why We Get Sick," Lecture 1 (transcript). Bikman is a metabolic scientist/professor (BYU) — credentialed academic source, not a media personality.*

**In plain words:** when the body stops responding well to insulin (insulin resistance), it makes more and more insulin to compensate, often for years before blood sugar rises. Bikman argues this starts in fat cells that grow too big, run short of oxygen and send out inflammation signals, and that stress, inflammation and too much insulin are the three main causes. He links it to type 2 diabetes, heart disease, fatty liver and more; the strength of the evidence for each link is marked below.

**Words in this note:**
- **NHANES**: a large U.S. government health and nutrition survey
- **HDL**: high-density lipoprotein, the "good" cholesterol carrier
- **LDL**: low-density lipoprotein, the "bad" cholesterol carrier
- **ApoB**: apolipoprotein B; counts all the cholesterol particles that can clog arteries
- **HbA1c**: a blood test showing average blood sugar over about three months
- **TG**: triglycerides, the main fat in the blood
- **AMPK**: the cell's fuel-gauge switch, turned on when energy is low

## Why This Matters

This lecture changes how we see insulin resistance (when the body stops responding well to insulin). It is not "one disease among many." It is "the common soil most long-term disease grows from." The lecture also gives the actual chemistry of how a fat cell goes from storing energy safely to poisoning the rest of the body. And it brings back the older name for metabolic syndrome, "insulin resistance syndrome," which describes the cause more honestly than the modern term.

## Why It Matters at Scale — The Disease List

Bikman says plainly that insulin resistance is either the direct cause or a major worsening factor in many conditions most people would never link to a single hormone:

- **Direct cause:** type 2 diabetes
- **Major driver or worsening factor:** heart disease, certain cancers, fatty liver disease (the most common liver disease), obesity (fat tissue growing out of control)
- **Less recognized, but still linked to insulin resistance:** infertility in men and women, joint and bone problems like arthritis, and brain disease, including dementia and Alzheimer's disease

This is the big claim behind the whole course. Insulin resistance is not just a diabetes topic. It is presented as a common root cause across heart and blood vessel disease, cancer, fertility, bones and joints, and the brain. Some of these links are well established (type 2 diabetes, heart disease, fatty liver). Others (infertility, arthritis, dementia) are described as influenced by or connected to insulin resistance, not directly caused by it. That difference is real and worth keeping in mind as the course reaches each one. 🟢 Strong evidence for type 2 diabetes, fatty liver and heart and blood vessel risk; 🟡 a link with believable mechanisms, not a proven cause, for most of the others.

## Punchlines & Quotable Facts (Lecture 1)

Short, strong lines worth remembering as they are. Each one packs a whole mechanism into one sentence.

- **"It is impossible for a fat cell to grow unless insulin is elevated. It cannot happen. It's impossible."** The hardest claim in the lecture: insulin as the absolute gatekeeper for storing fat, not just a helper. 🔴 Evidence check: insulin is the main fat-storage signal, but "impossible" is a figure of speech. In a study where people lived on a research ward with every meal measured, cutting insulin with a ketogenic diet (very low in carbohydrate) of the same calories did not speed up body-fat loss (Hall 2016).
- **"There is no type 2 diabetes without insulin resistance."** Stated as a requirement, not just something that tends to go along with it. 🟡 Evidence check: true for most cases, but one subgroup of adult-onset diabetes, found by sorting patient data, is driven mainly by too little insulin rather than resistance (Ahlqvist 2018).
- **"A low insulin diet is not necessarily a low energy diet."** The one-line summary of the "when insulin and energy part ways" section below. Insulin and calories are two separate levers.
- **"Insulin resistance starts in the fat cells."** The first domino. Everything else in the chain follows from fat tissue specifically, not from muscle or liver first. 🔴 Bikman's hypothesis; muscle insulin resistance is already present in young, lean adults who are insulin resistant (Petersen 2007), and which tissue fails first is debated.
- **"[Lowering blood glucose] is insulin's most famous job, but it is far from its most important."** A direct challenge to the idea that insulin is only about blood sugar.
- **"Too much of something will result in a resistance to that something."** Bikman's general rule of biology, which he applies to insulin, caffeine and antibiotics alike. It is the logic behind "too much insulin causes insulin resistance."
- **"[In diabetes] the insulin does not go to zero. It does not go even to as low as it used to be when they were insulin sensitive."** A direct correction to the common belief that people with diabetes have "low insulin." 🟢 True for most early type 2 diabetes; 🟡 insulin output falls as the insulin-making beta cells wear out over the years (DeFronzo 2009).
- **"It's not because we eat a lot of palmitate… the liver is so good at making it."** A punchline against blaming saturated fat in food directly for the damage done by ceramides (harmful fat molecules explained below). The liver makes the problem itself.
- **One bad night of sleep → about 25% more insulin resistance the next day, through cortisol (a stress hormone).** The most concrete number in the lecture linking sleep to metabolic health. 🟢 One night of about 4 hours' sleep cut insulin sensitivity by about 25% in 9 healthy adults (Donga 2010); 🟡 that study did not show that cortisol was the cause.
- **88% of US adults are metabolically unhealthy; only 12% are fully healthy.** The number the whole course is built to answer. 🟢 In the NHANES 2009–2016 survey, only 12.2% of US adults had ideal levels on all five markers (Araújo 2019).
- **"Notice the use of my word 'some.'"** Bikman's own emphasis: insulin resistance is never total, and never the same in every cell or tissue.

## Two Definitions of Metabolic Health

**1. Metabolic Syndrome** (Gerald Reaven named this cluster "Syndrome X" in 1988; it was later also called "insulin resistance syndrome") (Reaven 1988)

- A group of 5 signs, any 3 of which make the diagnosis: a large waist, high blood pressure, high fasting blood sugar, high triglycerides, and low HDL (Alberti 2009).
- Notably, ordinary LDL cholesterol is NOT part of this definition. Even the official metabolic syndrome criteria do not use LDL, although standard medical care focuses heavily on it.
- A study cited: 88% of US adults are not at ideal levels on all five markers (only 12.2% fully metabolically healthy) (Araújo 2019). This points the same way as the roughly 93% figure from Tufts (O'Hearn 2022) found in the Hyman fact-check, though the exact percentage changes with the study and the criteria, as already noted.

**2. Metabolic Flexibility**

- The ability to switch fuels: burning sugar after meals (when insulin is high) and burning fat between meals and during fasting (when insulin is low).
- **Metabolic inflexibility** means being stuck burning sugar even when fasting, because insulin never comes down far enough to allow the switch to fat burning. It is different from metabolic syndrome but related to it; both come from insulin resistance.

## The Global Picture — Prevalence

- 88% of US adults metabolically unhealthy (only 12% fully healthy) is NOT the worst in the world. Bikman ranks South Asia, the Middle East and the Pacific Islands as worse, and Mexico as worse than the US within North America. Southeast Asia is also worse. 🟡 This ranking is not verified; treat it as the lecturer's summary.
- He presents this as a world problem, not a failure of the American diet alone. That helps avoid blaming any single country's way of eating too much.
- Mentioned but not yet explained: fat cells behave differently in different ethnic groups (saved for a later lecture; listed under Open Threads below).

## Insulin's Full Job Description (Not Just Blood Sugar)

Bikman is clear: lowering blood sugar is insulin's **most famous** job, but far from its **most important**. Insulin also controls, directly or indirectly:

- Inflammation and nearly every part of immune function
- How the body handles fat and protein, not just sugar
- Building protein in muscle and other tissues (the "grow" signal)
- The movement of electrolytes (body salts): sodium and potassium in and out of cells

This is why problems with insulin show up far beyond blood sugar and weight. Insulin is a hormone that steers the whole body, so resistance to it has knock-on effects on the immune system, the body's structure and its salt balance, not just on how it handles fuel. Insulin is made by the beta cells of the pancreas, and only in picomolar amounts (extremely tiny). Even so, it has an outsized influence. Bikman's point: a hormone's power does not depend on how much of it there is.

## Insulin Resistance — The Two-Sided Coin

Bikman's key idea: insulin resistance is NOT just "cells stop listening." It always has two halves that cannot be separated:

1. **Some cells respond less to insulin** (the half the name describes)
2. **Hyperinsulinemia (too much insulin in the blood):** the body makes up for it by producing more insulin

These two usually go together while the pancreas can still keep up. 🟢 Strong evidence (Kahn 2003). 🟡 Not absolute: once the beta cells fail, the resistance stays while insulin output falls, and it is still debated whether high insulin is mainly the cause or the result of resistance (Shanik 2008).

**Bikman's stronger claim, close to his own words:** "There is no type 2 diabetes without insulin resistance." Not just linked or coming first: he states that insulin resistance is required before type 2 diabetes can exist, not just a common companion. Worth holding as a claim to test against other sources, rather than softening it into "they tend to occur together."

**A correction to a common belief:** in type 2 diabetes, insulin does NOT drop to zero, or even back to "normal." It usually stays several times higher than in a healthy person, even as it works less well. When doctors say insulin "becomes insufficient" in type 2 diabetes, they mean not enough *compared with the resistance*, not too little in absolute terms. 🟢 For early and middle-stage disease; 🟡 later on, the loss of beta cells lowers insulin output (DeFronzo 2009).

## The Critical Timeline

Insulin rises **years to decades** before blood sugar ever climbs out of the normal range. 🟢 In the Whitehall II study, which followed British civil servants, insulin sensitivity fell steeply in the roughly 5 years before diagnosis, while blood sugar rose sharply only in the last 3 or so (Tabák 2009); 🟡 "decades" is an estimate. There are two separate phases:

- **Phase 1 (silent):** insulin resistance is present, blood sugar is still normal, insulin is high. It is missed because insulin is not routinely tested.
- **Phase 2 (diabetes):** insulin resistance has spread to muscle, liver and the alpha cells of the pancreas. Now blood sugar climbs too, and the disease becomes "real" to standard medical care.

The disease can be present for a decade before the standard test catches it.

**The order of tissues matters:** insulin resistance starts in fat cells FIRST, then spreads to muscle, liver, and the alpha cells of the pancreas (not the beta cells: the alpha cells, which make glucagon, a hormone that raises blood sugar). Only when it reaches this second wave of tissues does blood sugar actually start to climb. 🔴 This is Bikman's model of the order. 🟢 Too much glucagon from insulin-resistant alpha cells is a real part of type 2 diabetes (DeFronzo 2009), but insulin resistance in muscle can also come early (Petersen 2007).

## Three Primary, Independent Causes of Insulin Resistance

Bikman's standard for calling a cause "primary": it has been shown in cells grown in the lab, in animals AND in humans. Three causes meet that standard:

**1. Stress** (the stress hormones cortisol and epinephrine)

- Bikman is precise here. Epinephrine (adrenaline) and cortisol have almost nothing in common chemically. They are made in different ways, by different cells in the adrenal gland, travel differently in the blood and act through different receptors. The only thing they share is that each, on its own, raises blood sugar (epinephrine quickly and strongly, cortisol more slowly but substantially).
- Both stress hormones push blood sugar UP, which forces insulin to work harder to bring it down. Long-term stress means insulin is overworked all the time, and that leads to resistance.
- **Lack of sleep:** one bad night raises cortisol enough to increase insulin resistance by about 25% the next day. One good night can undo the effect of a single bad night, but bad nights in a row add up rather than resetting. 🟢 One night of about 4 hours' sleep reduced insulin sensitivity by about 25% in healthy adults (Donga 2010); 🟡 cortisol as the cause, and the one-good-night reversal, are not verified.
- **Children and sleep:** a 2023 study he cited found that children who regularly lost just 1 hour of the sleep they needed each night were nearly 3 times more likely to show medically significant insulin resistance within 2 years. 🟡 Not verified; the specific 2023 study could not be identified.
- **Caffeine:** raises epinephrine, which helps the body burn fat but briefly lowers insulin sensitivity. 🟢 Evidence check: caffeine given straight into a vein cut insulin sensitivity by 15% in healthy adults (Keijzers 2002), yet people who drink coffee regularly have a lower risk of type 2 diabetes (Ding 2014). A real but small and short-lived effect, not a reason to avoid caffeine.

**2. Inflammation** (through cytokines)

- Cytokines are small signal proteins, made mainly by immune cells, that switch on inflammation throughout the body. Any source of inflammation quickly worsens insulin sensitivity: an infection, a flare of an autoimmune disease, air pollution, or the fat cells themselves.
- A notable example he cited: people with rheumatoid arthritis show clearly worse insulin resistance during active flares than when the disease is calm. This is real-time evidence that inflammation and insulin resistance move together, not just that they are linked over the long run. 🟡 The specific study was not verified; 🟢 that inflammatory cytokines interfere with insulin's signal is well established (Hotamisligil 2006).
- **Illness in real time:** people wearing continuous glucose monitors (small sensors that track blood sugar all day) can often tell they are getting sick before they feel symptoms. Their blood sugar becomes jumpy and high as cytokines rise to fight the infection. This is a practical, visible link between inflammation and insulin resistance.
- **How air pollution does it:** there are only two ways for things in the environment to get into the bloodstream. One is the skin, which is rare, because skin is built to keep things out. The other is swallowing or breathing them in. Fine particles that are breathed in build up in lung tissue and set off local inflammation there. The lungs respond by making cytokines, which then travel through the whole body and worsen insulin sensitivity everywhere, from something that only entered through breathing. 🟡 A believable mechanism with some support from animal and human studies; how big the effect is in people is uncertain.

**3. Hyperinsulinemia itself (too much insulin, a vicious circle)**

- Insulin that stays high for a long time, on its own, makes the body turn down its response to insulin. It is the same idea as getting used to caffeine, or bacteria becoming resistant to antibiotics: steady exposure to a signal makes you less sensitive to it. 🟢 Four days of steady, natural-range high insulin cut the body's ability to clear sugar from the blood by 20–40% in healthy young adults (Del Prato 1994); 🟡 how much this drives everyday insulin resistance, rather than following it, is debated (Shanik 2008).
- It creates a loop that feeds itself: resistance → more insulin → more resistance → more insulin, whatever cause started it.

## Fat Cell Growth — The Insulin/Calorie Distinction

**Background: how many fat cells, and how long they live.** By adulthood, the number of fat cells a person has is usually set and stays fixed for most people from then on, even after a lot of weight loss (Spalding 2008). (Some people keep the ability to make new fat cells, called hyperplasia; see below.) Single fat cells do not live forever, but they live a long time: about 10% are replaced each year, so a fat cell lasts roughly 10 years on average before a new one replaces it (Spalding 2008). This matters because it explains *why* hypertrophy (existing cells getting bigger) is the normal way most adults store more fat. Once the number of fat cells is set, simply making more of them is limited.

**The key mechanism:** fat cells CANNOT grow without insulin. Insulin is the growth *signal*; calories are the growth *fuel*. Both are needed, but they do different jobs. Bikman showed this in his own lab: fat cells kept in a nutrient-rich liquid (unlimited "calories") stay small until insulin is added; then they grow. So insulin does not just passively allow extra calories to be stored. It is an active instruction, and fat cells cannot grow without it. 🔴 This is Bikman's own lab result as he described it in the lecture; not independently verified here.

**Real-world confirmation:** people with diabetes who injected insulin again and again into the same spot of skin (instead of changing spots) had fat cells at that spot grown to about 10 times the size of nearby fat cells that got no direct insulin. It is striking and clear evidence that insulin is a growth signal, whatever the total calories eaten. 🟢 Fat lumps at repeated injection sites (insulin lipohypertrophy) are well documented; 🟡 the figure of 10 times the size is not verified.

## When Insulin and Energy Diverge — Two Instructive Scenarios

Bikman uses these two unusual cases to show that insulin and calories are separate levers, not one single "overeating" factor.

**Scenario A: high insulin + little energy coming in (why this cannot safely happen)**

If insulin stayed high while the energy (calories) coming in were low, insulin would still forcefully push whatever energy is in the blood into fat storage. That would starve the brain, which needs a large, steady supply of energy and cannot store its own fuel. This is the body's reason it cannot keep "high insulin, little food" going for long: it would be deadly, because the brain would run out of energy, so the body does not let this combination last unchecked. 🔴 The lecturer's reasoning, not a tested result.

- This is also why "just eat less" advice tends to work poorly when insulin stays high (for example, frequent small high-carbohydrate snacks), even with fewer calories.

**Scenario B: low insulin + lots of energy coming in (fat cells shrink even with plenty of food)**

If insulin is kept low even while a lot of energy comes in, fat cells shrink instead of growing. Two things cause this:

1. **More energy burned:** the metabolic rate (how fast the body uses energy) goes up.
2. **Energy actively thrown away:** extra sugar or ketones (a fuel the body makes from fat) are passed out in the urine (glucosuria, ketonuria) or breathed out as ketones. The body discards energy-rich molecules it would normally store.

- Bikman's clear point: **a low-insulin diet is not the same as a low-energy diet.** This is his chemical explanation for why very-low-carbohydrate (ketogenic) diets can cause fat loss without a strict calorie deficit on paper. The body discards energy instead of storing it, because the storage signal (insulin) is not there to send it into fat tissue. 🟡 Evidence check: in a research-ward study, a ketogenic diet with the same calories raised the energy burned by only about 57–151 kcal a day and did not speed up fat loss (Hall 2016). The energy lost as ketones in urine and breath is small, except in uncontrolled diabetes.
- Worth noting: this does not mean calories do not matter. It means insulin decides *where* incoming energy goes (stored, burned or wasted), which is a different claim from "calories do not count."

## Two Ways to Store Fat — Why It Matters Metabolically

| Mechanism | What happens | Effect on health |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Hypertrophy (most common) | Existing fat cells get bigger | Unhealthy for metabolism; problems below |
| Hyperplasia (some people, more capacity) | New fat cells are added | Healthier for metabolism; "more train cars" instead of overstuffing one |

**Why oversized fat cells become a problem, two ways:**

1. **They run out of room.** Once a fat cell reaches its size limit, it becomes resistant to insulin's "keep growing, keep the fat locked in" signal. This protects the cell, which would otherwise burst. But the cell starts leaking fat into the blood even though insulin is high.
2. **They outgrow their blood supply (hypoxia, a lack of oxygen).** Fat cells need to stay within about 50 micrometers of a capillary (the tiniest blood vessel) to get oxygen and nutrients. An oversized fat cell can reach about **300 micrometers across**. So a cell sitting one place back from a capillary is pushed far beyond the roughly 50 µm range by the bulk of its own neighbor, and it becomes short of oxygen. The size mismatch is the whole problem: the cell does not move away from the blood supply; it simply grows until the distance cannot be bridged. In response, the fat cell starts making its own cytokines, partly to call for new blood vessels, but these same cytokines also drive inflammation throughout the body. 🟢 Lack of oxygen and inflammation in fat tissue in obesity are well supported (Trayhurn 2013); 🟡 the exact 50 µm and 300 µm figures are the lecturer's.

**The result:** an overstuffed, oxygen-starved fat cell both (a) leaks fat it should not leak and (b) sends out inflammation signals, which is Primary Cause #2 above on its own. The fat cell actively makes insulin resistance worse throughout the body; it is not just passively storing extra energy.

## Ceramides — The Molecular Bridge

- Ceramides are harmful fat molecules built from **palmitate** (a saturated fat) + **serine** (an amino acid, a building block of protein). Each is harmless on its own; together, under the right signal, they do harm.
- **Most palmitate is made by the liver, not eaten.** The liver makes it through de novo lipogenesis (making new fat from scratch, mostly from sugar), and insulin is the main signal telling the liver to do this. Higher insulin → more liver-made palmitate → more raw material for ceramides. 🟡 Evidence check: palmitate is also the most common saturated fat in food, and in people with fatty liver about 26% of the liver fat came from new fat made in the liver, against about 59% from fatty acids already in the blood (Donnelly 2005).
- The three primary causes (stress, inflammation, too much insulin) are what actually flip the switch that turns palmitate + serine into ceramides.
- Once ceramides build up in the alpha cells of the pancreas, the liver and muscle, that is the tipping point from "insulin resistant" to full type 2 diabetes. 🔴 Bikman's hypothesis; 🟢 ceramides are strongly linked to insulin resistance in animal and human studies (Chavez 2012), but this exact tipping point is not established.
- **Effects further down the line named in the lecture:** as ceramides spread from fat tissue through the body, Bikman lists effects "from top to bottom": blood vessels that work poorly and higher blood pressure, infertility, and weaker thinking and memory, among others saved for later lectures. This is the bridge that explains *how* a problem in fat cells becomes the many-organ disease list at the top of this note. Ceramides are the traveling messengers that carry fat-tissue trouble to distant organs.

## The Reversibility Claim

Bikman describes the whole course as "a horror story with a happy ending," and says clearly that the fix is **"very real and very immediate."** Worth holding as his stated position, to test against the evidence as later lectures give the treatment material, not as something already proven. The reasoning behind it is believable: insulin levels respond quickly to changes in food and activity, and the chain above is driven by signals rather than by permanent damage. But "immediate" is a strong word, and how much and how fast things reverse probably depends on how far the spread through the tissues has gone. Noted as an open claim, not a conclusion.

## Key Corrections to Carry Forward

- Thinking a fat cell is a passive storage box: it is an active tissue that sends hormone and immune signals, and it can drive inflammation and leak fat whatever the diet.
- Thinking "insulin becomes insufficient" in type 2 diabetes means low insulin: it is usually still higher than in a healthy person, just less effective against the resistance.
- Thinking metabolic syndrome and ordinary cholesterol tests overlap: the official metabolic syndrome definition does not include LDL at all.

## Practical Takeaway

The chain of oversized fat cells, lack of oxygen and ceramides gives a second, separate reason (beyond the liver and blood-fat pathway already covered) why strength training and avoiding a constant calorie surplus matter. Both directly take pressure off single fat cells, so they do not swell into the oversized, oxygen-starved, inflammation-making state.
