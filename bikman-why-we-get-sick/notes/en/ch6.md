
# Bikman — Why We Get Sick, Ch.6: Mastering Insulin Control (Finale)
*This closing lecture lays out the four dietary pillars in their proper sequence, temperature-based interventions, and the specific numeric homework targets for reversing insulin resistance.*

*Source: Dr. Benjamin Bikman, Peterson Academy course "Why We Get Sick," Lecture 6 + Q&A (final lecture, transcript). This closes the six-chapter arc: what insulin resistance is (Ch.1–2), why it matters (Ch.3–4), and now the full practical implementation (Ch.5–6).*

## Why This Matters

This is the actual "how" chapter — the four dietary pillars in a specific, deliberate order, plus temperature-based interventions, plus the specific numeric homework targets that finally give concrete goals for everything built up across five prior chapters.

## The Central Organizing Principle — Order of Operations

**Bikman's core critique of 60 years of standard dietary advice:** "eat less and exercise more" as a *starting* strategy is literally the textbook recipe for maximizing hunger before a big meal — he draws this out explicitly with the buffet analogy. Doing this as the *first* intervention (rather than lowering insulin first) sets up a hunger battle that, in a food-abundant environment, hunger will eventually win.

**The corrected sequence:** lower insulin first (via carbohydrate *quality*, not quantity restriction alone) → this unlocks the body's own stored fat as usable fuel → *then*, if still needed, add deliberate energy restriction (ideally via intermittent fasting) once the body is already fat-adapted and hunger is genuinely lower. This is Bikman's answer to the plateau question in the Q&A below — it's not that low-carb "stops working," it's that this is precisely the point to layer in fasting, not abandon the approach.

## Punchlines & Quotable Facts (Lecture 6)

- **"Hunger always wins."** — stated as close to a law of human behavior in a food-abundant environment; the central justification for sequencing insulin-lowering before energy restriction.
- **"If God wanted us to drink an apple, then the apple would have come in a little slurpy form from the tree."** — Bikman's case against juicing/blending whole fruit.
- **"That's how God intended them to be. In our hubris, we have pulled them apart."** — on eating protein and fat together rather than isolated (e.g., lean protein powder alone).
- **"There is no such thing as an essential carbohydrate"** — restated from Ch.2, now backed explicitly by a National Academy of Sciences statement that the dietary carbohydrate lower limit is zero. 🟢 The Institute of Medicine wrote that the lower limit compatible with life "apparently is zero" if enough protein and fat are eaten — but it still set an RDA of 130 g/day, based on the brain's average glucose use (IOM 2005).
- **"We do need blood glucose. We do not need dietary carbohydrate."** — the key distinction Bikman insists people stop conflating.
- **Just 8 hours of carb restriction is enough to lose "first phase" insulin secretion** — explaining why a low-carb eater can temporarily "fail" an OGTT despite being more insulin sensitive, not less. 🟡 The 8-hour figure and first-phase mechanism are unverified; see the evidence check in the Q&A section below (WHO 1999).
- **A 12-minute sauna session, four times, dropped cortisol by 30%.** 🟡 Unverified.
- **Whatever manna was to the Israelites, "it was not fiber"** — Bikman's blunt dismissal of fiber as an essential nutrient (Q&A). 🟢 Evidence check: in meta-analyses, people eating the most fiber had 15–30% lower mortality, heart disease, stroke, type 2 diabetes and colorectal cancer than those eating the least (Reynolds 2019).

## The Four Pillars, In Detail

**1. Control Carbohydrates**

- Not "zero carbs" — focus on **whole** fruits/vegetables (fiber intact) over grains/bread/cereal/rice, which carry a much larger insulin impact
- **Never juice or blend** — removing fiber removes the natural brake on the sugar/starch hitting the bloodstream
- **Meal sequencing matters, independently of content:** in a cited study, eating protein/fat *before* carbs (rather than carbs first) cut the glycemic response by **50%** for the *identical* meal — a genuinely free, zero-cost intervention. 🟢 In a small study of adults with type 2 diabetes, eating vegetables and protein before carbohydrate markedly lowered post-meal glucose and insulin (Shukla 2015); 🟡 the size of the drop varies by time point
- **Fermentation as a carb-lowering tool:** bacteria consume the starches/sugars in fermented foods (dairy, vegetables) without touching the protein or fat — also produces beneficial short-chain fats as a byproduct
- **Apple cider vinegar (acetic acid — a 2-carbon short-chain fatty acid):** blunts glycemic response by altering starch/sugar digestion *and* accelerating muscle glucose uptake — called out by Bikman as one of the most overlooked, evidence-backed "hacks" available. 🟢 A meta-analysis found vinegar lowered post-meal glucose and insulin responses (Shishehbor 2017)
- **Sweeteners, ranked:** sugar alcohols (xylitol, erythritol, etc.) have some insulin effect, less than sugar; allulose, aspartame, stevia, monk fruit have essentially none — but watch for dextrose fillers in packaged stevia products specifically, which can undercut the point of choosing it

**2. Prioritize Protein**

- Target: **~1.5 g/kg body weight** (use ideal body weight if significantly overweight). 🟡 Above the RDA of 0.8 g/kg; an expert group recommends 1.0–1.2 g/kg/day for healthy older adults, more with illness (Bauer 2013)
- Protein needs **increase with age** — the body's ability to convert consumed protein into muscle/bone protein diminishes over time, meaning a 65-year-old needs *more* protein than a 25-year-old to achieve the same effect, not less. 🟢 "Anabolic resistance" in older age is well documented (Bauer 2013).
- Animal protein > any plant protein, including soy (the best plant option) — due to complete amino acid profiles and absence of anti-nutrients (phytic acid, tannins, trypsin inhibitors)
- **Fermented plant proteins are a genuine exception** — fermentation reduces anti-nutrients and heavy metal content (a cited third-party analysis found plant protein powders were the worst offenders for lead/arsenic contamination — 🟡 the analysis was not identified; unverified)
- **Eat protein WITH fat, not alone:** a 1:1 protein:fat ratio (like a whole egg) roughly **doubles** muscle protein synthesis compared to protein alone in cited human studies. 🟡 Evidence check: after resistance exercise, whole eggs stimulated more muscle protein synthesis than egg whites with the same protein (van Vliet 2017); the "double" figure is unverified. Historical parallel: "rabbit starvation" among early explorers eating only extremely lean meat — severe diarrhea from inability to properly digest protein without accompanying fat, due to bile acid dynamics. 🟡 The bile-acid explanation is unverified; "protein poisoning" is usually attributed to the liver's limited capacity to dispose of protein nitrogen.

**3. Do not Fear Fat**

- Natural fats (animal fats, fruit fats like coconut/avocado/olive) are fine and contain all three fat types (saturated/mono/poly) in a naturally low-PUFA ratio — the concern is specifically *refined seed oils*, not fat broadly, consistent with Ch.2–3's material
- **New mechanistic detail this chapter adds:** saturated and monounsaturated fats raise **GLP-1** secretion 15–20x above baseline; pure polyunsaturated fat produces at best half to a third of that GLP-1 response. A direct, previously-uncovered mechanistic reason natural fats support the same hormone (GLP-1) that the drug class in Ch.5 is designed to mimic — "do not fear fat" is not just about avoiding harm, it's actively supporting a beneficial insulin-sensitizing pathway. 🟡 Evidence check: the 15–20x figure is unverified. In healthy adults, an olive-oil (monounsaturated) meal raised GLP-1 more than a butter (saturated) meal (Thomsen 1999), and natural GLP-1 rises after meals are far smaller than drug levels (Drucker 2017).

**4. Watch the Clock (energy restriction — last, and optional)**

- Minimum version: eliminate snacking, especially evening snacking — effectively creates "mini-fasts" around three square meals
- **Fasting through breakfast vs. fasting through dinner — not equivalent, per cited studies:** fasting through breakfast (skip breakfast, eat lunch/dinner) increases next-day hunger and fat synthesis; fasting through dinner (normal breakfast/lunch, skip or minimize dinner) produces greater next-day satiety. 🟡 Evidence check: in a 4-week crossover trial, a big breakfast lowered hunger but did not change energy expenditure or weight loss compared with a big dinner (Ruddick-Collins 2022); an earlier trial found more weight loss with a big breakfast (Jakubowicz 2013).
- **"Breakfast like a king, lunch like a prince, dinner like a pauper"** — Bikman's summary framing, explicitly acknowledged as socially harder (family dinner dynamics) but metabolically superior
- **Isocaloric confirmation:** even at matched total calories, a low-fat/high-carb early eating window produced significantly greater hunger throughout the day than a low-carb version — same theme as Ch.5's isocaloric study, reinforced again here specifically for meal-timing
- **Fasting protocol menu mentioned:** 18:6 (18 hr fast/6 hr eating window), OMAD (one meal a day), 1:1 (alternate-day 24hr fasting), or 5:2 (5 days of normal eating, 2 days a week of very low intake, roughly 500–650 kcal — not 48-hour fasts) (Harvie 2011) — named but not deeply compared in this transcript segment

## Temperature Interventions — Both Directions Work

**Heat (sauna, hot bath):**

- Improves blood flow to a degree comparable to exercise in cited research — explicitly suggested as an exercise substitute for wheelchair-bound/bedridden individuals. 🟡 In Finnish men, frequent sauna use was linked to lower cardiovascular and all-cause mortality over ~20 years (Laukkanen 2015); observational
- **Sweat itself costs energy:** roughly 0.5 calorie per few drops of sweat; a session producing ~500g of sweat costs roughly ~300 calories — comparable to running a few miles. 🔴 Evidence check: evaporating 500 g of sweat carries off about 290 kcal of heat, but in a sauna that heat comes mostly from the room, not from burning extra fuel; making sweat costs little energy, so a sauna session does not burn calories like running
- **Cortisol reduction:** four 12-minute sauna sessions reduced cortisol by 30% — directly reduces one of Ch.1's three primary insulin-resistance causes (stress). 🟡 Unverified

**Cold (shivering, cold immersion):**

- Shivering recruits essentially every skeletal muscle simultaneously (vs. exercise, which is often more localized) — increases fuel demand broadly, lowering glucose/insulin
- Activates brown fat directly (mechanism from Ch.2–4) — burns fat/glucose specifically to generate heat
- Can induce "beiging" of white subcutaneous fat, raising its metabolic rate toward (not to) brown fat levels

**Bikman's own lab finding — the insulin/ketone contrast on brown vs. white fat:**

- **Insulin makes brown fat act more like white fat** — slows its normally-high metabolic rate, consistent with insulin's general "stop wasting energy" signal
- **Ketones make white fat act more like brown fat** — in Bikman's own human fat-biopsy data, ketosis alone raised white (belly) fat's metabolic rate roughly 3x, comparable to full cold immersion. **A genuinely practical finding: for someone unwilling to do cold immersion, achieving ketosis produces a similar magnitude of white-fat metabolic activation.** 🔴 Lecturer's own lab data; not independently verified.

## The Homework — Specific Numeric Targets (Bikman's Own Words)

| Marker | Target | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Fasting insulin | **~6 µIU/mL** or below |  |
| HOMA-IR | **~1.5**, adjusted by ethnicity (closer to 1 or 2 for some groups) |  |
| Adipose Insulin Resistance Score | Men <5, Women <8 | Needs fasting FFA/NEFA — not on standard panels |
| TG/HDL ratio | **~1.5**, ethnicity-adjusted |  |
| Coronary Artery Calcium (CAC) score | **~0** | Bikman's explicit recommendation: get this *before* starting a lipid-lowering medication, to know if plaque actually exists |
| Morning ketones (finger-stick meter) | **≥3 mmol/L**, several mornings/week | If this is not reached within a week of controlling dinner/snacking, insulin is likely still too high |

This table gives you a genuinely complete numeric scorecard.

🟡 Evidence check on the table: these are Bikman's optimal targets, tighter than standard lab ranges (see the Ch.5 evidence checks). A morning ketone target of ≥3 mmol/L is high — nutritional ketosis is commonly described from about 0.5 mmol/L — and ketone targets need medical supervision for anyone on SGLT2 inhibitors or with type 1 diabetes. US guidelines endorse a coronary artery calcium (CAC) score to guide statin decisions in intermediate-risk adults (Grundy 2019).

## Q&A Highlights — Several Directly Resolve Open Threads From Earlier Chapters

**Carnivore diet:** Bikman is explicit — **no rigorous published clinical trials exist**; his endorsement is anecdotal, though he considers the anecdotal pattern (rapid resolution of psoriasis, hypertension, infertility markers) compelling. He frames it as a legitimate, complete way of eating (especially ruminant meat) but flags social/family sustainability as the real constraint, not nutritional adequacy. Explicitly dismisses fiber as an essential nutrient in this same answer. 🟢 Fiber intake is consistently linked to better outcomes (Reynolds 2019).

**The "plateau" question — directly resolves Ch.5's diet section:** a plateau on low-carb is not the diet "stopping working" — it's the designed signal to *layer in* intermittent fasting on top of the now fat-adapted metabolism, per the Order of Operations principle above.

**Ethnicity and low-carb response:** Bikman states there is not controlled cross-ethnic low-carb trial data, but pushes back directly on the "some ethnicities are adapted to high-carb diets" assumption — pointing out that the *most* carbohydrate-dependent cultures (Asia, Middle East) show the *highest*, not lowest, rates of insulin resistance globally, the opposite of what a "well-adapted" hypothesis would predict. Explicitly labeled as his own reasoned speculation, not direct trial evidence.

**Why low-carb eaters can "fail" an OGTT — genuinely important, avoids a real misinterpretation trap:**

- Insulin secretion has two phases: **first-phase** (pre-packaged insulin the beta cells release immediately) and **second-phase** (newly synthesized insulin)
- Just **8 hours** of carbohydrate restriction is enough for beta cells to down-regulate their first-phase reserve, since it is not needed. 🟡 Evidence check: the 8-hour figure and this mechanism are unverified, but the testing pitfall is real — standard OGTT instructions call for at least 3 days of unrestricted diet with more than 150 g of carbohydrate per day beforehand (WHO 1999)
- Result: a low-carb eater given a sudden glucose load will show a *slower* initial insulin response — mimicking insulin resistance on a standard OGTT, despite being **more** insulin sensitive overall
- **Practical fix Bikman states directly:** eat some carbs in the ~6 hours before a scheduled OGTT to "remind" the beta cells to have first-phase insulin ready — genuinely useful and non-obvious for anyone tested this way while eating low-carb. 🟡 Standard preparation is longer: ≥150 g/day of carbohydrate for 3 days, plus a 30–50 g carbohydrate meal the evening before (WHO 1999)

**Food allergies and insulin resistance:** confirmed connection — food hypersensitivity triggers an immune/cytokine response, which is Ch.1's second primary cause (inflammation) directly — repeated exposure to a triggering food would produce recurring insulin resistance flares tied to each exposure. 🔴 Plausible via inflammation, but unverified.

**Earliest possible detection of brain insulin resistance:** reduced brain glucose uptake has been measured as early as a person's **20s–30s** (🟢 e.g., in young women with PCOS — Castellano 2015), well before any cognitive symptoms — but this specific test (direct brain glucose imaging) remains an academic research tool, not a clinically available test. Bikman's practical fallback: use standard insulin-resistance surrogate markers as the best currently-accessible proxy for brain-level risk, since the gold-standard direct test is not available outside research settings.

## Key Corrections to Carry Forward

- Assuming a low-carb eater failing an OGTT means they have become insulin resistant: it's frequently a first-phase insulin adaptation artifact from carb restriction itself, resolved by eating carbs briefly before the test — a real, non-obvious testing pitfall.
- Assuming skipping breakfast is metabolically equivalent to skipping dinner for intermittent fasting: cited studies show these are NOT equivalent — skipping dinner is metabolically superior despite being socially harder. 🟡 Lower hunger, yes; extra weight loss is not consistently shown (Ruddick-Collins 2022).
- Assuming "eat less and exercise more" is a reasonable starting strategy: per Bikman's explicit framing, doing this before addressing insulin sets up the exact conditions for hunger-driven failure.
- Assuming protein needs decrease with age: this chapter states the opposite (🟢 consistent with expert recommendations for older adults — Bauer 2013).

**2. Given the GLP-1 secretion data (saturated/monounsaturated fat raises it 15–20x, polyunsaturated fat only half to a third as much) — how does this change the case for "do not fear fat" beyond just avoiding seed-oil harm?**

A. It does not add anything beyond what was already established about avoiding seed oils

C. It means seed oils are actually preferable since they produce a smaller hormone spike

*Why B: this is new mechanistic detail this chapter adds — it upgrades "do not fear fat" from a defensive claim (fat is not the villain) to an active one (fat is doing something beneficial).*
