
# Bikman — Why We Get Sick, Ch.6: Mastering Insulin Control (Finale)
*This closing lecture lays out the four dietary pillars in their proper sequence, temperature-based interventions, and the specific numeric homework targets for reversing insulin resistance.*

*Source: Dr. Benjamin Bikman, Peterson Academy course "Why We Get Sick," Lecture 6 + Q&A (final lecture, transcript). This closes the six-chapter arc: what insulin resistance is (Ch.1–2), why it matters (Ch.3–4), and now the full practical implementation (Ch.5–6).*

**In plain words:** this last chapter is the step-by-step plan. Bikman's main rule is to lower insulin first, by choosing whole, fiber-rich carbohydrates, eating enough protein and not fearing natural fats, and only then, if still needed, to eat less or fast, because starting with hunger is a battle hunger usually wins. It also covers sauna and cold exposure, his number targets for blood tests, and a testing trap for people who eat low-carb. His targets are stricter than standard lab ranges, as marked below.

**Words in this note:**
- **OGTT**: oral glucose tolerance test: you drink sugar and blood sugar is checked over two hours
- **WHO**: World Health Organization
- **GLP-1**: a gut hormone that lowers appetite and blood sugar; also a class of drugs (such as semaglutide)
- **HOMA-IR**: a score calculated from fasting sugar and fasting insulin to estimate insulin resistance
- **TG**: triglycerides, the main fat in the blood
- **HDL**: high-density lipoprotein, the "good" cholesterol carrier
- **CAC**: coronary artery calcium score, a heart scan for hardened plaque
- **SGLT2**: a pump in the kidney that takes sugar back into the blood; also a class of diabetes drugs that block it
- **PCOS**: polycystic ovary syndrome, a hormone condition in women
- **ApoB**: apolipoprotein B; counts all the cholesterol particles that can clog arteries
- **TZD**: thiazolidinediones, a class of diabetes drugs

## Why This Matters

This is the real "how to" chapter. It gives the four pillars of eating in a specific, deliberate order, plus steps that use heat and cold, plus the specific number targets ("homework") that finally put concrete goals on everything built up over the five earlier chapters.

## The Central Organizing Principle — Order of Operations

**Bikman's main criticism of 60 years of standard diet advice:** "eat less and exercise more" as a *first* step is, literally, the textbook recipe for being as hungry as possible before a big meal. He spells this out with the picture of someone arriving starved at a buffet. Doing this *first* (rather than lowering insulin first) sets up a battle with hunger that, with food everywhere, hunger will win in the end.

**The corrected order:** lower insulin first (through the *kind* of carbohydrate eaten, not just by cutting the amount) → this unlocks the body's own stored fat as fuel it can use → *then*, if still needed, add a deliberate cut in energy (ideally through intermittent fasting, eating only within set hours) once the body is already used to burning fat and is truly less hungry. This is Bikman's answer to the "plateau" question in the Q&A below: low-carb does not "stop working"; a plateau is exactly the point to add fasting, not to give up.

## Punchlines & Quotable Facts (Lecture 6)

- **"Hunger always wins."** Stated as nearly a law of human behavior when food is everywhere. It is the main reason to lower insulin before cutting energy.
- **"If God wanted us to drink an apple, then the apple would have come in a little slurpy form from the tree."** Bikman's case against juicing or blending whole fruit.
- **"That's how God intended them to be. In our hubris, we have pulled them apart."** On eating protein and fat together rather than separately (for example, lean protein powder on its own).
- **"There is no such thing as an essential carbohydrate."** Repeated from Ch.2, now backed by a statement from the National Academy of Sciences that the lower limit for carbohydrate in the diet is zero. 🟢 The Institute of Medicine wrote that the lowest intake compatible with life "apparently is zero" if enough protein and fat are eaten, but it still set a recommended daily amount (RDA) of 130 g a day, based on how much sugar the brain uses on average (IOM 2005).
- **"We do need blood glucose. We do not need dietary carbohydrate."** The key difference Bikman wants people to stop mixing up: the body needs sugar in the blood, but it can make that sugar itself.
- **Just 8 hours without carbohydrate is enough to lose "first-phase" insulin release** (the quick first burst of insulin). This explains why someone eating low-carb can temporarily "fail" a sugar tolerance test (OGTT) even though they are more insulin sensitive, not less. 🟡 The 8-hour figure and the first-phase mechanism are not verified; see the evidence check in the Q&A section below (WHO 1999).
- **A 12-minute sauna session, four times, lowered cortisol (a stress hormone) by 30%.** 🟡 Not verified.
- **Whatever manna was to the Israelites, "it was not fiber."** Bikman's blunt dismissal of fiber as a necessary nutrient (Q&A). 🟢 Evidence check: in meta-analyses (pooled studies), people eating the most fiber had 15–30% lower rates of death, heart disease, stroke, type 2 diabetes and bowel cancer than those eating the least (Reynolds 2019).

## The Four Pillars, In Detail

**1. Control Carbohydrates**

- Not "zero carbs." Favor **whole** fruits and vegetables (with their fiber intact) over grains, bread, cereal and rice, which raise insulin much more.
- **Never juice or blend.** Removing the fiber removes the natural brake on how fast sugar and starch reach the blood.
- **The order of eating matters, whatever the meal:** in a study he cited, eating the protein and fat *before* the carbohydrate (rather than carbohydrate first) cut the rise in blood sugar by **50%** for the *same* meal. A truly free step that costs nothing. 🟢 In a small study of adults with type 2 diabetes, eating vegetables and protein before carbohydrate clearly lowered blood sugar and insulin after the meal (Shukla 2015); 🟡 the size of the drop varies with the time it is measured.
- **Fermentation to lower carbohydrate:** bacteria eat the starches and sugars in fermented foods (dairy, vegetables) without touching the protein or fat. They also produce helpful short-chain fats as a by-product.
- **Apple cider vinegar (acetic acid, a short-chain fatty acid with 2 carbons):** it softens the rise in blood sugar by changing how starch and sugar are digested *and* by speeding up the uptake of sugar by muscle. Bikman calls it one of the most overlooked "hacks" with real evidence behind it. 🟢 A meta-analysis found that vinegar lowered blood sugar and insulin after meals (Shishehbor 2017).
- **Sweeteners, ranked:** sugar alcohols (xylitol, erythritol and others) raise insulin somewhat, less than sugar; allulose, aspartame, stevia and monk fruit have essentially no effect. But watch for dextrose (a sugar) added as filler in packaged stevia products in particular, which can defeat the purpose of choosing it.

**2. Prioritize Protein**

- Target: **about 1.5 g per kg of body weight** (use your ideal body weight if you are well overweight). 🟡 This is above the recommended daily amount of 0.8 g/kg; an expert group recommends 1.0–1.2 g/kg a day for healthy older adults, and more with illness (Bauer 2013).
- Protein needs **rise with age.** The body gets worse over time at turning the protein eaten into muscle and bone protein, so a 65-year-old needs *more* protein than a 25-year-old for the same effect, not less. 🟢 This "anabolic resistance" in older age is well documented (Bauer 2013).
- Animal protein beats any plant protein, even soy (the best plant option), because it has all the amino acids (protein building blocks) and no anti-nutrients (phytic acid, tannins, trypsin inhibitors, substances that interfere with digestion).
- **Fermented plant proteins are a real exception:** fermentation lowers the anti-nutrients and the heavy metals. (An outside analysis he cited found plant protein powders were the worst for lead and arsenic contamination; 🟡 the analysis was not identified; not verified.)
- **Eat protein WITH fat, not alone:** a 1:1 ratio of protein to fat (as in a whole egg) roughly **doubles** muscle building compared with protein alone, in the human studies he cited. 🟡 Evidence check: after strength exercise, whole eggs stimulated more muscle protein building than egg whites with the same protein (van Vliet 2017); the "double" figure is not verified. A historical parallel: "rabbit starvation" among early explorers who ate only very lean meat. They got severe diarrhea because, he says, protein cannot be digested properly without fat, because of how bile acids (digestive juices from the liver) work. 🟡 The bile-acid explanation is not verified; "protein poisoning" is usually put down to the liver's limited ability to get rid of the nitrogen from protein.

**3. Do not Fear Fat**

- Natural fats (animal fats, and fats from fruits like coconut, avocado and olive) are fine. They contain all three kinds of fat (saturated, monounsaturated and polyunsaturated) with naturally little polyunsaturated fat (PUFA). The concern is specifically *refined seed oils*, not fat in general, in line with Ch.2–3.
- **A new mechanism this chapter adds:** saturated and monounsaturated fats raise the release of **GLP-1** (a gut hormone that helps control blood sugar and appetite) 15–20 times above its starting level; pure polyunsaturated fat gives at best a half to a third of that GLP-1 response. This is a direct, new reason why natural fats support the same hormone (GLP-1) that the drugs in Ch.5 are designed to copy. So "do not fear fat" is not only about avoiding harm; fat actively supports a helpful, insulin-sensitizing pathway. 🟡 Evidence check: the 15–20-times figure is not verified. In healthy adults, an olive-oil (monounsaturated) meal raised GLP-1 more than a butter (saturated) meal (Thomsen 1999), and the body's own GLP-1 rises after meals are far smaller than the levels the drugs reach (Drucker 2017).

**4. Watch the Clock (cutting energy: last, and optional)**

- The minimum version: stop snacking, especially in the evening. This in effect creates "mini-fasts" around three proper meals.
- **Fasting through breakfast vs. fasting through dinner: not the same, according to studies he cited.** Fasting through breakfast (skipping breakfast, eating lunch and dinner) increases hunger and fat-making the next day; fasting through dinner (normal breakfast and lunch, skipping dinner or keeping it small) leaves people more satisfied the next day. 🟡 Evidence check: in a 4-week trial where people tried both in turn, a big breakfast lowered hunger but did not change energy burned or weight loss compared with a big dinner (Ruddick-Collins 2022); an earlier trial found more weight loss with a big breakfast (Jakubowicz 2013).
- **"Breakfast like a king, lunch like a prince, dinner like a pauper."** Bikman's summary. He admits it is harder socially (family dinners) but says it is better for metabolism.
- **Same-calorie confirmation:** even with the same total calories, a low-fat, high-carbohydrate early eating window led to clearly more hunger through the day than a low-carb version. The same theme as Ch.5's same-calorie study, now shown again for meal timing.
- **Fasting schedules mentioned:** 18:6 (an 18-hour fast and a 6-hour eating window), OMAD (one meal a day), 1:1 (fasting 24 hours every other day), or 5:2 (5 days of normal eating and 2 days a week of very little food, roughly 500–650 kcal; not 48-hour fasts) (Harvie 2011). Named but not compared in depth in this part of the transcript.

## Temperature Interventions — Both Directions Work

**Heat (sauna, hot bath):**

- Improves blood flow about as much as exercise, in research he cited. He suggests it as a substitute for exercise for people in wheelchairs or confined to bed. 🟡 In Finnish men, frequent sauna use was linked to lower death rates from heart and blood vessel disease and from all causes over about 20 years (Laukkanen 2015); observational.
- **He says sweating itself costs energy:** roughly 0.5 calorie for a few drops of sweat; a session producing about 500g of sweat costs roughly 300 calories, similar to running a few miles. 🔴 Evidence check: evaporating 500 g of sweat carries off about 290 kcal of heat, but in a sauna that heat comes mostly from the hot room, not from burning extra fuel. Making sweat costs little energy, so a sauna session does not burn calories like running.
- **Lower cortisol:** four 12-minute sauna sessions lowered cortisol by 30%. That directly reduces one of Ch.1's three primary causes of insulin resistance (stress). 🟡 Not verified.

**Cold (shivering, cold-water immersion):**

- Shivering uses nearly every skeletal muscle at once (while exercise often uses only some muscles). That raises the demand for fuel throughout the body, lowering blood sugar and insulin.
- It switches on brown fat directly (the mechanism from Ch.2–4), which burns fat and sugar specifically to make heat.
- It can cause "beiging" of the white fat under the skin, raising its energy burning toward (not up to) the level of brown fat.

**A finding from Bikman's own lab: insulin and ketones have opposite effects on brown and white fat**

- **Insulin makes brown fat act more like white fat.** It slows brown fat's normally high energy burning, in line with insulin's general "stop wasting energy" signal.
- **Ketones make white fat act more like brown fat.** In Bikman's own data from samples of human fat, ketosis alone raised the energy burning of white (belly) fat about 3 times, similar to full cold-water immersion. **A truly practical finding: for someone unwilling to do cold-water immersion, getting into ketosis switches on white fat by a similar amount.** 🔴 The lecturer's own lab data; not independently verified.

## The Homework — Specific Numeric Targets (Bikman's Own Words)

| Marker | Target | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Fasting insulin | **About 6 µIU/mL** or below |  |
| HOMA-IR | **About 1.5**, adjusted for ethnic background (closer to 1 or 2 for some groups) |  |
| Adipose (fat tissue) Insulin Resistance Score | Men under 5, women under 8 | Needs a fasting free fatty acid test (FFA, also called NEFA), which is not on standard blood panels |
| TG/HDL ratio | **About 1.5**, adjusted for ethnic background |  |
| Coronary Artery Calcium (CAC) score (a scan for calcium in the heart arteries) | **About 0** | Bikman's clear advice: get this *before* starting a cholesterol-lowering medicine, to know whether plaque is actually there |
| Morning ketones (finger-prick meter) | **3 mmol/L or more**, several mornings a week | If this is not reached within a week of controlling dinner and snacks, insulin is probably still too high |

This table gives a truly complete scorecard of numbers.

🟡 Evidence check on the table: these are Bikman's ideal targets, stricter than standard lab ranges (see the Ch.5 evidence checks). A morning ketone target of 3 mmol/L or more is high; nutritional ketosis is usually described from about 0.5 mmol/L. Ketone targets need a doctor's supervision for anyone taking SGLT2 inhibitors or with type 1 diabetes. US guidelines support using a coronary artery calcium (CAC) score to guide decisions about statins in adults at medium risk (Grundy 2019).

## Q&A Highlights — Several Directly Resolve Open Threads From Earlier Chapters

**The carnivore diet (eating only animal foods):** Bikman is clear that **no rigorous published medical trials exist.** His support rests on stories from people, though he finds the pattern in those stories convincing (psoriasis, high blood pressure and fertility markers clearing up quickly). He presents it as a real, complete way of eating (especially meat from cattle, sheep and goats), but says the true limit is whether a person can keep it up socially and with family, not whether it gives enough nutrition. In the same answer he dismisses fiber as a necessary nutrient. 🟢 Eating fiber is consistently linked to better health (Reynolds 2019).

**The "plateau" question (this settles Ch.5's diet section):** a plateau on low-carb does not mean the diet has "stopped working." It is the planned signal to *add* intermittent fasting on top of a body now used to burning fat, following the Order of Operations above.

**Ethnic background and the response to low-carb:** Bikman says there are no controlled trials of low-carb across different ethnic groups. But he pushes back directly on the belief that "some ethnic groups are adapted to high-carbohydrate diets." He points out that the cultures *most* dependent on carbohydrate (Asia, the Middle East) have the *highest*, not the lowest, rates of insulin resistance in the world, the opposite of what you would expect if they were "well adapted." He clearly labels this as his own reasoned guess, not direct trial evidence.

**Why low-carb eaters can "fail" a sugar tolerance test (OGTT): truly important, and it avoids a real misreading**

- Insulin is released in two phases: the **first phase** (ready-made insulin that the beta cells release at once) and the **second phase** (newly made insulin).
- Just **8 hours** without carbohydrate is enough, he says, for the beta cells to scale back their first-phase reserve, since it is not needed. 🟡 Evidence check: the 8-hour figure and this mechanism are not verified, but the testing pitfall is real. Standard OGTT instructions ask for at least 3 days of normal eating, with more than 150 g of carbohydrate a day, beforehand (WHO 1999).
- Result: a low-carb eater suddenly given a load of sugar shows a *slower* first insulin response. On a standard OGTT this looks like insulin resistance, even though the person is **more** insulin sensitive overall.
- **The practical fix Bikman states:** eat some carbohydrate in the roughly 6 hours before a planned OGTT to "remind" the beta cells to have first-phase insulin ready. Truly useful and not obvious for anyone tested this way while eating low-carb. 🟡 Standard preparation is longer: at least 150 g a day of carbohydrate for 3 days, plus a meal with 30–50 g of carbohydrate the evening before (WHO 1999).

**Food allergies and insulin resistance:** he confirms a link. A food sensitivity sets off an immune and cytokine response, which is directly Ch.1's second primary cause (inflammation). Eating a trigger food again and again would cause repeated flares of insulin resistance, one with each exposure. 🔴 Believable through inflammation, but not verified.

**The earliest possible sign of insulin resistance in the brain:** lower sugar uptake in the brain has been measured as early as a person's **20s–30s** (🟢 for example, in young women with PCOS, Castellano 2015), long before any problems with memory or thinking. But this specific test (direct imaging of sugar use in the brain) is still a research tool, not a test available in clinics. Bikman's practical fallback: use the standard stand-in markers of insulin resistance as the best available clue to risk in the brain, since the gold-standard direct test is not available outside research.

## Key Corrections to Carry Forward

- Thinking that a low-carb eater who fails an OGTT has become insulin resistant: it is often a side effect of the first-phase insulin adapting to low carbohydrate, fixed by eating carbohydrate briefly before the test. A real, non-obvious testing pitfall.
- Thinking skipping breakfast is the same, metabolically, as skipping dinner for intermittent fasting: the studies he cited show they are NOT the same; skipping dinner is better for metabolism, though harder socially. 🟡 Less hunger, yes; extra weight loss is not consistently shown (Ruddick-Collins 2022).
- Thinking "eat less and exercise more" is a sensible first step: according to Bikman, doing this before tackling insulin sets up exactly the conditions for hunger to win.
- Thinking protein needs go down with age: this chapter says the opposite (🟢 in line with expert advice for older adults, Bauer 2013).

## Practical Takeaway

Lower insulin first by changing the kind of carbohydrate, putting protein first and not fearing natural fat, and only then, if still needed, add fasting or eating less, because starting with hunger is a battle hunger usually wins.

**1. Just 8 hours without carbohydrate can cause a temporary "first-phase insulin" effect on an OGTT. If someone eating low-carb were to have an OGTT, what would this chapter suggest doing first?**

A. Nothing; the test result would be accurate whatever the recent diet
B. *Eating some carbohydrate in the roughly 6 hours before the test, following Bikman's practical advice. Otherwise the result may wrongly look like resistance, reflecting beta cells that have adapted, not true insulin resistance
C. Fasting for 24 hours or more beforehand to get the most accurate low-insulin starting point

*Why B: this is a real, specific testing pitfall stated directly in the Q&A.*

**2. Given the GLP-1 data (saturated and monounsaturated fat raise it 15–20 times, polyunsaturated fat only a half to a third as much), how does this change the case for "do not fear fat," beyond just avoiding the harm from seed oils?**

A. It adds nothing beyond what was already known about avoiding seed oils
B. *It recasts eating natural fat as actively supporting a specific helpful hormone pathway (GLP-1), the same pathway the drugs in Ch.5 are designed to copy, rather than just "fat is not as harmful as once thought"
C. It means seed oils are actually better, since they cause a smaller hormone rise

*Why B: this is a new mechanism added by this chapter. It upgrades "do not fear fat" from a defensive claim (fat is not the villain) to an active one (fat is doing something helpful).*
