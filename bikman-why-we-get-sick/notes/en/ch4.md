
# Bikman — Why We Get Sick, Ch.4: Challenging the Origins of Chronic Disease
*This lecture extends the insulin resistance disease list to Alzheimer's, PCOS, erectile dysfunction/low testosterone, and cancer, challenging the amyloid plaque theory and detailing the insulin/aromatase and Warburg-effect mechanisms behind each.*

*Source: Dr. Benjamin Bikman, Peterson Academy course "Why We Get Sick," Lecture 4 + Q&A (transcript). Completes the disease list from Ch.1's scope-setting claim: Alzheimer's/dementia, migraines, PCOS, erectile dysfunction/low testosterone, and cancer.*

This chapter takes the same "is it cause or bystander?" question Ch.3 asked about LDL and applies it to amyloid plaques in Alzheimer's — with genuinely startling regulatory and research-integrity history behind it.

- **In November 2020 an FDA advisory panel voted 10 to 0 (1 uncertain) that the evidence did not show an Alzheimer's drug works — the FDA approved it anyway in June 2021**, reportedly in part to avoid discouraging other manufacturers. 🔴 That motive is unverified; the FDA cited amyloid-plaque reduction under its accelerated-approval pathway (Alexander 2021).

## Alzheimer's Disease — A Direct Challenge to the Amyloid Plaque Theory

**Framing note, same caveat as Ch.3's LDL section:** this is Bikman's minority-position synthesis, presented with real regulatory/research-integrity evidence — not a settled consensus replacement for the amyloid hypothesis, which remains the dominant model in mainstream neurology despite the problems cited here.

**Problem 1 — Plaques do not track with symptoms:**

- Autopsy studies find people who died with **no cognitive decline** but heavy plaque burden, and people who died with **confirmed Alzheimer's** but minimal plaques. Plaque presence does not reliably predict who had the disease.

**Problem 2 — The 2021 aducanumab (Aduhelm) FDA approval:**

- An independent FDA advisory panel of experts reviewed the drug in November 2020, found the evidence that it slowed cognitive decline **unconvincing**, and voted against it (10 of 11 members; price was not part of the vote)
- The FDA **overruled its own expert panel** and approved it anyway in June 2021, reportedly partly to avoid discouraging other companies from pursuing Alzheimer's drug development (well-documented, publicly reported controversy). 🟢 The approval used the accelerated pathway based on plaque reduction (Alexander 2021); three panel members resigned in protest, and Biogen discontinued the drug in 2024
- The drug does reduce plaques effectively — but that was explicitly *not* shown to translate into the only outcome that actually matters (cognition)

**Problem 3 — An influential 2006 study may have been fraudulent:**

- A 2022 Science magazine investigative report found evidence that images in an influential 2006 study of one specific amyloid form (Aβ*56) appear to have been **falsified**; the paper was retracted in 2024 (Lesné 2006) (this is a real, widely-reported scientific scandal, independently confirmed in subsequent reporting beyond just this lecture)
- This does not mean amyloid plaques play zero role, but it undermines a foundational pillar the field built substantial research and drug-development investment on. 🟡 Evidence check: the amyloid hypothesis predates this paper (early 1990s) and rests on genetics and many other studies; newer anti-amyloid antibodies modestly slowed clinical decline over 18 months, with risks of brain swelling and bleeding (van Dyck 2023; Sims 2023)

**The alternative Bikman proposes — Alzheimer's as brain insulin resistance:**

- **APOE4 gene variant** carries a well-established, repeatedly replicated higher Alzheimer's risk — about 3x with one copy and about 15x with two, in white populations (Farrer 1997) — conventionally explained as reduced plaque clearance
- Bikman's alternative explanation: APOE4 may instead directly disrupt insulin receptor function in the brain, independent of its plaque-clearing role (his interpretation, not yet the dominant explanation). 🔴
- **Brain glucose uptake is measurably reduced (~13%) even in early cognitive decline** — a genuine, quantified finding. 🟢 Early regional glucose hypometabolism with preserved ketone uptake is well described (Cunnane 2020); 🟡 the exact 13% figure was not verified
- **Bikman's own lab's study** (postmortem brains, matched for age/sex/ethnicity/obesity/lifestyle, comparing confirmed Alzheimer's vs. no Alzheimer's): every gene involved in the brain's glucose-burning machinery was significantly suppressed in Alzheimer's brains; genes for ketone-burning were completely normal. A direct, controlled comparison — stronger evidence tier than the observational plaque studies above. 🟡 Evidence check: the published study (RNA sequencing of postmortem brains from three brain banks) found glucose-burning genes impaired; ketone-burning genes were unchanged in neurons, astrocytes and microglia but impaired in oligodendrocytes (Saito 2021). It measures gene activity, not actual fuel use.
- **Finland cross-sectional study**: only 8 variables significantly predicted Alzheimer's risk across a large population — age, education, APOE4 genotype, and **five separate metabolic/glucose-handling markers**. Correlational, but a striking, simple result: metabolic markers dominated the list. 🟡 Unverified — the specific study could not be identified.
- Bikman's preferred framing: **"Alzheimer's disease is insulin resistance of the brain"** — he explicitly dislikes the popular "type 3 diabetes" label as needlessly complicating a simpler statement.

## Ketones — The Brain's Preferred (Not Backup) Fuel

- **What a ketone actually is:** a byproduct of sustained fat-burning. When insulin stays low for roughly 12–16 hours, fat-burning runs high enough that the liver starts converting the excess into ketones.
- **Critical correction:** "the brain needs/prefers glucose" is **demonstrably false**. In a fasted state, glucose may be present at ~5 mmol/L and ketones at only ~2 mmol/L — yet the brain draws roughly 65–70% of its energy from the smaller ketone pool. The brain actively prefers ketones when both are available, it does not just fall back on them. Direct experimental measurement, not inference. 🔴 Evidence check: the classic measurement was made after 5–6 weeks of total starvation, when blood ketones are far higher than ~2 mmol/L; then ketones replaced glucose as the main brain fuel (Owen 1967). At the levels reached by overnight fasting or a typical ketogenic diet, ketones supply a smaller share, roughly in proportion to blood level, and the brain always needs some glucose (Cunnane 2020).
- **Why this matters for Alzheimer's specifically:** ketone-burning machinery stays fully intact even when glucose-burning genes are suppressed (per Bikman's own lab study above) — meaning a hungry, insulin-resistant brain still has a working alternative fuel pathway, if ketones are actually present.
- **The catch:** most people rarely have meaningful ketones available, because chronic hyperinsulinemia (the modern eating-pattern problem from Ch.2–3) keeps fat-burning, and therefore ketone production, permanently suppressed.

**Real interventions cited (with appropriate caveats):**

- **Coconut oil case study:** documented improvement (not cure) in Alzheimer's cognitive testing (clock-drawing test) and functional independence (dressing self) from adding coconut oil to the diet. A case study — illustrative, not a controlled trial.
- **Exogenous ketone supplements:** shown to improve cognition acutely in Alzheimer's patients. Bikman is explicit both times: improvement, not reversal or cure. 🟡 Small RCTs: a medium-chain triglyceride drink improved cognitive scores at 45 days in mild-to-moderate Alzheimer's, mainly in APOE4 non-carriers (Henderson 2009).
- **Migraines:** share "brain glucose hypometabolism" with Alzheimer's; ketosis produces documented improvement, with published case reports dating back to **1929** — a genuinely long research history, not a recent fad. 🟡 Small studies; the exact year is unverified.

## Fat Chain Length — Why Some Fats Are More "Ketogenic" Than Others

| Type | Storage potential | Examples | Ketogenic tendency |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Long-chain** (most dietary fat) | Can be stored OR burned — insulin decides which | Meat, dairy, coconut/avocado/olive (mixed) | Lowest — depends entirely on insulin status |
| **Medium-chain** | Very little storage capacity — mostly forced to burn | Coconut oil, goat dairy (caprylic/capric/caproic — named for goat) | Higher — burned regardless of insulin |
| **Short-chain** | No storage capacity — must be burned | Products of fermentation (yogurt, kimchi, sour milk) | Highest — no alternative to burning |

Direct mechanistic explanation for why MCT oil has a disproportionate ketogenic/metabolic effect relative to its dose — it is not subject to insulin's storage-vs-burn decision the way long-chain fats are. 🟡 Evidence check: coconut oil is about half lauric acid (12 carbons), which is handled largely like a long-chain fat; purified MCT oil (8–10 carbons) is the strongly ketogenic form. Most short-chain fats in the body are made by gut bacteria from fiber, not eaten.

## Insulin Resistance and Young Women — Not Just an Aging Problem

- Study: women in their **20s** with higher insulin resistance showed **9–14% lower brain glucose consumption** in specific frontal, parietal and temporal regions (Castellano 2015) — in the same range as older adults with confirmed cognitive decline, despite these young women having no cognitive symptoms at all.
- These women were being studied for **PCOS**, not cognition — the metabolic/brain-glucose effect was an incidental finding, directly bridging into the fertility section.

## PCOS — The Aromatase Mechanism

- **PCOS clinical definition (Rotterdam criteria):** any two of three — elevated androgens (testosterone), infrequent periods (oligomenorrhea), and polycystic ovaries (Teede 2023) — all traced, per Bikman, to one underlying driver: insulin resistance. 🟡 Insulin resistance is common in PCOS and worsens it, but it is not present in every woman with PCOS and is one of several drivers (Diamanti-Kandarakis 2012).
- **The ovary's job:** aromatase enzyme normally converts testosterone into estrogens (all estrogens in both sexes are made from androgens — estradiol from testosterone, estrone from androstenedione).
- **What insulin does:** high insulin directly **inhibits aromatase** — testosterone cannot convert to estrogen. Result: ovaries can produce **up to 13x more testosterone** than normal, with a corresponding estrogen deficit. 🔴 Evidence check: the established mechanism is different — high insulin acts with LH to boost ovarian androgen production and lowers SHBG, raising free testosterone (Diamanti-Kandarakis 2012). The 13x figure is unverified.
- **Why ovulation fails:** the disrupted testosterone/estrogen ratio prevents ovulation — and without ovulation's "stand down" signal, developing follicles never resolve and accumulate cycle after cycle as cysts. This is the direct mechanistic origin of the disease's name.
- **Subtle but important finding:** women with PCOS can show *normal* whole-body insulin resistance markers while still having *significant* fat-cell-level insulin resistance — confirming (again) that fat tissue is the first domino, and whole-body measures can miss early-stage resistance entirely. Directly reinforces the Ch.1–2 tissue-cascade concept with a real clinical population. 🟡 The specific study was not verified.

## Erectile Dysfunction and Low Testosterone — Two Compounding Mechanisms

**1. Direct testicular suppression:** high insulin directly inhibits testosterone production at the testes themselves. The testes are not damaged — they are being chemically told to slow down. 🟡 Evidence check: worse insulin sensitivity tracks with lower testosterone output from the testes' Leydig cells (Pitteloud 2005), but in obesity the main drivers are lower SHBG, more conversion to estradiol in fat, and reduced LH signaling from the brain; weight loss raises testosterone (Corona 2013).

**2. Fat cells acting as an "ovary":** as fat cells hypertrophy, they begin expressing aromatase (the same enzyme from the PCOS section) — converting whatever testosterone is still being made into estrogen. A genuine double hit: less testosterone made, and more of what's made gets converted away.

**Bikman's direct claim on the modern low-T epidemic:** "It's not because the testes are broken... it's the insulin that's disrupting it" — framed as the primary explanation for population-wide testosterone decline, rather than assuming individual testicular failure. A strong causal claim presented with mechanistic support but not itself a randomized trial finding — worth holding as Bikman's synthesis rather than settled consensus. 🔴

**Dietary fat and testosterone:** multiple studies cited show low-fat diets correlate with lower testosterone and lower sperm production in men. 🟢 A meta-analysis of 6 intervention studies (206 men) found low-fat diets modestly lowered testosterone (Whittaker 2021); 🟡 the sperm claim is unverified.

**Erectile dysfunction as an early insulin-resistance warning sign:** same nitric-oxide/vasodilation mechanism as the hypertension section in Ch.3 — blood vessels in the penis require nitric oxide-driven dilation, and insulin-resistant vessels cannot produce enough. Bikman notes this is considered by some clinicians one of the **earliest** presenting signs of insulin resistance in otherwise-healthy young men. 🟢 Shared mechanism: insulin resistance impairs nitric-oxide-dependent vessel relaxation (Muniyappa 2013).

## Cancer — The Metabolic Theory (Warburg / Seyfried)

**Two competing theories:**

- **Genetic theory (mainstream):** cancer originates from nuclear DNA mutations causing uncontrolled growth
- **Metabolic theory (older than most assume, dating to Otto Warburg ~100 years ago):** cancer originates from damaged mitochondria

**The cell-swap (nuclear/mitochondrial transfer) experiments reviewed by Seyfried's group — genuinely elegant (Seyfried 2014):**

- Tumor mitochondria + healthy nucleus → cell became **cancerous**
- Healthy mitochondria + tumor nucleus → cell behaved **normally**, growth-regulated
- Directly challenges the pure-genetic model — the mitochondria, not the nucleus, determined the outcome in this experiment
- Proposed mechanism: damaged mitochondria produce oxidative stress (reactive oxygen species) → *that* damages the nucleus → producing the mutations conventionally blamed as the root cause. Nucleus mutations are real and relevant, but potentially downstream of a mitochondrial problem, not the origin. A specific causal chain proposed by this research group — not yet the dominant paradigm in oncology, but a serious, published line of research, not fringe speculation. 🔴 Minority view: the somatic-mutation model remains mainstream, and many cancer cells keep working mitochondria while still using the Warburg effect (Liberti 2016).

**The Warburg effect:**

- Cancer cells are stuck fermenting glucose outside the mitochondria (like bacteria do) rather than flexibly burning multiple fuels the way healthy cells can
- Cancer cells burn glucose at **~200x** the rate of normal cells (🟡 the 200x figure is unverified; the increase varies widely by tumor)
- This is the actual basis of PET scan cancer imaging: radio-labeled glucose concentrates wherever cancer cells are, because of this extreme glucose dependence

**Why insulin resistance specifically fuels this:**

- Hyperinsulinemia = a growth signal reaching every cell, cancer cells included
- Hyperglycemia = the fuel to match that growth signal, feeding the glucose-fermentation-dependent cancer cell
- **Breast tumors: ~7x more insulin receptors than normal breast tissue; prostate tumors similarly over-expressed** (🟢 more than 6x for breast cancer in Papa 1990) — cancer cells appear to actively upregulate their own insulin responsiveness.
- **Men with insulin resistance: 3x more likely to die from cancer.** Similar mortality increase in insulin-resistant women with breast cancer, independent of obesity, heart disease, or smoking status. 🟡 Evidence check: in US NHANES data, adults without diabetes whose fasting insulin was ≥10 µU/mL had about twice the cancer death rate (Tsujimoto 2017); observational. The 3x figure for men was not verified.

**Bikman's careful, explicit position (worth preserving verbatim in spirit):** insulin resistance is **not** claimed as the cause of cancer — some other event likely initiates it (possibly mitochondrial damage, per Seyfried). But insulin resistance clearly **accelerates** existing cancer significantly. "It maybe did not light the fire, but it sure as hell is pouring gasoline on it."

**Clinical application (from Q&A) — the glucose-ketone index:**

- Seyfried's group treats certain cancers (e.g., glioblastoma, an inoperable brain tumor) with therapeutic ketosis — driving glucose down to ~3.5 mmol/L while raising ketones to match or exceed that level, deliberately starving the glucose-dependent cancer cell
- Reported as "fantastically effective" in certain cancer types — Bikman frames this as not directly fixing the mitochondria, but starving the cancer cell of the one fuel it depends on. 🔴 Evidence check: trials in glioblastoma so far are small feasibility studies, and a 2025 systematic review could draw no firm conclusions about survival (McKerill 2025)

## Q&A Highlights

**Protein source (whey vs. plant) and insulin:** the insulin effect depends on amino acid composition, not protein source per se. Animal protein is described as reliably complete; plant proteins (even soy, called the most complete plant option) can be amino-acid-deficient and contain anti-nutrients (phytic acid, tannins, trypsin inhibitors) that impair digestion — partly mitigated by traditional fermentation.

**Exogenous ketone supplements — who actually benefits:** valuable specifically for people with cognitive decline/Alzheimer's risk (direct brain fuel benefit) or endurance athletes (extra fuel source) — but for a general overweight/diabetic person seeking metabolic improvement, the benefit comes from *lowering insulin itself*, not from ketones as a supplement; exogenous ketones do not replicate that insulin-lowering benefit. An important nuance — ketone supplementation is not a substitute for actually resolving hyperinsulinemia.

**Psychological/psychiatric disorders and insulin resistance:** Bikman references Dr. Chris Palmer's book *Brain Energy*, which extends the metabolic-brain-disease framework to schizophrenia, bipolar disorder, anxiety, and depression. Bikman calls this a genuine, known effect area, though it's outside his own direct research focus. 🔴 Emerging hypothesis; evidence is limited to small pilot studies.

**Insulin reference ranges across ethnicities:** Bikman states insulin target ranges do **not** differ by ethnicity or body type — unlike the personal fat threshold (which does vary), the actual insulin number itself should be interpreted the same way for everyone. Specific numeric ranges deferred to a later "solutions" lecture. 🟡 Contested: Ch.5 itself gives ethnicity-adjusted cutoffs for other markers, and marker performance differs by ethnicity (Sumner 2005).

## Key Corrections to Carry Forward

- Assuming amyloid plaques are a settled, established cause of Alzheimer's: real regulatory and research-integrity problems (the 2021 FDA approval over its panel's objection, the 2022 image-manipulation report and the 2024 retraction) complicate this more than commonly acknowledged, though it remains the dominant model in mainstream neurology.
- Assuming "the brain needs glucose": demonstrably false when ketones are available — the brain actively prefers ketones even when glucose is more abundant. 🔴 See the evidence check above: ketones become the main brain fuel only when levels are high (prolonged fasting), and the brain still needs some glucose (Owen 1967; Cunnane 2020).
- Assuming low testosterone reflects testicular failure: per this chapter, it's more often insulin actively suppressing testicular function and fat cells actively converting testosterone to estrogen — the testes themselves are typically not damaged.
- Assuming cancer is purely a nuclear-DNA disease: the Seyfried mitochondrial-swap experiment is a serious, published challenge to that assumption, though it has not displaced the genetic model as mainstream oncology's primary framework.

## Practical Takeaway

The MCT-oil mechanism now has a specific, named explanation: medium-chain fats have very limited storage capacity and are preferentially burned regardless of insulin status.

## Questions to Learn From (Socratic) — with Answer Options

**1. Given that ketone-burning genes stay normal in Alzheimer's brains while glucose-burning genes are suppressed (per Bikman's own lab study) — does this mean ketogenic interventions should be pursued as prevention regardless of current cognitive status?**

A. Yes — this proves everyone should adopt permanent ketosis to prevent Alzheimer's
B. *The finding supports ketones as a legitimate *alternative fuel pathway* worth understanding, particularly for anyone with insulin resistance risk factors, but Bikman himself frames existing interventions (coconut oil, exogenous ketones) as producing *improvement*, not *prevention* or *cure* — the evidence supports ketone availability as protective/supportive, not a proven preventive protocol
C. Ketones have no established relevance to brain health at any life stage

*Why B: it's important not to overstate a real, well-documented mechanism into a prevention guarantee that the cited evidence does not actually support.*

**2. Given the parallel Bikman draws between LDL-at-a-plaque (Ch.3) and amyloid-at-a-plaque (this chapter) — what's the key difference in evidence strength between these two "bystander" arguments?**

A. Both arguments have identical evidence strength and should be weighted equally
B. *The Alzheimer's case has additional research-integrity evidence (the 2022 fraud finding, the FDA panel override) that the LDL case does not have an equivalent for — though both remain minority positions relative to their respective mainstream models
C. The LDL case is stronger because cardiology guidelines are more established than neurology guidelines

*Why B: the two arguments share a similar logical structure but differ in their specific supporting evidence — worth not conflating them as equally supported just because they use the same rhetorical device.*

**3. Given that women with PCOS can show normal whole-body insulin resistance markers while having significant fat-cell-level resistance — what does this imply about relying on standard insulin/glucose testing to rule out insulin resistance as a contributor to a health issue?**

A. Normal whole-body insulin markers definitively rule out any insulin-resistance contribution
B. *Normal whole-body markers can miss early or tissue-specific insulin resistance, particularly at the fat-cell level where it originates — a normal result does not fully exclude insulin resistance as a contributing factor, especially for tissue-specific conditions
C. Whole-body insulin testing is completely useless and should never be performed

*Why B: this is the PCOS study's direct lesson — fat tissue as the "first domino" can be significantly affected before whole-body markers pick it up, a theme now confirmed across three separate populations (general insulin resistance progression, PCOS, and by extension possibly others).*
