
# Bikman — Why We Get Sick, Ch.4: Challenging the Origins of Chronic Disease
*This lecture extends the insulin resistance disease list to Alzheimer's, PCOS, erectile dysfunction/low testosterone, and cancer, challenging the amyloid plaque theory and detailing the insulin/aromatase and Warburg-effect mechanisms behind each.*

*Source: Dr. Benjamin Bikman, Peterson Academy course "Why We Get Sick," Lecture 4 + Q&A (transcript). Completes the disease list from Ch.1's scope-setting claim: Alzheimer's/dementia, migraines, PCOS, erectile dysfunction/low testosterone, and cancer.*

**In plain words:** Bikman argues that insulin resistance plays a large part in Alzheimer's disease ("insulin resistance of the brain"), in PCOS and low testosterone, and in how fast cancer grows. He questions the standard amyloid-plaque explanation of Alzheimer's, pointing to a disputed drug approval and a withdrawn study, and says the brain can run well on ketones, a fuel the body makes from fat. Several of these are minority views; the evidence checks next to each claim show where mainstream medicine agrees and where it does not.

**Words in this note:**
- **PCOS**: polycystic ovary syndrome, a hormone condition in women
- **LDL**: low-density lipoprotein, the "bad" cholesterol carrier
- **TRT**: testosterone replacement therapy
- **FDA**: U.S. Food and Drug Administration
- **APOE4**: a gene variant that raises Alzheimer's risk
- **RNA**: a working copy of genetic code
- **RCTs**: randomized controlled trials, the strongest kind of study for testing a treatment
- **MCT**: medium-chain triglycerides, fats the liver turns quickly into ketones
- **LH**: luteinizing hormone, a brain hormone that tells the testes or ovaries to make sex hormones
- **SHBG**: sex hormone binding globulin, a protein that holds sex hormones in the blood
- **DNA**: the genetic code inside each cell
- **NHANES**: a large U.S. government health and nutrition survey
- **DHEA-S**: the stored, blood-test form of DHEA

## Why This Matters

This chapter asks about amyloid plaques in Alzheimer's disease the same "cause or bystander?" question that Ch.3 asked about LDL cholesterol. Behind it lies a truly startling history of problems with drug approval and research honesty.

## Scope-Setting Claim — The Plaque Parallel to LDL

Bikman deliberately takes the "fire truck at a burning house" logic from Ch.3's LDL discussion and applies it to amyloid plaques (sticky protein clumps found in the brain) in Alzheimer's disease.

## Punchlines & Quotable Facts (Lecture 4)

- **"Alzheimer's disease... maybe trumps them all"** for how poorly it is understood, despite huge investment in research.
- **"It's not the testes that are broken... it's the insulin that's disrupting it."** Bikman's new way of seeing today's widespread low testosterone.
- **"Water, water everywhere, nor any drop to drink."** Bikman borrows Coleridge's line to describe a brain swimming in sugar it cannot use because of insulin resistance.
- **Breast tumors have 7 times more insulin receptors (the "docking stations" for insulin) than normal breast tissue; prostate tumors also have too many.** Cancer adapts itself to take advantage of high insulin. 🟢 Breast cancers had more than 6 times the insulin-receptor content of normal breast tissue (Papa 1990).
- **"It maybe did not light the fire, but it sure as hell is pouring gasoline on it."** Bikman's clear and careful position on insulin resistance and cancer: not claimed as the cause, but a major accelerant.
- **"If you are a man and you are trying to follow a low-fat or vegan diet, it's a self-correcting problem."** A dark joke about falling testosterone and fertility on very low-fat diets.
- **Cancer cells burn sugar about 200 times faster than normal cells (the Warburg effect).** 🟡 The 200-times figure is not verified; tumors take up much more sugar than normal tissue, but how much varies widely (Liberti 2016).
- **In November 2020 an FDA advisory panel voted 10 to 0 (1 uncertain) that the evidence did not show an Alzheimer's drug works. The FDA (the US agency that approves medicines) approved it anyway in June 2021,** reportedly partly so as not to discourage other drug makers. 🔴 That motive is not verified; the FDA pointed to the drug's reduction of amyloid plaques under its fast-track approval route (accelerated approval) (Alexander 2021).

## Alzheimer's Disease — A Direct Challenge to the Amyloid Plaque Theory

**A note on framing, with the same caution as Ch.3's LDL section:** this is Bikman's summary of a minority view, backed by real evidence about drug approval and research honesty. It is not a settled, agreed replacement for the amyloid hypothesis, which is still the main model in mainstream neurology (brain medicine) despite the problems listed here.

**Problem 1: plaques do not match symptoms**

- Studies of brains after death find people who died with **no loss of memory or thinking** but many plaques, and people who died with **confirmed Alzheimer's** but few plaques. Having plaques does not reliably show who had the disease.

**Problem 2: The 2021 aducanumab (Aduhelm) approval by the FDA**

- An independent FDA advisory panel of experts reviewed the drug in November 2020, found the evidence that it slowed the decline in memory and thinking **unconvincing**, and voted against it (10 of 11 members; price was not part of the vote).
- The FDA **overruled its own expert panel** and approved the drug anyway in June 2021, reportedly partly so as not to discourage other companies from developing Alzheimer's drugs (a well-documented, publicly reported controversy). 🟢 The approval used the fast-track route based on plaque reduction (Alexander 2021); three panel members resigned in protest, and Biogen (the maker) stopped selling the drug in 2024.
- The drug does reduce plaques well. But it was clearly *not* shown that this leads to the only result that actually matters: better memory and thinking.

**Problem 3: an influential 2006 study may have been fraudulent**

- A 2022 investigation by Science magazine found evidence that images in an influential 2006 study of one specific form of amyloid (Aβ*56) seem to have been **falsified**. The paper was retracted (officially withdrawn) in 2024 (Lesné 2006). This is a real, widely reported scientific scandal, confirmed in later reporting beyond this lecture.
- It does not mean amyloid plaques play no role at all, but it weakens a pillar on which the field built a great deal of research and drug development. 🟡 Evidence check: the amyloid hypothesis is older than this paper (early 1990s) and rests on genetics and many other studies; newer anti-amyloid antibody drugs slightly slowed the decline in memory and daily function over 18 months, with risks of brain swelling and bleeding (van Dyck 2023; Sims 2023).

**The alternative Bikman proposes: Alzheimer's as insulin resistance of the brain**

- The **APOE4 gene variant** carries a well-established, repeatedly confirmed higher risk of Alzheimer's: about 3 times with one copy and about 15 times with two, in white populations (Farrer 1997). The usual explanation is that it slows the clearing of plaques.
- Bikman's alternative explanation: APOE4 may instead directly interfere with how insulin receptors work in the brain, apart from any effect on clearing plaques (his interpretation, not yet the main explanation). 🔴
- **Sugar uptake by the brain is measurably lower (about 13%) even in early decline of memory and thinking.** A real, measured finding. 🟢 Lower sugar use in parts of the brain early on, while ketone uptake stays normal, is well described (Cunnane 2020); 🟡 the exact 13% figure was not verified.
- **A study from Bikman's own lab** (brains after death, matched for age, sex, ethnic background, obesity and lifestyle, comparing confirmed Alzheimer's with no Alzheimer's): every gene involved in the brain's sugar-burning machinery was clearly turned down in the Alzheimer's brains; the genes for burning ketones were completely normal. A direct, controlled comparison, a stronger kind of evidence than the observational plaque studies above. 🟡 Evidence check: the published study (RNA sequencing, which reads which genes are switched on, of brains from three brain banks) found sugar-burning genes impaired. Ketone-burning genes were unchanged in neurons (nerve cells), astrocytes and microglia (support and immune cells) but impaired in oligodendrocytes (the cells that insulate nerves) (Saito 2021). It measures gene activity, not how much fuel is actually used.
- **A Finnish study of a large population at one point in time:** only 8 factors clearly predicted Alzheimer's risk: age, education, APOE4 gene type, and **five separate markers of metabolism and sugar handling.** It shows links, not cause, but it is a striking, simple result: metabolic markers dominated the list. 🟡 Not verified; the specific study could not be identified.
- Bikman's preferred way of saying it: **"Alzheimer's disease is insulin resistance of the brain."** He openly dislikes the popular label "type 3 diabetes," which he feels complicates a simpler statement.

## Ketones — The Brain's Preferred (Not Backup) Fuel

- **What a ketone actually is:** a by-product of burning fat for a long stretch. When insulin stays low for roughly 12–16 hours, fat burning runs high enough that the liver starts turning the extra into ketones.
- **An important correction:** "the brain needs or prefers sugar" is, he says, **clearly false.** When fasting, blood sugar may be about 5 mmol/L and ketones only about 2 mmol/L, yet the brain takes roughly 65–70% of its energy from the smaller ketone supply. The brain actively prefers ketones when both are there; it does not just fall back on them. Direct measurement in experiments, not guesswork. 🔴 Evidence check: the classic measurement was made after 5–6 weeks of total starvation, when blood ketones are far higher than about 2 mmol/L; then ketones replaced sugar as the brain's main fuel (Owen 1967). At the levels reached after an overnight fast or on a typical ketogenic diet, ketones supply a smaller share, roughly in line with their level in the blood, and the brain always needs some sugar (Cunnane 2020).
- **Why this matters for Alzheimer's in particular:** the ketone-burning machinery stays fully intact even when sugar-burning genes are turned down (according to Bikman's own lab study above). So a hungry, insulin-resistant brain still has a working backup fuel route, if ketones are actually there.
- **The catch:** most people rarely have useful amounts of ketones, because insulin that stays high all the time (the modern eating pattern problem from Ch.2–3) keeps fat burning, and so ketone production, switched off.

**Real interventions he cites (with suitable cautions):**

- **A coconut oil case study:** documented improvement (not a cure) in Alzheimer's testing of memory and thinking (the clock-drawing test) and in independence in daily life (dressing oneself) after adding coconut oil to the diet. A single case: it illustrates, it is not a controlled trial.
- **Ketone supplements taken by mouth (exogenous ketones):** shown to improve memory and thinking in the short term in people with Alzheimer's. Bikman says clearly both times: improvement, not reversal or cure. 🟡 Small randomized trials: a drink of medium-chain triglycerides (MCT, a fat that is easily turned into ketones) improved memory and thinking scores at 45 days in mild to moderate Alzheimer's, mainly in people without APOE4 (Henderson 2009).
- **Migraines:** they share low sugar use in the brain with Alzheimer's; ketosis (the state of burning fat and making ketones) brings documented improvement, with published case reports going back to **1929**. A truly long research history, not a recent fad. 🟡 Small studies; the exact year is not verified.

## Fat Chain Length — Why Some Fats Are More "Ketogenic" Than Others

Fats differ by the length of their carbon chains, and that changes what the body does with them.

| Type | Can it be stored? | Examples | Tendency to make ketones |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Long-chain** (most fat in food) | Can be stored OR burned; insulin decides which | Meat, dairy, coconut, avocado and olive (mixed) | Lowest; depends entirely on insulin |
| **Medium-chain** | Very little storage; mostly forced to burn | Coconut oil, goat dairy (caprylic, capric and caproic acids, named after the goat) | Higher; burned whatever the insulin level |
| **Short-chain** | No storage; must be burned | Products of fermentation (yogurt, kimchi, sour milk) | Highest; burning is the only option |

This is a direct explanation of why MCT oil has a bigger effect on ketones and metabolism than its dose would suggest: unlike long-chain fats, it is not subject to insulin's "store or burn" decision. 🟡 Evidence check: coconut oil is about half lauric acid (12 carbons), which the body handles mostly like a long-chain fat; purified MCT oil (8–10 carbons) is the form that strongly makes ketones. Most short-chain fats in the body are made by gut bacteria from fiber, not eaten.

## Insulin Resistance and Young Women — Not Just an Aging Problem

- A study: women in their **20s** with more insulin resistance used **9–14% less sugar in the brain** in certain regions at the front, top and sides of the brain (frontal, parietal and temporal) (Castellano 2015). That is in the same range as older adults with confirmed decline in memory and thinking, even though these young women had no such symptoms at all.
- These women were being studied for **PCOS** (polycystic ovary syndrome), not for memory or thinking. The effect on brain sugar use was a chance finding, which leads straight into the fertility section.

## PCOS — The Aromatase Mechanism

- **The medical definition of PCOS (Rotterdam criteria):** any two of three: high androgens (male-type hormones such as testosterone), infrequent periods (oligomenorrhea), and ovaries with many small cysts (Teede 2023). According to Bikman, all three trace back to one underlying driver: insulin resistance. 🟡 Insulin resistance is common in PCOS and makes it worse, but it is not present in every woman with PCOS and is one of several drivers (Diamanti-Kandarakis 2012).
- **The ovary's job:** an enzyme called aromatase normally turns testosterone into estrogens (female hormones). All estrogens, in both sexes, are made from androgens: estradiol from testosterone, estrone from androstenedione.
- **What insulin does, according to Bikman:** high insulin directly **blocks aromatase**, so testosterone cannot be turned into estrogen. As a result, the ovaries can make **up to 13 times more testosterone** than normal, with a matching lack of estrogen. 🔴 Evidence check: the established mechanism is different. High insulin works together with LH (a brain hormone that drives the ovaries) to boost the ovaries' androgen production, and it lowers SHBG (a protein that holds testosterone inactive in the blood), raising free, active testosterone (Diamanti-Kandarakis 2012). The 13-times figure is not verified.
- **Why ovulation fails:** the upset balance of testosterone and estrogen prevents ovulation (the release of an egg). Without ovulation's "stand down" signal, the developing follicles (small fluid sacs that each hold an egg) never clear and build up cycle after cycle as cysts. This is the direct origin of the disease's name.
- **A subtle but important finding:** women with PCOS can show *normal* whole-body insulin resistance markers while still having *significant* insulin resistance in their fat cells. This confirms once again that fat tissue is the first domino, and that whole-body tests can miss early resistance entirely. It strongly supports the Ch.1–2 idea of a cascade from tissue to tissue, in a real group of patients. 🟡 The specific study was not verified.

## Erectile Dysfunction and Low Testosterone — Two Compounding Mechanisms

**1. Direct slowing of the testes:** high insulin directly holds back testosterone production in the testes themselves. The testes are not damaged; they are being chemically told to slow down. 🟡 Evidence check: worse insulin sensitivity goes with less testosterone from the testes' Leydig cells (the cells that make it) (Pitteloud 2005). But in obesity the main drivers are lower SHBG, more conversion to estradiol in fat, and weaker LH signals from the brain; weight loss raises testosterone (Corona 2013).

**2. Fat cells acting like an "ovary":** as fat cells swell, they start making aromatase (the same enzyme as in the PCOS section), turning whatever testosterone is still made into estrogen. A real double hit: less testosterone is made, and more of what is made is converted away.

**Bikman's direct claim about today's widespread low testosterone:** "It's not because the testes are broken... it's the insulin that's disrupting it." He presents this as the main explanation for falling testosterone across the population, rather than assuming each man's testes are failing. A strong claim about cause, with mechanisms to support it, but not itself the result of a randomized trial; worth holding as Bikman's summary rather than settled agreement. 🔴

**Dietary fat and testosterone:** several studies he cites show that low-fat diets go with lower testosterone and lower sperm production in men. 🟢 A combined analysis of 6 studies that changed diets (206 men) found low-fat diets slightly lowered testosterone (Whittaker 2021); 🟡 the sperm claim is not verified.

**Erectile dysfunction as an early warning sign of insulin resistance:** the same nitric oxide and vessel-widening mechanism as in Ch.3's blood pressure section. The blood vessels of the penis need nitric oxide to widen, and insulin-resistant vessels cannot make enough. Bikman notes that some doctors see this as one of the **earliest** signs of insulin resistance in otherwise healthy young men. 🟢 Shared mechanism: insulin resistance impairs the nitric-oxide-driven relaxing of blood vessels (Muniyappa 2013).

## Cancer — The Metabolic Theory (Warburg / Seyfried)

**Two competing theories:**

- **The genetic theory (mainstream):** cancer starts from mutations (changes) in the DNA of the cell's nucleus (its control center), causing growth out of control.
- **The metabolic theory (older than most people think; it goes back to Otto Warburg about 100 years ago):** cancer starts from damaged mitochondria (the cell's power plants).

**The cell-swap experiments (moving nuclei and mitochondria between cells) reviewed by Seyfried's group, truly elegant (Seyfried 2014):**

- Tumor mitochondria + healthy nucleus → the cell became **cancerous**.
- Healthy mitochondria + tumor nucleus → the cell behaved **normally**, with controlled growth.
- This directly challenges the purely genetic model: in this experiment, the mitochondria, not the nucleus, decided the outcome.
- Proposed mechanism: damaged mitochondria produce oxidative stress (reactive oxygen species, a kind of chemical damage) → *that* damages the nucleus → producing the mutations usually blamed as the root cause. The mutations in the nucleus are real and relevant, but they may come after a mitochondrial problem, not be the origin. This is a specific chain of cause proposed by this research group. It is not yet the main view in oncology (cancer medicine), but it is a serious, published line of research, not fringe speculation. 🔴 Minority view: the model of mutations in body cells is still mainstream, and many cancer cells keep working mitochondria while still showing the Warburg effect (Liberti 2016).

**The Warburg effect:**

- Cancer cells are stuck fermenting sugar outside the mitochondria (as bacteria do), rather than burning several fuels flexibly as healthy cells can.
- Cancer cells burn sugar about **200 times** faster than normal cells (🟡 the 200-times figure is not verified; the increase varies widely from tumor to tumor).
- This is the actual basis of PET scans for cancer: sugar tagged with a radioactive marker gathers wherever cancer cells are, because they depend so heavily on sugar.

**Why insulin resistance in particular feeds this:**

- Too much insulin = a growth signal reaching every cell, cancer cells included.
- High blood sugar = the fuel to match that growth signal, feeding the cancer cell that depends on fermenting sugar.
- **Breast tumors: about 7 times more insulin receptors than normal breast tissue; prostate tumors also have too many** (🟢 more than 6 times for breast cancer in Papa 1990). Cancer cells seem to actively turn up their own response to insulin.
- **Men with insulin resistance are 3 times more likely to die of cancer.** A similar rise in deaths is seen in insulin-resistant women with breast cancer, regardless of obesity, heart disease or smoking. 🟡 Evidence check: in US NHANES data, adults without diabetes whose fasting insulin was 10 µU/mL or higher had about twice the cancer death rate (Tsujimoto 2017); observational. The 3-times figure for men was not verified.

**Bikman's careful, clear position (worth keeping close to his words):** insulin resistance is **not** claimed as the cause of cancer. Some other event probably starts it (possibly mitochondrial damage, as Seyfried suggests). But insulin resistance clearly **speeds up** existing cancer a great deal. "It maybe did not light the fire, but it sure as hell is pouring gasoline on it."

**Use in treatment (from the Q&A): the glucose-ketone index**

- Seyfried's group treats certain cancers (for example glioblastoma, a brain tumor that cannot be operated on) with therapeutic ketosis. They drive blood sugar down to about 3.5 mmol/L while raising ketones to match or exceed that level, deliberately starving the sugar-dependent cancer cell.
- Reported as "fantastically effective" in certain types of cancer. Bikman explains that this does not directly fix the mitochondria; it starves the cancer cell of the one fuel it depends on. 🔴 Evidence check: trials in glioblastoma so far are small studies of whether the approach is doable, and a 2025 systematic review (a careful summary of all studies) could draw no firm conclusions about survival (McKerill 2025).

## Q&A Highlights

**Type of protein (whey vs. plant) and insulin:** the effect on insulin depends on the mix of amino acids (protein building blocks), not on where the protein comes from as such. Animal protein is described as reliably complete. Plant proteins (even soy, called the most complete plant option) can lack some amino acids and contain anti-nutrients (phytic acid, tannins, trypsin inhibitors) that interfere with digestion; traditional fermentation partly fixes this.

**Ketone supplements, and who really benefits:** they are useful specifically for people with declining memory and thinking or at risk of Alzheimer's (direct fuel for the brain) or for endurance athletes (an extra fuel source). But for an ordinary overweight or diabetic person looking to improve their metabolism, the benefit comes from *lowering insulin itself*, not from ketones as a supplement; ketone supplements do not copy that insulin-lowering benefit. An important nuance: taking ketones is no substitute for actually fixing too much insulin.

**Mental and psychiatric disorders and insulin resistance:** Bikman mentions Dr. Chris Palmer's book *Brain Energy*, which extends the idea of brain disease as a metabolic problem to schizophrenia, bipolar disorder, anxiety and depression. Bikman calls this a real, known area of effect, though outside his own research. 🔴 An emerging hypothesis; the evidence is limited to small early studies.

**Insulin reference ranges across ethnic groups:** Bikman says target insulin ranges do **not** differ by ethnic background or body type. Unlike the personal fat threshold (which does vary), the insulin number itself should be read the same way for everyone. Specific number ranges are saved for a later "solutions" lecture. 🟡 Contested: Ch.5 itself gives cutoffs adjusted for ethnic background for other markers, and how well the markers work differs by ethnic group (Sumner 2005).

## Key Corrections to Carry Forward

- Thinking amyloid plaques are a settled, established cause of Alzheimer's: real problems with drug approval and research honesty (the 2021 FDA approval over its panel's objection, the 2022 report of manipulated images and the 2024 retraction) make this more complicated than people usually admit, though it remains the main model in mainstream neurology.
- Thinking "the brain needs sugar": clearly false, he says, when ketones are available; the brain actively prefers ketones even when sugar is more plentiful. 🔴 See the evidence check above: ketones become the brain's main fuel only when their levels are high (long fasting), and the brain still needs some sugar (Owen 1967; Cunnane 2020).
- Thinking low testosterone means the testes are failing: according to this chapter, it is more often insulin actively holding the testes back and fat cells actively turning testosterone into estrogen; the testes themselves are usually not damaged.
- Thinking cancer is purely a disease of the DNA in the nucleus: Seyfried's mitochondria-swap experiment is a serious, published challenge to that idea, though it has not replaced the genetic model as the main framework of mainstream cancer medicine.

## Practical Takeaway

The way MCT oil works now has a specific, named explanation: medium-chain fats can barely be stored, so they tend to be burned whatever the insulin level.

## Questions to Learn From (Socratic) — with Answer Options

**1. Ketone-burning genes stay normal in Alzheimer's brains while sugar-burning genes are turned down (according to Bikman's own lab study). Does this mean ketogenic approaches should be used for prevention, whatever a person's current memory and thinking?**

A. Yes; this proves everyone should stay in ketosis permanently to prevent Alzheimer's
B. *The finding supports ketones as a real *backup fuel route* worth understanding, especially for anyone with risk factors for insulin resistance. But Bikman himself presents the existing approaches (coconut oil, ketone supplements) as bringing *improvement*, not *prevention* or *cure*. The evidence supports having ketones available as protective or supportive, not as a proven prevention plan
C. Ketones have no established relevance to brain health at any age

*Why B: it is important not to stretch a real, well-documented mechanism into a promise of prevention that the evidence cited does not actually support.*

**2. Bikman compares LDL at a plaque (Ch.3) with amyloid at a plaque (this chapter). What is the key difference in strength of evidence between these two "bystander" arguments?**

A. Both arguments are equally strong and should be weighed the same
B. *The Alzheimer's case has extra evidence about research honesty (the 2022 fraud finding, the FDA overruling its panel) that the LDL case has nothing to match. Still, both remain minority views compared with their mainstream models
C. The LDL case is stronger because heart guidelines are better established than brain guidelines

*Why B: the two arguments share a similar logical shape but differ in their supporting evidence; worth not treating them as equally supported just because they use the same rhetorical device.*

**3. Women with PCOS can show normal whole-body insulin resistance markers while having significant insulin resistance in their fat cells. What does this mean about relying on standard insulin and sugar tests to rule out insulin resistance as a factor in a health problem?**

A. Normal whole-body insulin markers definitely rule out any role for insulin resistance
B. *Normal whole-body markers can miss early insulin resistance, or resistance in one tissue, especially in fat cells, where it begins. A normal result does not fully rule out insulin resistance as a contributing factor, especially for conditions tied to a particular tissue
C. Whole-body insulin testing is completely useless and should never be done

*Why B: this is the PCOS study's direct lesson. Fat tissue, the "first domino," can be significantly affected before whole-body markers pick it up, a theme now seen in three separate groups (insulin resistance as it develops in general, PCOS, and possibly others).*
