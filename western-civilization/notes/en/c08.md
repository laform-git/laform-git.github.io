**Source:** How the Catholic Church Built Western Civilization — Thomas E. Woods, Jr., Ch. 8: "The Church and Economics"

## The Insight

Economic history is usually told starting with Adam Smith. Woods argues the actual foundations — subjective value theory, monetary theory, and expectations theory — were worked out two centuries earlier by Catholic monks, friars, and cardinals, with even hostile Marxist-adjacent economists (Schumpeter) crediting them as the real "founders of scientific economics." The chapter's throughline is a single correct insight (value is subjective, not tied to labor or cost) discovered early by Catholic thinkers, lost by Protestant Britain, and later recovered by the Austrian School.

## Why It Matters

This chapter makes an unusually precise, falsifiable claim: that a specific correct economic principle (subjective value) has a documented paper trail running from a 13th-century Franciscan through named Scholastics to the modern Austrian School — while a specific incorrect principle (the labor theory of value) has its own separate paper trail through Locke, Smith, and Marx. Worth testing both trails independently rather than accepting the whole package at once.

## Evidence / Support

### The credentialing quote and modern rediscovery

🟢 Joseph Schumpeter's History of Economic Analysis (1954) is quoted directly: the Late Scholastics "come nearer than does any other group to having been the 'founders' of scientific economics." Murray Rothbard traced their influence forward into the Austrian School (Menger, Böhm-Bawerk, Mises, Hayek — the last a 1974 Nobel laureate). Other cited modern scholars: Raymond de Roover, Marjorie Grice-Hutchinson, Alejandro Chafuen.

### Monetary theory before Smith

🟢 Jean Buridan (rector of the University of Paris, 1300–1358) explained money as a spontaneous market phenomenon, not a state creation — a useful, portable, divisible, durable, high-value commodity chosen freely by traders. One expert credits him with starting the classification scheme used in money-and-banking textbooks into the 1930s gold-standard era.

🟢 Nicolas Oresme (1325–1382), Buridan's student, wrote what's called a monetary-theory milestone "not surpassed for many centuries." He first stated what's now called Gresham's Law — quoted directly: when a fixed legal exchange ratio between two currencies diverges from their real market ratio, the artificially undervalued currency vanishes from circulation. Woods illustrates with a worked gold/silver example. Oresme also condemned currency debasement as pure government enrichment at public expense, arguing government should stay out of the monetary system entirely.

🟢 Martín de Azpilcueta (1493–1586) is credited with the first formulation of the quantity theory of money, quoted directly observing that money is worth less where it's abundant (citing post-conquest Spain's price inflation from New World silver/gold) and more where scarce — with a memorable analogy: a short man looks shorter next to a tall one, the way scarce goods look "cheaper" next to abundant money.

🟢 Cardinal Cajetan (Thomas de Vio, 1468–1534) — also notable for out-arguing Martin Luther on papal authority via a parallel citation from Isaiah 22:22 — wrote De Cambiis (1499) arguing money's present value reflects expectations about the future (harvests, war, money-supply changes). Rothbard credits him as the founder of expectations theory in economics.

### Subjective value theory — the chapter's central thread

🟢 Franciscan friar Pierre de Jean Olivi (1248–1298) first proposed that a good's value comes from individuals' subjective assessment of its usefulness, not objective production costs — meaning the "just price" emerges from market interaction, not a calculated formula. San Bernardino of Siena adopted this nearly word for word roughly 150 years later.

🟢 Luis Saravía de la Calle is quoted at length rejecting cost-based pricing outright: "the just price arises from the abundance or scarcity of goods, merchants, and money... and not from costs, labor, and risk," using the example that a printed book (cheaper to produce, often better) should not be worth less than a hand-copied one if that's not how the market actually values them.

🟢 Cardinal Juan de Lugo (Jesuit, 1583–1660) is quoted making the same point via comparison: mice are more "perfect" than corn but worth less, because value tracks utility and estimation, not intrinsic quality — and glass trinkets fetch gold in Ethiopia purely because local estimation values them highly there.

🟢 Luis de Molina (Jesuit) is quoted making essentially the same argument via pearls versus grain — pearls cost more despite grain being objectively more useful for survival, because value tracks what people actually want, not "nobler" utility.

🟢 Carl Menger's 1871 Principles of Economics (explicitly tied by Woods to the Thomistic-Aristotelian tradition) is used to explain the logic formally: if tobacco lost all value to consumers, a machine built only to process tobacco would instantly become worthless too — proving value flows from the finished good's subjective demand backward to its inputs, never the reverse.

### The direct rebuttal to Marx

🟢 Woods walks through why this matters against Karl Marx's labor theory of value — Marx held a good's value is set by labor hours invested (once some baseline use-value is granted), which underwrote his theory that capitalist profit represents unpaid, "exploited" labor value. Woods notes the labor theory cannot explain a simple observed fact: an artist's works rising in value after death, despite zero additional labor being applied. The Scholastic sequence is the reverse of Marx's: consumer demand for the finished good gives value to the labor and materials that made it, not the other way around.

🟢 Woods notes even Adam Smith was ambiguous enough on value theory to feed forward into Marx's error — Rothbard's claim, cited here, is that economics would have been better served staying with the Scholastic/French/Italian line than the British line that led to Marx.

### Emil Kauder's Catholic/Protestant divergence thesis

🟢 Kauder's A History of Marginal Utility Theory (1965) asks why subjective value theory flourished in Catholic countries (France, Italy) while the labor theory dominated Protestant Britain (Locke, Smith, eventually Marx). His answer: Calvin's theology gave labor itself a kind of sanctified, God-glorifying status, which Kauder argues predisposed Protestant thinkers to build labor into the very center of value theory — quoted directly on this point. Catholic countries, shaped instead by an Aristotelian/Thomist view that treated pleasure and happiness (inherently subjective, non-quantifiable states) as economic activity's actual purpose, naturally arrived at subjective value instead.

🟡 Woods is explicit this causal theological explanation "is impossible to prove" strictly — offered as suggestive correlation Kauder assembled evidence for, not a demonstrated mechanism.

### Later transmission and influence

🟢 Protestant jurist Hugo Grotius (international law) explicitly cited and adopted the late Scholastics' economic thought in the 17th century, as did Jesuits Leonardus Lessius and Juan de Lugo. In 18th-century Italy, Abbé Ferdinando Galiani (credited as an originator of utility/scarcity-based pricing) and Antonio Genovesi both show clear Scholastic influence; Rothbard traces the line onward to French abbés Condillac and Turgot, and even to the physiocrats (Quesnay), often called economic science's own founders.

🟡 Woods closes by noting these ideas were already centuries old by the time the explicitly anti-Catholic French Encyclopédie repeated the same Scholastic price analysis — direct rebuttal to any claim that free-market economics was an anti-Catholic Enlightenment invention.

## Meditations (3)

- A pearl costs more than bread not because it's more useful, but because enough people have decided it should be — value was never a measurement, it was always a vote.
- The labor theory of value cannot explain why a dead artist's paintings get more valuable with no new labor ever applied to them again.
- An idea can be centuries old, thoroughly worked out by monks and cardinals, and still get credited entirely to the century that merely popularized it.

## Discussion Points for Teachers

- Kauder's Calvin-vs-Aristotle explanation for the Catholic/Protestant value-theory split is explicitly labeled unprovable even by Woods. Is it responsible to include a causal theory this speculative in a chapter otherwise built on hard textual citations (Buridan, Oresme, Cajetan)? Where's the line between suggestive pattern and demonstrated cause?
- This chapter reads as far more sympathetic to free-market economics specifically (not just "the Church contributed to X") than earlier chapters. Does Woods's own stated intellectual commitments (he's written elsewhere defending free-market economics from a Catholic perspective) color how this chapter is framed, compared to, say, the more historically neutral tone of Ch2 or Ch3?
- The chapter treats "correct" (subjective value) versus "incorrect" (labor theory) as settled economic fact. Is that framing appropriate for a history chapter, or does it import one side of a live economic-theory debate as though it were beyond dispute?

## Socratic Question

"If subjective value theory was fully worked out by Catholic friars and cardinals three centuries before Marx, why did an entire alternative theory — built on a premise those same thinkers had already refuted — go on to shape the twentieth century so completely?"
