**Source:** How the Catholic Church Built Western Civilization — Thomas E. Woods, Jr., Conclusion: "A World Without God"

## The Insight

Woods closes by naming four attributes of the Catholic conception of God (unity, absolute sovereignty, transcendence, goodness) that he argues shaped the Western mind even among people who have abandoned belief — then pivots to a striking claim: modern art, literature, and music's turn toward chaos, fragmentation, and nihilism directly tracks the West's retreat from the theological worldview that once gave those art forms their sense of order and purpose.

## Why It Matters

This is Woods's attempt to demonstrate his thesis by its absence — rather than showing what the Church built, the Conclusion argues you can watch civilization visibly fray as its theological foundation is removed, using Duchamp, Kafka, Schoenberg, Stravinsky, and Sartre as evidence.

## Evidence / Support

### Four attributes of the Catholic God, contrasted with ancient Near Eastern religion

🟢 God is one (versus polytheistic deities each governing a limited domain); absolutely sovereign (subject to no prior force, unlike gods bound by fate, hunger, or illness); transcendent — wholly other than creation, never identified with any physical thing — which Woods argues is precisely what made it possible to conceive of nature as governed by regular, discoverable laws rather than animated by competing wills; and good, actively willing human welfare rather than indifferent or petty like the Greek pantheon.

🟢 The Incarnation adds something beyond even Old Testament Judaism: God seeking not just worship but friendship. Woods cites Søren Kierkegaard's parable of a king who disguises himself as a commoner to win a common woman's genuine love (rather than love compelled by awe or fear) as an image for why God became man rather than simply overwhelming humanity with majesty.

🟢 Even avowedly secular movements retain Christian structure underneath: Woods cites Murray Rothbard's argument that Marxism borrowed substantially from 16th-century Christian heresies, and notes early-20th-century Progressive intellectuals who considered themselves post-Protestant still spoke in a recognizably Christian idiom.

### Summary of the book's whole argument

🟢 Woods explicitly re-lists the entire book's claims as "the very foundations of a civilization": economic thought, international law, science, university life, charity, religious ideas, art, and morality — arguing every one of them emerged specifically from the Catholic Church, not merely received Church influence in passing.

🟢 David Lindberg is quoted again (as in Ch4) specifically rejecting the stereotype of the medieval professor as a "spineless" authority-follower, insisting medieval scholars operated with real intellectual freedom within broad theological limits.

### The historical retreat and its visible consequences

🟢 Woods traces the Church's declining public influence through the Enlightenment's challenge to her authority, the German Kulturkampf, Italian anticlerical nationalism, and France's 1905 school secularization — conceding the Church nonetheless flourished in the U.S. during this same period.

🟢 Philosopher Jude Dougherty is quoted arguing a direct causal link between "impoverished anti-metaphysical philosophy" and "debilitating effect on the arts" — once a transcendent, ordering divine intellect is denied, reality collapses into pure materialism and life into meaninglessness, which Dougherty argues shows up directly in modern art's "perverseness and sterility," from Bauhaus to Cubism to postmodernism.

🟢 Specific evidence offered: Nietzsche's Joyful Wisdom passage celebrating an "open sea" of self-created meaning once divine purpose is rejected (with historian Frederick Copleston's gloss quoted directly); Kafka's Metamorphosis opening line as an example of literature abandoning narrative order for arbitrary absurdity; Schoenberg's atonality and Stravinsky's "chaotic rhythms" (Rite of Spring, the 1945 Symphony in Three Movements) as musical parallels; and the acknowledged "degeneration" even of contemporary Catholic church architecture.

🟢 Sartre's existentialism is presented as the logical endpoint: the universe is genuinely absurd, life has no inherent meaning, and one's task is to construct personal values while frankly acknowledging the void — explicitly tied back to Nietzsche's earlier framework.

🟢 A specific visual-arts genealogy is traced: medieval artists, who saw themselves as communicating something beyond themselves, typically did not sign their work; the Renaissance began shifting focus toward the individual artist; 19th-century Romanticism (partly a reaction against Enlightenment scientism) made the artist's own emotional interior the actual subject of art; photography's invention removed realistic representation as art's necessary task, freeing artists further toward pure self-expression — a lineage Woods argues terminates in outright nihilism.

🟢 Two concrete closing examples: Marcel Duchamp's 1917 signed urinal (Fountain), voted the single most influential work of modern art in a 2004 poll of 500 art experts; and Tracey Emin's My Bed (an unmade bed with liquor bottles, used contraceptives, and bloodied underwear, Turner Prize–nominated), vandalized by two nude men at the Tate in 1999 while the gallery applauded, assuming the vandalism was part of the exhibit.

### Closing statement

🟢 Woods notes Europe's declining birthrate and the European Union's refusal to acknowledge Christianity in its constitutional preamble as concrete symptoms of the broader retreat, and closes by quoting French philosopher Simone Weil — explicitly not a Catholic — stating the Christian idea, rooted partly in Greek thought, has nourished European civilization to the point that renouncing it "one cannot... without becoming degraded."

## Meditations (3)

- A civilization that stops believing nature has a maker eventually stops believing art needs a subject.
- Even a philosopher who was not Catholic concluded you could not reject the Christian idea without becoming smaller for it.
- The same four centuries that produced Kepler and Bach's structured order also, on this account, produced the conditions for Duchamp's urinal to be voted the century's most influential artwork.

## Discussion Points for Teachers

- Attributing modern art's nihilism directly to secularization is a strong, contestable cultural claim, not a historical fact like a papal charter date. How would a secular art historian explain the same shift (Duchamp, Schoenberg, Sartre) without reference to religious decline?
- The chapter cites deliberately provocative modern art (Duchamp's urinal, Emin's bed) as representative of "where secularism leads." Is this a fair sampling of modern and contemporary art, or does it select the most shocking examples to make the argument land harder?
- Woods closes the entire book on a note of cultural decline. Does ending on this note change how a reader should weigh the historical claims in Chapters 1–11, or are the historical and the cultural-commentary arguments best evaluated separately?

## Socratic Question

"If the same civilization that produced Aquinas's ordered Summa and the geometric precision of Chartres cathedral also, centuries later and after rejecting much of that same theological framework, produced Duchamp's urinal as its most celebrated modern artwork — is that a story about decline, or a story about a civilization simply becoming something else entirely?"
