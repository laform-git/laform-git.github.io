**Source:** How the Catholic Church Built Western Civilization — Thomas E. Woods, Jr., Ch. 7: "The Origins of International Law"

## The Insight

Woods opens with the Columbus controversy itself — celebrated in 1892, condemned by the 1990s — not to settle it, but to point at something historically unusual: Spanish mistreatment of New World natives triggered a genuine crisis of conscience among the conquering culture's own theologians. Woods contrasts this directly with the absence of comparable self-criticism among, say, the Aztecs regarding human sacrifice, or Attila regarding conquest. That crisis of conscience, worked out by Spanish Dominican theologians, is presented as the actual origin of international law as a discipline.

## Why It Matters

The chapter's real argument is narrower and more defensible than "Spain behaved well in the New World" — it's that the process of Spanish self-criticism produced durable universal principles (natural rights belonging to all humans regardless of religion, limits on just war, the state's moral non-autonomy) that still structure how we think about relations between nations.

## Evidence / Support

### The provocation: Montesinos's sermon

🟢 December 1511, Hispaniola: Dominican friar Antonio de Montesinos delivered a sermon (quoted at length) directly accusing Spanish colonists of mortal sin for their treatment of the Indians — forced labor, neglect, violence — asking directly, "Are these not men? Have they not rational souls?" Historian Lewis Hanke notes it was "designed to shock and terrify."

🟢 When Spanish officials (including Admiral Diego Columbus) demanded a retraction, Montesinos instead used his next sermon to reaffirm every charge and announced the friars would refuse to hear colonists' confessions until their behavior changed.

🟢 The controversy reached King Ferdinand and prompted the Laws of Burgos (1512) and Valladolid (1513), later informing the New Laws of 1542 — poorly enforced given the distance from Spain, but the direct trigger for the more systematic theological work that followed.

### Francisco de Vitoria — "father of international law"

🟢 Born c. 1483, Dominican from 1504, educated at Paris, later held the Prime Chair of Theology at Salamanca. His 1532 lectures (published as Readings on the Indians and on the Law of War) laid out systematic principles later scholars credit as the first modern treatise on the law of nations.

🟢 Building on two Aquinas-derived principles — that divine law does not annul natural law, and that natural entitlements are not forfeited by sin — Vitoria argued natural rights (not to be killed, not to be despoiled of property) belong to all humans as humans, regardless of religion. Colleague Domingo de Soto is quoted stating plainly that grace confers no advantage over pagans "in what concerns natural rights."

🟢 Vitoria is quoted directly: the natives "undoubtedly had true dominion in both public and private matters, just like Christians," and could not be justly stripped of it for not being Christian. He also argued legitimately ruling pagan kings could not be deposed merely for being pagan.

🟢 Vitoria held that rejecting the Gospel was never grounds for just war, citing Aquinas's principle that belief "depends upon the will" and must be free — consistent with the Fourth Council of Toledo (633) condemning forced baptism of Jews.

🟢 He explicitly rebutted claims the natives lacked reason, arguing (quoted directly) that their ordered cities, marriages, magistrates, and laws were themselves evidence of reason's use — concluding God would not fail to grant humanity's defining trait to any "great part of a species."

🟢 Vitoria did allow limited grounds for force against native peoples (e.g., protecting others from certain native practices) — a concession Las Casas considered dangerously exploitable.

### Bartolomé de Las Casas — the more radical pacifist position

🟢 Las Casas, a priest and bishop whose own writings are the main historical source for Montesinos's sermons, agreed with Vitoria on native rationality but rejected any use of coercion whatsoever, including Vitoria's limited exceptions.

🟢 The 1550 Valladolid debate between Las Casas and Juan Ginés de Sepúlveda is described by one scholar as history's clearest case of an imperial power publicly interrogating its own conquest's legitimacy. Sepúlveda argued Spanish tutelage was a necessary precondition for civilizing natives before conversion; Las Casas insisted force would poison the missionary project itself and that natives should be won "with gentle and divine words."

🟢 Las Casas spent roughly fifty years (from 1514) agitating against the encomienda system — in which colonists were nominally responsible for natives' welfare and religious instruction in exchange for tribute, but which functioned in practice as forced labor — having once held an encomienda himself. His 1564 will is quoted directly describing his life's purpose as pleading before the Crown for the natives' "primitive liberty."

🟢 Las Casas is still widely revered across Latin America; Lewis Hanke credits his arguments with strengthening everyone since who has insisted all peoples share full human potential and responsibility.

### The larger philosophical claim and its limits

🟢 Underlying both thinkers: natural law as a universal ethic binding all peoples — Christian or not — rooted in Saint Paul's claim that natural law is written on the human heart, giving grounds to judge even those who'd never heard the Gospel.

🟢 Woods contrasts this with the absence of comparable universalism elsewhere: Samuel Eliot Morison is cited noting many native groups had no common self-designation beyond "We, the People" versus derogatory names for neighboring tribes (the Iroquois Confederacy is noted as an exception). Peruvian novelist Mario Vargas Llosa is quoted at length crediting the West specifically with being the first civilization to permit an individual to morally question his own society's actions rather than treating state and morality as identical.

🟢 Machiavelli's The Prince (1513) is presented as the polar opposite position — the state as morally autonomous, answerable to no external standard — explicitly condemned by the Church precisely because Spanish theologians had just finished arguing the reverse.

🟡 Woods is careful to note enforcement remained genuinely unresolved: Vitoria's answer leaned on just-war theory (a violated state has grounds for war against the violator), and Woods explicitly cautions against assuming these theologians would have endorsed anything like a UN-style enforcement body, working through a Hobbesian argument that any supranational enforcer merely relocates the problem of unaccountable power rather than solving it.

## Meditations (3)

- No comparable outpouring of self-criticism met Aztec human sacrifice or Attila's conquests. The fact that Spain produced one is itself the data point worth explaining.
- A natural right that depended on being baptized to be real would not have been a natural right at all.
- The West may be the first civilization where a citizen could morally indict his own country's conquest — and did.

## Discussion Points for Teachers

- Vitoria and Las Casas actually disagreed on real substance (limited just-war exceptions vs. absolute pacifism toward natives). Is it fair to credit "the Church" as a whole with founding international law when its own theologians were in genuine dispute about the details?
- Woods's Hobbesian aside on why a UN-style enforcement body does not actually solve the problem it claims to solve is a live political-philosophy argument, not a historical claim. Worth flagging this shift from history to political theory explicitly in discussion.
- The chapter argues Spanish self-criticism is historically unusual and admirable. Does documenting the theological critique risk understating how limited its actual real-world enforcement was, given Woods's own admission the Laws of Burgos and Valladolid were poorly applied?

## Socratic Question

"If the very moral vocabulary used today to criticize Columbus and the conquistadors — universal human rights, natural law, the state's non-autonomy — was developed by the theologians of the conquering culture itself, what does that imply about where our capacity for self-criticism actually comes from?"
