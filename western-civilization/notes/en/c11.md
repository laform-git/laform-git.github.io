**Source:** How the Catholic Church Built Western Civilization — Thomas E. Woods, Jr., Ch. 11: "The Church and Western Morality"

## The Insight

Woods argues Western morality's foundation — the sacredness of individual human life, rooted in the soul's immortality — simply did not exist in the ancient world, and traces four specific moral reforms directly to Catholic teaching: the abolition of infanticide, the condemnation of suicide, the end of gladiatorial combat, and opposition to dueling. He then extends the argument to just war theory and sexual morality, closing with the claim that Catholic moral teaching demands not just correct outward action but genuine internal discipline — a much higher bar than mere legal compliance.

## Why It Matters

Each of the four reforms follows the same pattern Woods uses throughout the book: document the ancient practice matter-of-factly, then show specific Catholic theological reasoning that opposed it, then cite (often hostile or neutral) historians confirming the Church's role in ending it.

## Evidence / Support

### Infanticide

🟢 Explicitly endorsed in the ancient world: Plato suggested the chronically ill poor should be left to die; Seneca is quoted directly — "We drown children who at birth are weakly and abnormal." Deformed boys and simply inconvenient girls were routinely abandoned, skewing the ancient Roman male population roughly 30% above female.

🟢 The Church's absolute opposition, rooted in the sacredness of each human life via the immortal soul, ended the practice — presented as continuous with the charity argument from Ch9.

### Suicide

🟢 Defended by several ancient schools, especially Stoics (some of whom killed themselves as a final act of philosophical detachment) though Aristotle himself criticized the practice.

🟢 Saint Augustine (City of God) is quoted directly rejecting the idea that suicide reflects "greatness of spirit" — arguing real strength endures suffering rather than escaping it, and that Christ's own refusal to counsel suicide to His persecuted followers, despite promising them eternal reward, settles the question.

🟢 Aquinas (Summa Theologiae) gives reasoned arguments plus an explicitly theological one, quoted directly: life is a gift from God alone empowered to give or take it, making suicide a trespass against God's exclusive jurisdiction (citing Deuteronomy 32:39).

🟡 Woods cites an early-20th-century scholar's observation of sharply lower suicide rates in Catholic versus Protestant Swiss cantons, and in heavily Catholic Ireland, as suggestive (not conclusive) evidence of lasting cultural effect.

### Gladiatorial combat

🟢 Historian Jerome Carcopino is quoted stating flatly that "the butcheries of the arena were stopped at the command of Christian emperors" — ended in the Western empire by the late 4th century, Eastern by the early 5th. W. E. H. Lecky (again used as the critical-but-honest witness) calls the suppression of gladiatorial games one of history's most important moral reforms, "almost exclusively" attributable to the Church.

### Dueling

🟢 Defended by proponents as channeling violence into a controlled, honor-bound alternative to unregulated blood feuds. The Church rejected this reasoning outright: the Council of Trent (1545–1563) excommunicated duelers and denied them Church burial; Popes Benedict XIV and Pius IX reaffirmed and extended these penalties (including to witnesses/accomplices).

🟢 Pope Leo XIII is quoted at length condemning dueling as unjustified risk to life outside legitimate self-defense or duty, rooted purely in vengeance-seeking — explicitly naming revenge as "a deadly sin" reserved to God's own judgment.

### Just war theory

🟢 Woods argues classical antiquity (even Cicero, who came closest) never produced a full theory of just war comparable to Aquinas's treatment — scholar Ernest Fortin is quoted noting nothing in Plato or Aristotle compares to Aquinas's systematic treatment.

🟢 Augustine's foundational formulation: a just war is justified only by an aggressor's injustice, which "ought to be a source of grief to any good man." Aquinas's Summa gives the three conditions still cited today: legitimate sovereign authority to declare war, a just cause (avenging real wrongs, not aggression), and right intention (pursuing good/avoiding evil, not vengeance or lust for power) — all three quoted directly from Aquinas, who cites Augustine within his own argument.

🟢 The tradition matures further with Vitoria's three canons (moderation in seeking war, proportionate conduct during war, humble and judicial — not vindictive — use of victory) and Francisco Suárez's parallel three conditions (legitimate authority, just cause, just methods), both quoted directly.

🟢 Machiavelli's The Prince is again invoked as the counter-example — politics as a morally unaccountable "game" where even fifty thousand lives are just pieces on a board — framed as exactly what just-war theory was developed to refuse.

### Sexual morality

🟢 Ancient sources (Juvenal, Ovid, Catullus, Martial, Suetonius, Tacitus) are cited describing widespread Roman sexual immorality and rare marital fidelity; Augustus's legal attempts to curb it are noted as largely ineffective.

🟢 The Church confined intimacy to marriage and — significantly — extended the definition of adultery to include husbands' infidelity, not just wives', a genuine equalization historian Edward Westermarck credits directly to Christian influence. Even Edward Gibbon (no friend to Christianity in his account of Rome's fall) is quoted conceding "the dignity of marriage was restored by the Christians"; physician Galen is quoted praising Christian sexual self-discipline as rivaling that of philosophers.

🟢 Women's status improved concretely: marriage's elevation to a sacrament and the prohibition on divorce (preventing husbands from discarding wives for new ones) is credited with drawing disproportionate numbers of women into the early Church. Philosopher Robert Phillips is quoted noting women ran self-governing religious communities, schools, hospitals, and orphanages under Catholicism — unprecedented in the ancient world.

### Internal discipline, not just outward compliance

🟢 Woods closes by arguing Christ's ethical teaching demands interior discipline beyond mere rule-following — not just refraining from adultery but avoiding lustful thought, not just avoiding theft but resisting envy — because objectifying another person (even in thought) is itself a moral failure.

🟢 Seneca is quoted ("What a contemptible thing is man, if he fail to rise above the human condition!") as evidence even pagans sensed humanity's need to transcend mere instinct — with Catholic teaching adding that grace, not willpower alone, makes heroic virtue (the saints) actually possible.

## Meditations (3)

- A civilization that drowned its own weak infants needed something more than better laws. It needed a different idea of what a person is.
- Even Gibbon, no friend of the Church, had to admit marriage owed its dignity to the very religion he blamed for Rome's fall.
- Not committing adultery and not wanting to were treated as two different achievements — and only one of them can be legislated.

## Discussion Points for Teachers

- The chapter treats four separate reforms (infanticide, suicide, gladiatorial games, dueling) as a single unified pattern of Catholic moral influence. Are these four cases equally well-documented, or does the strength of evidence vary chapter-claim to claim?
- Woods's closing argument (interior discipline, not just outward compliance) is more a piece of moral theology than a historical claim. Does shifting from "what changed and why" to "what the ideal actually demands" change the chapter's register in a way worth naming explicitly?
- The suicide-rate comparison (Catholic vs. Protestant Swiss cantons) is offered as merely "suggestive." What confounding factors besides religion might explain regional suicide-rate differences?

## Socratic Question

"If a society's willingness to abandon its own weakest members (infanticide) and its willingness to kill for entertainment (gladiatorial games) both required a specifically theological argument to end — not just better laws — what does that suggest about the actual limits of secular law alone in producing moral change?"
