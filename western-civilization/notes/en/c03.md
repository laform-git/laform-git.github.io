**Source:** How the Catholic Church Built Western Civilization — Thomas E. Woods, Jr., Ch. 3: "How the Monks Saved Civilization"

## The Insight

The monks never set out to build civilization — Woods opens with the Gospel line about seeking the kingdom first and having everything else added, and treats that as literally what happened historically: men who withdrew from the world for purely spiritual reasons ended up, almost as a byproduct, rescuing agriculture, literacy, technology, and craft knowledge for the whole of Europe. The chapter is essentially a catalogue chapter — its power is cumulative breadth, not a single argument.

## Why It Matters

For a teacher audience, this chapter is best used as a factual reservoir — dozens of specific, checkable claims across agriculture, technology, book-preservation, and charity. The individual claims matter more here than any single thesis; below is organized to make every one of them retrievable.

## Evidence / Support

### Origins of monasticism

🟢 Early consecrated virgins (3rd century) are the root of the nun tradition. Saint Anthony of Egypt (3rd–4th century) is the more famous origin point for monasticism proper — a hermit whose example drew thousands despite his own retreat into solitude.

🟡 Monsignor Philip Hughes is quoted describing some hermits' extreme practices — near-total fasting, standing motionless for weeks, even sealing themselves in tombs.

🟢 Cenobitic monasticism (communal, not solitary) developed partly as a corrective to hermit extremism, associated with Saint Basil the Great in the East. The hermit tradition never fully died — a hermit was elected pope as Celestine V roughly a thousand years after Paul of Thebes.

🟢 Eastern monastic influence reached the West through Saint Athanasius and Saint John Cassian.

### Saint Benedict and the Rule

🟢 Benedict founded twelve small communities at Subiaco, then Monte Cassino, where around 529 he wrote the Rule of Saint Benedict — moderate compared to the Irish monasteries' harsher self-denial, guaranteeing adequate food and sleep even in penitential seasons.

🟢 The Rule is quoted directly asserting equality regardless of birth status — "whether we are bond or free, we are all one in Christ." Benedict also ended the practice of monks wandering between houses, binding each monk to one monastery under an abbot.

🟢 Monte Cassino's own survival is treated as symbolic: sacked by Lombards (589), destroyed by Saracens (884), leveled by earthquake (1349), pillaged by French troops (1799), bombed in WWII (1944) — rebuilt by returning monks every time.

🟢 By the 14th century the Benedictine order alone had produced 24 popes, 200 cardinals, 7,000 archbishops, 15,000 bishops, 1,500 canonized saints, and drew in 20 emperors, 10 empresses, 47 kings, and 50 queens as members; the order at its height ran 37,000 monasteries.

### The practical arts — agriculture and land reclamation

🟢 Henry Goodell (Massachusetts Agricultural College) is quoted crediting the monks with saving agriculture across 1,500 years when no one else could or would. Multiple historians, including the religiously unsympathetic François Guizot, independently credit the monks with Europe's agricultural restoration — clearing forests, draining swamps, breeding cattle.

🟢 Two paired descriptions of the English fen district are given: a scholar's account of the seventh-century swamp as chaotic wasteland, versus William of Malmesbury's twelfth-century description of the same land, post-monastic cultivation, as a near-paradise of orchards and vineyards — a direct before/after literary comparison.

🟢 Specific regional introductions credited to monks: the Swedish corn trade, Parma cheesemaking, Irish salmon fisheries, vineyards in many regions, directing spring water to Paris (Saint Laurent and Saint Martin monasteries), and irrigation techniques introduced to Lombardy's peasants.

🟡 Pope Saint Gregory the Great's story of the abbot Equitius, found by a papal envoy not in the scriptorium but out cutting hay in the valley — illustrating manual labor's real priority even for a noted, eloquent missionary-abbot.

🟢 Monks pioneered winemaking (for Mass and ordinary use, permitted by the Rule) and, specifically, Dom Pérignon of Hautvilliers-on-the-Marne (appointed cellarer 1688) developed the foundational principles of champagne production still used today.

### Monastic technology

🟢 The Cistercians (founded 1098 at Cîteaux) were especially advanced technologically; a scholar is quoted calling their monasteries "the most economically effective units that had ever existed in Europe" to that point. A twelfth-century account of the Clairvaux monastery's waterpower system — crushing wheat, sieving flour, brewing beer, fulling cloth, tanning leather — is quoted at length as evidence of how thoroughly mechanized monastic life had become; Jean Gimpel notes this same setup existed at all 742 Cistercian monasteries then operating in Europe.

🟢 Cistercians were also leading iron producers in Champagne from the mid-13th through 17th centuries, and used furnace slag (rich in phosphates) as fertilizer — an early instance of industrial byproduct reuse.

🟡 A monk named Eilmer flew over 600 feet on a glider in the early eleventh century, remembered for three centuries afterward. Jesuit Father Francesco Lana-Terzi, later called the father of aviation, wrote the first systematic treatment of flight physics in 1670.

🟢 Clockmaking: the future Pope Sylvester II built the first recorded clock (Magdeburg, c. 996). Peter Lightfoot's 14th-century clock survives in London's Science Museum. Richard of Wallingford's Saint Albans astronomical clock (also predicting lunar eclipses) was unmatched in sophistication for roughly two centuries, though the physical clock itself was likely lost in Henry VIII's monastic confiscations — his design notes allowed modern scholars to build a working reconstruction.

🟢 Modern archaeology (Gerry McDonnell, University of Bradford, late 1990s) found slag at Rievaulx Abbey's Laskill outstation with iron content comparable to a modern blast furnace — evidence the monks were on the verge of industrial-scale cast iron production centuries before the actual Industrial Revolution, a potential breakthrough cut short specifically by Henry VIII's dissolution of the monasteries and the resulting collapse of the Cistercians' cross-monastery technology-sharing network.

### Charitable works (previewed here, developed fully in Ch9)

🟢 The Rule mandated hospitality — "all guests who come shall be received as though they were Christ" — with monasteries functioning as free inns for travelers, pilgrims, and the poor. A historian of the Norman abbey of Bec is quoted confirming its doors and bread were open to all nationalities without distinction.

🟡 Specific charitable infrastructure named: Aubrac's nightly "bell of the wanderers" calling lost travelers to shelter; Arbroath's floating "Bell Rock" warning sailors off a hidden reef in Scotland; Copenhagen's traditional founding tied to a monastery caring for shipwrecked sailors; general monastic contributions to roads and bridges.

### Preserving the written word

🟢 Cassiodorus (a retired Roman senator) founded Vivarium monastery in the sixth century with the era's only known library, explicitly prioritizing manuscript copying — though most surviving ancient Latin literature actually passed through other monastic libraries and cathedral schools, not Vivarium specifically.

🟢 A long list of named monks and abbots demonstrating deep, specific familiarity with classical authors is given: Alcuin (Aristotle, Cicero, Lucan, Pliny, Virgil, and more in correspondence); Lupus of Ferrières (Cicero, Horace, Martial, Suetonius, Virgil); Abbo of Fleury (Horace, Sallust, Terence, Virgil); Desiderius/Pope Victor III (oversaw transcription of Horace, Seneca, Cicero, Ovid); Archbishop Alfano (Apuleius, Aristotle, Cicero, Plato, Varro, Virgil); Gerbert of Aurillac/Pope Sylvester II (Horace, Juvenal, Lucan, Persius, Terence, Statius, Virgil); Saint Hildebert, of whom Cardinal Newman suggested he knew Horace nearly by heart.

🟢 Monte Cassino's 11th-century revival is called "the most dramatic single event in the history of Latin scholarship" for that century, credited with rescuing texts including Tacitus's later Annals and Histories, Apuleius's Golden Ass, Seneca's Dialogues, Varro's De lingua latina, Frontinus's De aquis, and lines of Juvenal's sixth satire found nowhere else.

🟢 Monks also preserved the Bible itself through repeated copying (the Lindau and Lindisfarne Gospels are named as artistically embellished examples), and personal book-devotion stories are given by name: Saint Benedict Biscop's five sea voyages collecting library volumes; Lupus's persistent requests to borrow and copy classical texts; Gerbert's similar book-collecting enthusiasm; Saint Maieul of Cluny reading even on horseback. Direct quotes on the value of books come from a monk of Muri ("without study and without books, the life of a monk is nothing") and Saint Hugh of Lincoln (books as "our offensive and defensive arms in time of war... our medicine when we are sick").

### Monks as teachers

🟢 Saint John Chrysostom (4th–5th c.) records Antioch families already sending sons to be educated by monks in his own lifetime. Saint Benedict personally taught sons of Roman nobles. Saint Boniface built a school at every monastery he founded in Germany; Saint Augustine's monks did the same across England; Saint Patrick is credited with sparking Irish monastic scholarship.

🟢 Woods draws a direct comparison to the Mycenaean Greek collapse (c. 12th century BC), which produced three centuries of total illiteracy (the Greek Dark Ages) after invasion — arguing monastic commitment to literacy specifically prevented the same fate from repeating in Europe after Rome's fall.

🟡 Even scholars unsympathetic to the Church are quoted crediting monastic schools with laying the actual foundations of the university system and providing the continuity of thought linking the ancient world to the modern one.

## Meditations (3)

- The monks were not trying to save civilization. They were trying to pray well. The saving was the byproduct.
- A single surviving library, deliberately built by one retired senator, became one of the few threads connecting an entire literary inheritance across centuries.
- The industrial revolution may have been cut short once already, three hundred years early, by a king closing the wrong doors.

## Discussion Points for Teachers

- This chapter is almost entirely a list of specific claims rather than a single argument — which three or four claims here would a skeptical student find most surprising, and which would benefit most from independent verification?
- The McDonnell/Rievaulx claim (monks on the verge of blast-furnace iron production) is a genuinely contested and relatively recent archaeological finding, not settled historical consensus the way Benedict's Rule is. Worth distinguishing "well-established fact" from "live archaeological hypothesis" when using this chapter with students.
- Several quotes here come from historians explicitly described as unsympathetic to the Church (Guizot). Is this pattern (friendly evidence from hostile witnesses) starting to feel like Woods's default rhetorical move across every chapter?

## Socratic Question

"Of all the specific monastic contributions catalogued here — agriculture, clockmaking, book preservation, charity, metallurgy — which one would most surprise someone who thinks of monks purely as men who prayed in isolation, and why does that particular gap in public knowledge exist?"
