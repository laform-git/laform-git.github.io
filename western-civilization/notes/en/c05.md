**Source:** How the Catholic Church Built Western Civilization — Thomas E. Woods, Jr., Ch. 5: "The Church and Science"

## The Insight

This is the book's longest and most heavily documented chapter, built around one question: was it coincidence that modern science arose specifically in a Catholic milieu? Woods runs two arguments in parallel — first, a detailed re-examination of the Galileo affair (the Church's "greatest debit" in popular memory), and second, a much larger positive case that Christian theology itself supplied the philosophical preconditions science needed, developed at length through historian Stanley Jaki's comparative-civilizations thesis, the Chartres cathedral school, the Condemnations of 1277, and an extensive roll call of priest-scientists culminating in the Jesuits.

## Why It Matters

Given the density here, the chapter is best used as a structured reference rather than summarized — organized below by its own major sections so any specific claim can be located and checked.

## Evidence / Support

### The Galileo affair, examined in detail

🟢 Copernicus (1473–1543): probably never ordained despite being called a priest in some popular accounts; came from a family in the Third Order of Saint Dominic; consulted by the Fifth Lateran Council on calendar reform; his astronomy outline drew a favorable Vatican lecture arranged by Pope Clement VII; published Six Books on the Revolutions in 1543 at churchmen's urging, dedicated to Pope Paul III. His heliocentric model kept Ptolemaic/Aristotelian features (perfect spheres, circular orbits, constant speed) — the only change was sun-centered rather than earth-centered.

🟢 Galileo's telescope observations (moons of Jupiter, phases of Venus, lunar mountains) undermined specific Ptolemaic assumptions. He was initially celebrated: Jesuit astronomers (Clavius, Grienberger) confirmed his findings; Pope Paul V gave him a long audience; Cardinal Barberini (the future Urban VIII) wrote him a congratulatory letter after his 1612 sunspot publication.

🟢 The Church's actual objection, per Woods: it had no problem with heliocentrism as a useful hypothesis, but Galileo insisted it was literally, provably true — without adequate proof. His own tidal argument for Earth's motion is now considered mistaken, and he could not answer the standing parallax objection. Historian Jerome Langford is quoted at length concluding Galileo "was convinced he had the truth" but lacked the proof to compel open-minded assent, and that he, not just his opponents, bears real responsibility for what followed by refusing a hypothesis-only compromise.

🟢 Cardinal Bellarmine's famous position is quoted directly: if heliocentrism were actually proven, Scripture's apparent geocentrism would need reinterpreting — he simply held that no such proof yet existed. Saint Albert the Great and Saint Thomas Aquinas are both quoted making the same general point centuries earlier: scriptural interpretation must yield to demonstrated truth, or unbelievers will rightly mock the faith.

🟢 1616: Galileo told to treat Copernicanism as hypothesis only — he agreed. 1624: Urban VIII received him warmly, gave him gifts, and stated the Church had never declared and would never declare heliocentrism heretical, encouraging further work. 1632: Galileo's Dialogue ignored the hypothesis-only instruction. 1633: declared suspect of heresy, ordered to stop publishing on the topic (he still produced his major Two New Sciences afterward).

🟢 J. L. Heilbron is quoted showing contemporaries across Europe (Gassendi 1642, Riccioli 1651, Mengoli 1675, Baldigiani 1678) understood the condemnation carried no binding theological weight. The 1633 decree, unlike 1616, banned even hypothetical discussion of Earth's motion — but since Catholic scientists like Boscovich kept using the idea afterward, scholars suspect it targeted Galileo personally rather than science generally.

### Stanley Jaki's comparative-civilizations thesis

🟢 Jaki (dual doctorate, theology and physics) argues Christian theology's insistence on a rational, orderly, law-governed universe (citing Sirach 42:21 and Wisdom 11:21, "ordered all things by measure, number, weight") was philosophically indispensable to science's birth — not incidental to it.

🟢 In Science and Creation, Jaki argues science suffered "stillbirth" in seven other great cultures (Arabic, Babylonian, Chinese, Egyptian, Greek, Hindu, Maya), each lacking belief in a transcendent Creator who imposed consistent law on a non-divine nature. Babylon treated natural order as so fragile it required annual ritual expiation. China (per Marxist historian Joseph Needham, notably not a Christian apologist) lacked the idea of a celestial lawgiver whose decrees could be rationally deciphered — Needham's own words are quoted directly on this point.

🟢 Greece came closest but fell short: Aristotle assigned things like "love of their natural place" to explain motion, personalizing nature rather than depersonalizing it — Jaki argues the Scholastics' "de-personalization of nature" was the necessary further step.

🟢 Islamic science (real and substantial in medicine and optics, and crucial for transmitting Greek texts west) is argued to have succeeded despite orthodox theology, not because of it — mainstream Islamic theology held that Allah's absolute sovereignty meant natural "laws" were mere habits, revocable at will, undermining the very idea of fixed law scientific inquiry depends on.

🟢 Saint Anselm's distinction between God's potentia ordinata (ordered power, by which He binds Himself to act consistently) and potentia absoluta (absolute power) is presented as the Christian middle path — Aquinas is credited with balancing divine freedom to create any universe against consistent governance of the one actually created, meaning the universe's specific nature had to be discovered by experience, not deduced a priori. Woods notes William of Ockham later over-emphasized absolute divine will in ways unhelpful to science.

🟢 A direct, striking citation: Nietzsche himself is quoted conceding that science always rests on a prior "metaphysical faith," supporting Jaki's claim that science needs philosophical/theological ground to stand on.

🟢 Applied concretely to physics: Aristotle held rest was nature's default state, requiring an explanation for all motion, and struggled badly to explain projectile motion once thrown objects left the thrower's hand. Jean Buridan (14th-century Sorbonne professor), compelled by Christian belief in creation ex nihilo to reject an eternal universe, reasoned that God imparted motion to the heavens at creation and that motion never dissipated in frictionless space — an early, theologically-motivated version of impetus/inertia, later credited as a real precursor to Newton's first law. Jaki is quoted concluding: "science is not Western, but Christian," insofar as this theological consensus produced it. Woods notes Newton and Descartes both obscured their debts to this medieval lineage.

### The Cathedral School of Chartres

🟢 Chartres, reaching maturity in the 12th century under Fulbert (a student of Gerbert of Aurillac/Pope Sylvester II) and later chancellor Thierry, became a hub connecting nearly everyone significant in early Western science. Its west façade personifies the seven liberal arts as ancient teachers (Aristotle, Boethius, Cicero, Euclid, Ptolemy, Pythagoras, and Donatus or Priscian).

🟢 Adelard of Bath and William of Conches, both Chartres-linked, are quoted defending rational investigation of nature as compatible with, not opposed to, faith — William specifically scorns those who call natural investigators heretics for wanting explanations beyond "believe like peasants."

🟢 Thierry explicitly rejected the idea that heavenly bodies were semi-divine or exempt from ordinary physical law, describing them as composed of ordinary water and air, subject to change like everything else — historian Thomas Goldstein calls this circle's work in the mid-12th century the actual launch point of Western science, predicting Thierry will eventually be recognized as one of its true founders.

### The Condemnations of 1277 — restriction that backfired productively

🟢 As newly translated Aristotelian texts (arriving via Arab translations after Christian victories in Spain and Sicily) revealed real contradictions with Christian doctrine (an eternal universe; denial that a vacuum was even possible), the Latin Averroists proposed that Aristotelian conclusions could be logically sound yet still false relative to revelation. Aquinas rejected this instability, arguing genuine faith and genuine reason cannot actually contradict — any apparent conflict signals a misunderstanding somewhere.

🟢 Shortly after Aquinas's death, the Bishop of Paris (without papal involvement, and against what one scholar calls "less than enthusiastic papal approval") issued 219 Condemned Propositions banning specific Aristotelian teachings at the University of Paris.

🟢 Historians Pierre Duhem, A. C. Crombie, and Edward Grant are all cited arguing the condemnations, though restrictive, had a paradoxically liberating effect — forcing scholars to imagine alternatives to strict Aristotelian physics. Once God's omnipotence required conceding a vacuum was at least possible, and once the condemned idea that heavenly motion was driven by "intellective souls" was rejected, thinkers like Buridan, Nicholas Oresme, and Robert Grosseteste pursued mechanistic, de-animated explanations of celestial motion instead — a necessary step, per Jaki, toward treating the universe as something intelligible to human inquiry rather than alive and willful.

### The Scientist-Priest — named figures and their specific work

🟢 Roger Bacon (Franciscan, Oxford): forerunner of scientific method, quoted directly insisting nothing can be adequately known "without experiment."

🟢 Saint Albert the Great/Albertus Magnus (Aquinas's teacher, canonized 1931, later named patron of natural scientists): insisted science must investigate causes directly rather than simply accept authority, quoted saying so in De Mineralibus.

🟢 Robert Grosseteste (Oxford chancellor, Bishop of Lincoln): credited by historian A. C. Crombie as possibly the first to write down a complete, formal set of experimental steps.

🟢 Father Nicolaus Steno (1638–1686, Lutheran convert to Catholicism): dissecting a massive shark's head in 1666, connected its teeth to mysterious "tongue stones" (glossopetrae), which led him to found modern stratigraphy. His three named principles — superposition (lower layers are older), original horizontality (sediment settles flat regardless of later tilting), and lateral continuity (matching strata across a valley were once joined) — remain foundational geology. Beatified by Pope John Paul II.

### The Jesuits — an extensive catalogue

🟢 Collectively credited (per a cited historian) with contributions to pendulum clocks, barometers, reflecting telescopes/microscopes, magnetism, optics, electricity, early observation of Jupiter's bands, the Andromeda nebula, Saturn's rings, tidal theory, and the wave nature of light — with Fermat, Huygens, Leibniz, and Newton among their regular correspondents. One historian calls the Society "the single most important contributor to experimental physics in the seventeenth century." Of 303 mathematicians historian Charles Bossut ranked as most eminent across 2,700 years, 16 were Jesuits — 5% of the list from an order that existed for roughly 2 of those 27 centuries. ~35 lunar craters are named for Jesuit scientists.

🟢 Father Giambattista Riccioli: first to measure a freely falling body's acceleration; compiled the 1651 Almagestum novum (still consulted decades later by England's Astronomer Royal John Flamsteed); ran an extraordinarily precise pendulum experiment (enlisting nine fellow Jesuits to count tens of thousands of oscillations across repeated 24-hour trials) to calculate the constant of gravity.

🟢 Father Francesco Maria Grimaldi: Riccioli's essential collaborator; discovered and named the diffraction of light via a direct experiment (a beam through a pinhole casting a shadow larger than straight-line travel would allow), laying groundwork for the wave theory of light that later scientists built on.

🟢 Father Roger Boscovich (1711–1787): polymath — astronomy, mathematics, optics, poetry; consulted by Pope Benedict XIV on structurally reinforcing St. Peter's Basilica's cracked dome (his iron-ring solution worked); developed the first geometric method to calculate planetary orbits from three position observations; his 1758 Theory of Natural Philosophy is credited by multiple modern scholars as anticipating twentieth-century atomic physics, and was explicitly praised by name by Faraday, Mendeleev, Maxwell, and Kelvin — all quoted directly endorsing "Boscovichianism" as superior to rival atomic models of their own eras. A 1958 Belgrade bicentennial symposium on his work drew papers from Niels Bohr and Werner Heisenberg.

🟢 Father Athanasius Kircher (1602–1680): polymath compared to Leonardo da Vinci; helped debunk alchemy; identified Coptic as a descendant of ancient Egyptian language, credited as effectively founding Egyptology decades before the Rosetta Stone made hieroglyphics broadly legible.

🟢 Jesuit seismology: Father Frederick Odenbach founded what became the Jesuit Seismological Service (1908–9) using the order's network of American colleges as a distributed observation network — called the first continental-scale seismic network with standardized instruments. Father J. B. Macelwane wrote America's first seismology textbook (1936) and has an American Geophysical Union medal named for him.

🟢 Kepler's direct, warm correspondence with Jesuit astronomers is documented — Father Zucchi (inventor of the reflecting telescope) sent Kepler an instrument during a period of financial hardship, prompting a personally grateful letter from Kepler quoted directly.

🟢 Cathedral-as-observatory: Bologna's Basilica of San Petronio functioned as one of the world's most precise solar observatories, via a hole admitting sunlight onto a calibrated floor meridian line. Giovanni Cassini (a student of Riccioli and Grimaldi) used it in the 1650s to confirm Kepler's elliptical orbits were correct over competing circular models — astronomer Jérôme Lalande later called this an epochal moment in scientific history. Between 1655 and 1736 the observatory logged roughly 4,500 solar observations. Heilbron's direct quote is repeated here (also cited in Ch1): the Church funded astronomy for six centuries more generously than any other institution.

## Meditations (3)

- A civilization that believes the universe is alive, willful, and unpredictable has no reason to look for laws inside it.
- Bellarmine's actual position was never "prove nothing exists that contradicts Scripture" — it was "prove it, and we will rethink the reading." That's a narrower claim than the popular version of the story remembers.
- An order of priests produced 5% of history's most eminent mathematicians while existing for roughly 7% of the timeline being measured.

## Discussion Points for Teachers

- The Galileo section here is Woods's most detailed rebuttal in the whole book — does the level of textual and quoted support here (Langford, Bellarmine, Heilbron) meet a higher evidentiary bar than his broader claims in Ch1, or does it just feel that way because the topic is more familiar?
- Jaki's seven-culture "stillbirth" thesis is a strong, contestable claim about entire civilizations' theology causing or preventing scientific development. What would a historian of Islamic or Chinese science who disagrees with Jaki likely say in response?
- The chapter leans heavily on modern scientists (Faraday, Mendeleev, Maxwell, Kelvin, Bohr, Heisenberg) praising Boscovich specifically. Does citing later scientific approval of a churchman's theory function as evidence for the church-and-science thesis, or is it really just evidence Boscovich was a good scientist independent of his vocation?

## Socratic Question

"If the only Church-and-science story most people know is Galileo, what does it mean that this single, genuinely complicated case has done more to shape the public's whole picture than every priest-scientist named in this chapter combined?"
