**Source:** How the Catholic Church Built Western Civilization — Thomas E. Woods, Jr., Ch. 10: "The Church and Western Law"

## The Insight

Legal scholar Harold Berman's central claim anchors this chapter: modern Western law is "a secular residue of religious attitudes" whose roots, once forgotten, make parts of the law look groundless. Woods traces three separate legal contributions: the Gregorian Reform's separation of Church and state (which produced the very idea of two distinct legal systems), canon law as history's first truly systematic legal code, and the surprising origin of natural rights theory in 12th-century commentators on Church law — centuries before the Enlightenment thinkers usually credited with inventing it.

## Why It Matters

The opening example (a condemned murderer kept alive until sane enough to confess before execution) is a small, strange legal detail that Woods uses to demonstrate his larger point: much of Western law makes no sense without its theological origin, even where the theology itself has been forgotten by the people still following the rule.

## Evidence / Support

### The Gregorian Reform — separating Church and state

🟢 Church-state entanglement grew for centuries after Constantine's Edict of Milan (313) despite Saint Ambrose's stated principle ("palaces belong to the emperor, churches to the priesthood") and Pope Gelasius's "two swords" doctrine — secular rulers increasingly appointed bishops, abbots, and eventually even popes.

🟢 Hildebrand (later Pope Gregory VII) led the Gregorian Reform, originally aimed at clerical celibacy and ending simony, which exposed the deeper problem: lay control of Church appointments. Gregory's decisive move was declaring the king simply a layman with no special religious authority — rejecting even the idea, previously entertained, that royal coronation was itself a sacrament.

🟢 This forced clear legal boundaries between Church and state jurisdiction for the first time, and canon law — the first systematic body of law in medieval Europe — became the template secular legal systems would follow.

### Canon law as the first modern legal system

🟢 Before the 12th century, canon law was an unsystematic scatter of council decrees, penitentials, papal statements, and patristic writing, mostly regional rather than universal. The monk Gratian's Concordance of Discordant Canons (Decretum, c. 1140) changed this — Harold Berman calls it possibly "the first comprehensive and systematic legal treatise... in the history of mankind," synthesizing contradictory sources (Old Testament, Gospel, Aristotle, Justinian, Church Fathers, councils) into one coherent body via scholastic method and natural-law theory.

🟢 Berman credits canon law specifically with introducing rational trial procedure (replacing trial by ordeal/combat/oath), consent as the foundation of valid marriage, wrongful intent as the basis of criminal guilt, and equity protections for the poor against the powerful.

🟢 Canon law's marriage doctrine — requiring free consent, invalidating marriages made under duress or based on mistaken identity — is credited by Berman as the direct root of modern contract law's concepts of free will, mistake, duress, and fraud, and helped end barbarian-custom infant marriage.

🟢 Canonists developed sophisticated causation analysis (Woods gives the example of a thrown stone indirectly causing a death through a chain of accident and medical negligence) and the modern principle that insanity, sleep, honest mistake, or involuntary intoxication could exempt someone from criminal liability — but only if the condition was not self-inflicted and truly prevented the person from knowing they acted wrongly.

### Saint Anselm's atonement theory and the depersonalization of crime

🟢 Anselm's Cur Deus Homo addresses why God could not simply forgive sin outright: rebellion against God disrupted the moral order itself, requiring restitution proportionate to the offense — restitution man owes but cannot pay alone, resolved only through the God-Man's mediation.

🟢 Woods argues this theological framework shaped how Western law came to treat crime generally: not primarily as harm to a specific victim, but as a violation of abstract justice/moral order requiring proportionate punishment to "repair" that order — the conceptual root of contracts requiring remedy for breach, torts requiring damages, and property requiring restoration, principles so embedded in Western legal consciousness that alternative systems (built on honor/vengeance, covenant/community, or deterrence/rehabilitation) can feel almost unimaginable by comparison.

### The origins of natural rights — centuries earlier than assumed

🟢 Historian Brian Tierney's research overturns the standard assumption that natural rights theory emerged spontaneously in the 17th century — Tierney traces it to 12th-century "decretists" commenting on Gratian's Decretum, who found the term ius naturale used inconsistently across older sources and, in sorting out the confusion, developed a genuinely new subjective sense: natural right as a power or faculty inhering in the individual person, not just an abstract rule.

🟢 This developed within a broader 12th-century culture already fluent in group rights-language (the investiture controversy, lord/vassal feudal relationships, urban charters) — concrete individual natural rights (e.g., the right to defend oneself in court, held to derive from natural law itself, not mere government grant) emerged from this soil. Historian Kenneth Pennington notes that by 1300, jurists had developed rights language covering property, self-defense, non-Christians, marriage, and legal procedure, all placed beyond the prince's power to revoke because rooted in natural rather than positive law.

🟢 Pope Innocent IV directly rejected an extreme pro-papalist claim that only Christians could legitimately hold property or exercise governmental authority, ruling instead that ownership and jurisdiction belong to "every rational creature," not just believers — a text later cited directly by the Spanish theologians in Ch7's discussion of New World natives' rights.

🟢 A 14th-century internal Franciscan dispute over absolute poverty (the "Spirituals" wing versus more moderate reformers) is noted as an unexpected further contributor — forcing sustained theological debate over the nature of property itself, feeding directly into the questions later 17th-century rights theorists would inherit.

## Meditations (3)

- A legal system can still be obeying a theological argument its own citizens have forgotten was ever theological.
- The idea that even a king is "just a layman" before Church law took real institutional courage to assert — and it's the reason Church and state exist as separate legal systems at all.
- Natural rights were being formally defended by canon lawyers four centuries before anyone associates the idea with Locke or Jefferson.

## Discussion Points for Teachers

- Woods's claim that Western criminal law's whole logic (abstract justice requiring proportionate punishment) traces to Anselm's atonement theory is a strong causal claim about legal history. What would a legal historian who did not share this theological framing say instead about where this logic came from?
- The chapter credits canon law with ending trial by ordeal and infant marriage — genuinely positive reforms. Does crediting the same intellectual tradition with the concept of natural rights (later used to criticize the Church's own conduct, per Ch7) create any tension worth discussing?
- Tierney's natural-rights thesis is presented as settled scholarly consensus. Is there room in this narrative for the possibility that natural-rights language existed in nascent form elsewhere (Roman law, Islamic jurisprudence) that this chapter does not address?

## Socratic Question

"If the concept of natural rights was formally developed by 12th-century Catholic canon lawyers rather than 17th-century Enlightenment philosophers, why does 'natural rights' function today as a secular, almost anti-religious idea in popular understanding?"
