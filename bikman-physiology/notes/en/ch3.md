
# Bikman — Fundamentals of Physiology, Ch.3: Skeletal Muscle Physiology
*From the nerve signal at the neuromuscular junction to calcium release, the cross-bridge cycle, relaxation, fiber types, hypertrophy, fatigue, and the lactate myth.*

*Source: Dr. Benjamin Bikman, Peterson Academy course "Fundamentals of Physiology," Lecture 3 (transcript). Third lecture of a seven-lecture systems course; it builds directly on the electrophysiology (Ch.1) and peripheral nervous system (Ch.2) lectures. Ends with a short student Q&A on brain fuel, lactate, and ketones.*

**In plain words:** muscles move because tiny protein threads inside each muscle cell grab onto each other and pull. A nerve signal releases calcium inside the muscle, the calcium lets the threads grab, and ATP, the cell's energy molecule, lets them let go again. This lecture walks through those steps, explains the different types of muscle fiber, and corrects two common myths: that "lactic acid" causes the burn, and that muscles grow by adding new cells.

**Words in this note:**
- **ATP**: adenosine triphosphate, the cell's energy currency
- **ATPase**: an enzyme that uses ATP for energy
- **ADP**: adenosine diphosphate, "spent" ATP
- **SERCA**: a pump that pulls calcium back so a muscle can relax
- **GLUT4**: the sugar door in muscle and fat cells that insulin opens
- **TRT**: testosterone replacement therapy
- **DHEA**: a hormone made by the adrenal glands that the body turns into sex hormones

## Why This Matters
By weight, muscle is the largest single tissue in most people (about 38% of body weight in men and 31% in women; Janssen 2000), and it is one of the most active tissues in the body when it comes to using fuel. Bikman's view: muscle is the one cell type that physically changes size in milliseconds, and every idea from the earlier lectures (nerve signals, channels opened by chemicals, ATP, active and passive transport, nicotinic receptors) is used here. The only new things are the parts specific to muscle. The lecture also corrects two common beliefs: that "lactic acid" causes the burn, and that bigger muscles come from more muscle cells.

## How Muscle Is Organized — From Whole Muscle to Fiber
- **From big to small:** whole muscle → **fascicles** (bundles of muscle cells) → **muscle fibers** (each fiber is one muscle cell) → **myofibrils** (protein structures inside the cell) → **sarcomeres** (the small repeating units that do the pulling). ✅
- **One nerve per fiber:** each muscle fiber is controlled by a single motor nerve at a **neuromuscular junction**, the meeting point of nerve and muscle (Bikman's "fingers on my hand" picture: one nerve with several touch points on one fiber). ✅ Note: each fiber has one junction, but one motor nerve branches out to many fibers. A nerve and all the fibers it controls form a **motor unit** (Guyton & Hall).
- **Partial effort:** because each fiber has its own nerve connection, a muscle does not have to fire all at once. Lifting something light uses only part of the muscle. ✅ Force is increased by calling in more motor units and firing them faster (Guyton & Hall).

## The Nerve-Muscle Junction — Nothing New, Just New Names
1. A signal (action potential) runs down the **motor nerve**.
2. **Calcium channels that respond to voltage** open at the nerve ending; calcium enters.
3. Calcium pulls tiny bubbles (**vesicles**) full of **acetylcholine**, a nerve chemical, to the edge and releases them into the gap between nerve and muscle.
4. Acetylcholine attaches to **nicotinic cholinergic receptors** on the muscle side (the **motor end plate**). These are ion channels that open in response to a chemical.
5. The muscle membrane **depolarizes** (its voltage flips toward positive) and fires its own signal. ✅

**The Botox example:** botulinum toxin stops the vesicles from releasing acetylcholine. The signal arrives and calcium enters, but no messenger is released, so the muscle cannot be told to contract. The targeted muscle is paralyzed (his example: face and forehead). ✅ The toxin cuts the SNARE proteins that let vesicles merge with the membrane (Dong 2019).

## Getting the Signal Deep Inside (Excitation-Contraction Coupling)
- **T-tubules:** the signal does not just travel across the surface. Tunnels shaped like a letter T carry it deep into the fiber. ✅
- **Sarcoplasmic reticulum (SR):** a mesh-like network running right beside the T-tubules (without touching them), filled with stored calcium. ✅
- **The cork picture:** the signal moving down the T-tubule switches on the **DHP (dihydropyridine) receptor**. This works like pulling a cork: it opens the **ryanodine receptors** on the SR, and calcium "gushes" out of the SR into the cell's fluid. ✅
- **Calsequestrin:** a large molecule inside the SR that holds on to calcium until the next release. Bikman calls it "the babysitter of the calcium." ✅

Releasing calcium is only the first step. It sets up the contraction; it is not the contraction itself.

## Inside the Sarcomere — The Hardware

| Feature | Thick filament | Thin filament |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Protein** | **Myosin** (shaped like a "golf club" with a head) | **Actin** (a "strand of pearls") plus **tropomyosin** and **troponin** |
| **Band** | **A band** (darker) | **I band** (lighter) |
| **Anchor** | **M line**, down the middle of the A band | **Z line**, down the middle of the I band |
| **Key feature** | An **ATP-splitting enzyme (ATPase) on the head**; many heads sticking out all around the filament | **Spots where myosin can grab** on each actin "pearl," covered at rest |

- **A sarcomere runs from one Z line to the next**, repeated again and again along the cell. The striped (striated) look under an electron microscope comes from the alternating A and I bands. ✅
- **How the thin filament is layered:** troponin holds tropomyosin in place, and tropomyosin covers the myosin-grabbing spots on actin. ✅
- **What contraction looks like:** the filaments slide over each other, so the cell gets **shorter and thicker** (he shows this by sliding his hands together). ✅

## The Grab-and-Pull Cycle (Cross-Bridge Cycle) — Step by Step

| Step | What happens | What it allows |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **0. Rest** | Tropomyosin covers the grabbing spots. The myosin head is cocked and holds **used ATP (ADP + a loose phosphate, Pi)**. | Ready but blocked |
| **1. Calcium arrives** | Calcium attaches to **troponin**, which changes shape and moves tropomyosin aside. | Grabbing spots uncovered |
| **2. Attach** | The myosin head locks onto actin, forming the **cross-bridge**. | Bridge formed |
| **3. Phosphate released** | The loose phosphate leaves the head. | Triggers the pull |
| **4. Power stroke** | The head pivots and pulls the actin, the first moment of shortening; **ADP** leaves at the end of the pull. | Force and shortening |
| **5. Detach** | **Fresh ATP** attaches to the empty head and pulls it off actin (still in the bent position). | Release |
| **6. Reset** | The enzyme **splits the ATP** into ADP + phosphate, cocking the head again. | Ready to go again |

- **It repeats as long as calcium is there.** It happens in milliseconds, millions of times per muscle per contraction. ✅
- **Bikman's one-line summary:** calcium controls the thin filament; ATP controls the thick filament. ✅

## Relaxing — Where ATP Matters Twice
- **Stop the signal:** when the nerve stops firing, the DHP/ryanodine "cork" goes back in. ✅
- **SERCA pumps** (sarco-endoplasmic reticulum calcium ATPase) actively pump calcium back into the SR. Because the pump uses ATP to push against a gradient, this is **active transport**. ✅
- **Calcium leaves, troponin relaxes, tropomyosin covers the grabbing spots again,** and the contraction ends. ✅
- **ATP is needed for only two things:** (1) detaching and resetting the myosin head, and (2) pumping calcium back into the SR. ✅ 🟢 Evidence check: textbooks add a third use, the sodium-potassium pump restoring the muscle membrane's salt balance after each signal (Guyton & Hall).
- 🟢 **The irony:** ATP is arguably more important for relaxing than for contracting, but a muscle has to relax before it can contract again.

## Types of Muscle Fiber

| Feature | Type I | Type IIA | Type IIX (older books: IIB) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Nickname** | Slow, oxygen-using ("diesel engine in a big rig") | Fast ("sports car") | Fastest, most powerful |
| **Main fuel** | Mainly fat | Burns fuel much faster than type I | Burns fuel much faster than type I |
| **Twitch speed** | Slow | Fast | Very fast |
| **Myosin enzyme speed** | Slower | Fast | Fast |
| **Calcium clearing** | Slower | Faster | Faster |
| **Power / tiredness** | Low power, can "go all day" | Medium power, tires eventually | Highest power, tires very quickly |
| **Example use** | Posture, back muscles, endurance | Jogging | Sprinting |

- ✅ Three things differ together: how fast the myosin enzyme works, how fast calcium is handled, and which fuel is used. Together they decide what each fiber is good at.
- 🟡 Bikman describes type I as "relies primarily on fat" and type II as "fast glycolytic" (fast sugar-burning) in broad terms; he gives no numbers for the fuel split. ✅ In adult human arm and leg muscle the fastest fibers are type IIX; true type IIB fibers are found in small mammals such as rodents (Schiaffino & Reggiani 2011).

## Growing Muscle — Bigger Cells, Not More Cells
- Regular, challenging exercise makes muscle grow through **hypertrophy**: the **number of muscle cells stays the same**, but each cell gets bigger. ✅
- The mechanism he gives: each cell fills up with more actin and myosin (more thick and thin filaments). ✅
- 🟡 The lecture does not cover the signals that trigger this extra protein (mTOR, satellite cells, hormones); it only describes the result.

## Tiredness and the Lactate Myth
- **Fatigue** means the muscle cannot keep producing the same force. There are two causes, which can overlap: **running out of fuel** (uncommon, but possible in very long events like a marathon) and **build-up of by-products** (more common). ✅
- **Potassium** building up gets in the way of the cell's handling of calcium, which disrupts the grab-and-pull cycle. 🟢 Evidence check: potassium building up outside the fibers mainly makes them harder to fire. Loose phosphate from the breakdown of ATP and creatine phosphate (a quick energy reserve) is a major cause of fatigue that weakens calcium release and the force of each pull (Allen 2008).
- **Hydrogen ions** come from splitting ATP. They make the muscle more acidic and switch on **pain nerves (nociceptors)**. The "burn" is a change in acidity (pH), not a change in temperature. ✅ 🟢 Evidence check: in human experiments, the burning feeling needed hydrogen ions (protons), lactate and ATP together acting on the muscle's sensory nerves (Pollak 2014).
- **Lactate, not lactic acid:** Bikman says humans make **lactate**, not lactic acid. He says lactate does not affect acidity and is a usable fuel: the liver can turn it back into glucose, and the brain can burn it directly. ✅ for lactate as a fuel (Brooks 2018). 🟡 Evidence check on acidity: at the body's normal pH, lactic acid splits almost completely into lactate plus a hydrogen ion, so "no lactic acid" is true only in that sense. Robergs 2004 argues the hydrogen ions come from splitting ATP, not from lactate; Böning & Maassen 2008 argue that making lactic acid is the main cause of exercise acidity. The question is still debated.
- **Where the myth comes from:** a working muscle gets more acidic, and blood lactate rises at the same time. So they look like cause and effect, but both are simply signs of a very active cell. ✅

## Student Q&A — Brain Fuel, Ketones and Exercise
- **The brain's two main fuels** are glucose and ketones (fuel made from fat). At equal levels (his example: 5 mM of each), the brain takes about **70% of its energy from ketones**. His explanation: high ketones signal that little carbohydrate is being eaten, so the brain leaves the liver's glucose for other tissues. 🟢 Evidence check: after weeks of fasting, ketones became the brain's main fuel, about two-thirds of its energy (Owen 1967; Cahill 2006). The "equal 5 mM levels" framing is his. 🔴 The reason (saving glucose for other tissues) is his interpretation.
- **Lactate** is also a good brain fuel. A low-lactate IV drip is mentioned as a therapy after a traumatic brain injury. 🟡 Evidence check: small studies of sodium lactate drips after severe brain injury improved signs of brain energy (Bouzat 2014), but it is not standard care.
- **Exercising early in a fast** gets you into ketosis sooner (his group published a paper on this), because it lowers glucose and insulin and speeds up fat burning. 🟢 Evidence check: in his group's trial of 20 adults, who each did both conditions, treadmill exercise at the start of a 36-hour fast raised ketone (BHB) levels over the fast, but exercise did not lower insulin any further (Deru 2021).
- **Brain disorders:** he names reduced glucose use by the brain as a common thread (migraine, Alzheimer's, Parkinson's, bipolar disorder) and says raising ketones helps in each: clearer thinking in Alzheimer's, migraines stopping "almost instantly." He is clear that this does not reverse the disease. 🟡 These are his claims in a Q&A answer, not backed with data in the lecture. 🟡 Evidence check: the ketogenic diet is an established treatment for epilepsy that does not respond to drugs (Martin-McGill 2020). For Alzheimer's, only small, short trials exist (for example Phillips 2021, 26 patients, 12 weeks), and evidence for migraine and bipolar disorder is at an early stage. "Almost instantly" is not supported.

## Punchlines & Quotable Facts (Lecture 3)
- **"By mass, most people are more muscle than they are anything else."** 🟡 Muscle is the largest single tissue in lean adults (about 31–38% of body weight; Janssen 2000), but fat can outweigh it in people with obesity.
- **ATP is needed for only two parts of the process:** resetting the myosin head and pumping calcium back. Not every step of contraction burns ATP directly.
- **"ATP is actually more important to the muscle relaxing than it is to contracting."**
- **Calcium is the "uncorking": the DHP receptor is the cork, the ryanodine receptor is the opening.**
- **"Humans don't make lactic acid. You don't have a single speck of lactic acid in your bodies right now."** 🟡 See the evidence check in the lactate section: true in the sense that it is almost fully split apart, but whether making lactate drives acidity is debated.
- **Muscle burn is a change in acidity from hydrogen ions, not heat, and not lactate.**
- **Hypertrophy: the same number of cells, each one bigger.**
- **At equal levels, the brain takes about 70% of its energy from ketones.** 🟢 Studies of long fasting give about two-thirds (Owen 1967; Cahill 2006).

## Key Corrections to Carry Forward
- 🔴 "Lactic acid causes the muscle burn": there is no lactic acid in the human body, lactate does not change acidity, and it is a fuel. The burn follows hydrogen ions from splitting ATP. 🟡 The fuel part is solid; the acidity part is debated (Robergs 2004; Böning & Maassen 2008).
- 🔴 "Muscle grows by adding cells": in this framework, growth is hypertrophy, with the same number of cells and more pulling protein in each.
- 🔴 "ATP powers contraction and calcium just triggers it": ATP is also needed to let go and to pump calcium out. Without fresh ATP, myosin stays stuck and the muscle cannot relax.
- 🔴 "The whole muscle always contracts together": fibers with their own nerve connections allow partial, graded effort.
- 🔴 "Muscle contraction is one process": it is three linked ones (electrical signal, calcium release, filament pulling), and each has its own point where it can fail or be blocked (for example, Botox at the first).

## Practical Takeaway
The lecture explains mechanisms and gives no training plan, so its practical value is in the ideas. The burn is an acidity signal rather than a lactate problem. Endurance and power rely on different fiber types with different fuel preferences. And muscle size reflects cell size. The Q&A adds one idea that sounds actionable (exercising early in a fast speeds up ketosis), which is Bikman's reported finding and best treated as a pointer rather than a plan.

## Questions to Learn From (Socratic) — with Answer Options
**1. ATP is needed both to release myosin and to pump calcium back into the SR. What would you expect to happen to a muscle that runs out of ATP in the middle of a contraction?**
A. It relaxes immediately because there is no energy to keep contracting
B. It stays weak but can relax normally once the calcium clears
C. \* It can get stuck contracted, because myosin cannot let go of actin and calcium cannot be pumped back into the SR, so the contraction cannot be released
*Why C: the lecture shows that ATP attaching is what pulls myosin off actin and what powers the SERCA pump, so a lack of ATP blocks relaxing rather than causing it. This is a logical step from the lecture's mechanism, not a case Bikman describes.*

**2. A friend says a hard set "burns" because lactic acid builds up in the muscle. Based on this lecture, what is the best correction?**
A. \* There is no lactic acid in the body; the burn comes from hydrogen ions released when ATP is split, which make the muscle acidic and switch on pain nerves, while lactate rises at the same time but does not cause it
B. Lactic acid is real but is cleared within seconds, so it cannot cause burning
C. The burn is caused by the muscle heating up as the myosin heads work
*Why A: Bikman says lactate does not affect acidity, that the burn is a change in acidity rather than temperature, and that lactate and acidity just happen together in an active cell.*

**3. Botulinum toxin (Botox) and the nerve-muscle junction: which step does it block, and what does the muscle do?**
A. It blocks the receptor on the muscle, so acetylcholine is released but cannot attach
B. \* It blocks the release of the vesicles, so calcium still enters the nerve ending but acetylcholine is never released, and the muscle gets no signal to contract
C. It blocks the ryanodine receptor, so the SR cannot release calcium
*Why B: in the lecture the signal travels down the nerve and calcium enters, but the vesicles cannot release acetylcholine, so the muscle is paralyzed.*

**4. Why does a type IIX fiber tire faster but produce more power than a type I fiber?**
A. It has fewer myosin heads, which saves energy
B. It stores more calcium, which delays tiredness
C. \* Its myosin enzyme and calcium handling are faster, so it cycles the grab-and-pull and burns fuel much faster, giving high power and speed but quick tiredness
*Why C: the lecture links twitch speed to how fast the enzyme works and how fast calcium is cleared, and describes type IIX as high power, high speed and quick to tire, against type I as low power but able to go all day.*
