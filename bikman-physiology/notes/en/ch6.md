
# Bikman — Fundamentals of Physiology, Ch.6: Endocrine Physiology
*Hormone classes, the hypothalamic-pituitary axis (growth hormone, thyroid, adrenal), and the pancreas — ending on how insulin resistance becomes type 2 diabetes.*

*Source: Dr. Benjamin Bikman, Peterson Academy course "Fundamentals of Physiology," Lecture 6 (transcript). Same lecturer as the "Why We Get Sick" and "Essentials of Endocrinology" series — this is the compact, physiology-level version of the endocrine system, with disorders as the teaching device.*

## Why This Matters
This is the one "system" lecture in the course that is not a single organ — it is about signals moving between organs. Its two most reusable ideas: (1) **negative feedback lets you predict whether a hormone goes up or down**, and (2) **a hormone's chemistry (peptide, amine, steroid) dictates how it travels and how it signals**. It also closes with the cleanest short statement of how insulin resistance matures into type 2 diabetes.

## Scope — What a Hormone Is
A hormone lets one cell type in one tissue signal a different cell type in a different tissue — "the telegram or the email." ✅ Example: there is no direct nerve from muscle to fat, so exercising muscle releases hormones that tell fat "we need energy," and fat breaks down its stores.

## Punchlines & Quotable Facts
- **"If you can remember negative feedback, then you can almost always anticipate the direction these hormones are going."**
- **Free vs. bound: roughly 1,000 times more lipid-soluble hormone is bound than free** — the bound pool is a protected reservoir; only the free pool acts. 🟡 Evidence check: the ratio varies widely by hormone: about 0.03% of T4 is free (several thousand to one), but roughly 2% of testosterone and 5–10% of cortisol (Guyton & Hall; Mendel 1989).
- **Growth hormone is a metabolic hormone, not just a growth hormone** — it mobilizes fat and liver glycogen to fuel growth. 🟢 Evidence check: its most prominent metabolic effect is lipolysis; it raises glucose mainly by making liver and muscle resistant to insulin, and it tends to *spare* glycogen rather than release it (Møller & Jørgensen 2009).
- **TSH sends two different signals: make thyroid hormone AND grow the gland** — the key to understanding goiter.
- **Brown fat is the main producer of active T3 when turned on by cold** — but the T4 still comes from the thyroid. 🔴 Evidence check: in rodents, cold-activated brown fat makes T3 for its own use; in humans, most circulating T3 is made from T4 in skeletal muscle and liver, and a role for human brown fat as the main source is unverified (Maia 2005; Bianco 2019).
- **"Salt, sugar, sex. The deeper you go, the sweeter it gets."** — the three adrenal cortex layers.
- **Insulin always wins the insulin-glucagon fight** — it directly shuts off the alpha cell.
- **"The canary in the metabolic coal mine"** — rising insulin, years before glucose moves.
- **"Too much of something will result in a resistance to that something."**

## The Three Hormone Classes — Full Comparison

| Feature | Peptide | Amine | Steroid |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Built from** | String of amino acids | One or two amino acids (tyrosine or tryptophan) | Cholesterol (the steroid nucleus) |
| **Examples** | Pancreas, hypothalamus, pituitary, and gut hormones (the majority of all hormones) | Thyroid hormone, epinephrine/norepinephrine (tyrosine); melatonin (tryptophan) | Cortisol and other adrenal corticoids; all estrogens and androgens |
| **Water or lipid soluble** | Water-soluble | Catecholamines: water-soluble. Thyroid hormone: lipid-soluble | Lipid-soluble |
| **Blood transport** | Free, unregulated (exceptions: IGF-1 and part of growth hormone travel bound) | Catecholamines: free. Thyroid: needs a carrier | **Needs a carrier** (escort protein) |
| **How it signals** | Knocks on a surface receptor; second messengers do the work | Catecholamines: surface receptor. Thyroid: enters the cell | **Slips through the membrane** — binds an inside receptor or goes to the nucleus and turns genes on |

✅ The "knock on the door" vs. "thief in the night" analogy: water-soluble hormones cannot cross the fatty membrane, so a chain of second messengers (dog barks, kids run, someone fetches me) delivers the message. Lipid-soluble hormones cross the membrane and ARE the messenger — no second messenger needed.

## Free vs. Bound Pools (Lipid-Soluble Hormones)
- **Free pool:** tiny, but the only fraction that can act. ✅
- **Bound pool:** about 1,000x larger; the carrier protein protects the hormone from degradation and holds it as a reservoir that can release fast when needed. ✅
- While bound, a hormone cannot signal. ✅

## Three Signaling Distances
- **Autocrine:** the cell releases a hormone that acts back on itself ✅
- **Paracrine:** acts on an adjacent, different cell type; never enters the blood — the pancreatic alpha-beta relationship is the example ✅
- **Endocrine:** released into the blood, acts throughout the body ✅

## The Hypothalamic-Pituitary Axis
- The hypothalamus sends releasing hormones through a **portal system** (blood passes capillaries, a vessel, then capillaries again — same design as the liver portal system) a very short distance to the anterior pituitary. ✅
- **CEO / manager / line worker:** hypothalamus (tertiary gland), pituitary (secondary), end gland (primary). ✅
- **Growth hormone** is the exception: it comes straight from the pituitary and acts on its own. **Thyroid and adrenal** pathways add a third gland. ✅
- The pancreas sits **outside** this axis — it acts on its own. ✅

## Growth Hormone
**GHRH (hypothalamus) → growth hormone (pituitary) → direct effects plus liver IGF-1.** Growth hormone is unique among pituitary hormones because it acts directly, and it also tells the liver to release **somatomedins (IGFs, mainly IGF-1)**. ✅
- **Growth effects:** muscle growth, long-bone growth, and body getting "bigger and thicker" via growth hormone plus IGF-1. ✅
- **Metabolic effects:** stimulates **lipolysis** (raising free fatty acids) and tells the liver to release **stored glycogen** (raising blood glucose) — energy to fuel growth. ✅ for lipolysis. 🟢 Evidence check: growth hormone raises glucose mainly by opposing insulin in liver and muscle, while sparing glycogen stores (Møller & Jørgensen 2009).

| Condition | When hypersecretion starts | What happens |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Gigantism** | Before long bones stop growing (adolescence) | Extreme height (over 7 feet in severe cases; the tallest recorded man reached about 8 ft 11 in); usually a growth-hormone-secreting tumor |
| **Acromegaly** | After long bones have stopped growing | Normal or slightly tall height; continued growth of facial bones, jaw, hands, feet (coarse features); heart thickens |

✅ The deadly part of acromegaly: **hypertrophic cardiomyopathy** — the heart wall thickens until the chamber compresses and cannot fill (the filling problem from the cardiopulmonary lecture). Constant glycogen release also keeps glucose and insulin high, pushing toward **insulin resistance and type 2 diabetes**. ✅ 🟢 Evidence check: the heart disease of acromegaly is usually called acromegalic cardiomyopathy (both ventricles thicken, filling is impaired, heart failure can follow) and is a leading cause of death in untreated disease (Colao 2004); the diabetes risk comes from growth hormone's insulin-opposing effect (Møller & Jørgensen 2009).

## Thyroid Axis
**TRH (hypothalamus) → TSH (anterior pituitary) → thyroid → mostly T4, some T3.**
- **T4 is the inactive form, T3 the active form.** Tissues convert T4 to T3 after taking it up. ✅
- **Brown adipose tissue** (high metabolic rate, scattered through the chest) becomes the main T3 producer when switched on by cold exposure. 🔴 Evidence check: shown in rodents; in humans most circulating T3 comes from skeletal muscle and liver (Maia 2005; Bianco 2019). Bikman says he is generally an advocate of cold immersion.
- **TSH has two effects:** stimulates thyroid hormone production AND thyroid growth. ✅
- **Thyroid hormone is the metabolic "choke valve" / idle of the engine** for every cell: high means each cell does what it does faster and more; low means slower. ✅

| System | Hyperthyroid (everything dialed up) | Hypothyroid (everything delayed) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Weight / temperature** | Rapid weight loss, heat intolerance, sweating, thirst | Weight gain, always cold |
| **Heart** | Rapid pulse, arrhythmias, excess clotting | Too slow |
| **Nerve / muscle** | Spasms, tremors | Slow responses; delayed reflexes (knee-jerk kicks out late) |
| **Gut** | Fast peristalsis, diarrhea | Slow peristalsis, constipation |
| **Eyes** | Exophthalmos (fat pads behind the eye swell; lid lag) — bulging eyes are specific to Graves' disease, while lid lag can occur in any hyperthyroidism | Puffy, edematous skin around the eyes (looks sunken) |

### Thyroid Disorders
- **Hashimoto's thyroiditis:** the most common thyroid problem in the US. Autoimmune attack on the thyroid; early on it swings between hyper and hypo, trending toward true hypothyroidism. ✅ Bikman: most autoimmune diseases have an environmental trigger; the most common one here is **gluten** — people with gluten sensitivity or celiac are about **20x more likely** to develop Hashimoto's. He acknowledges this is likely to be controversial but says the literature is robust. 🟡 Taken as his stated claim; no study is named in the lecture. 🔴 Evidence check: in a Swedish national cohort, people with celiac disease had about 4x the risk of hypothyroidism and 3.6x the risk of thyroiditis, not 20x (Elfström 2008); that gluten is the most common trigger of Hashimoto's is unverified.
- **Graves' disease:** autoimmune, but the antibodies **mimic TSH** — the gland is told both to over-produce hormone and to grow. Result: hyperthyroidism plus goiter. ✅
- **Goiter = enlarged thyroid, and it does NOT tell you hyper vs. hypo.** ✅

| Cause of goiter | TSH | Thyroid hormone state |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Iodine deficiency** (most common cause worldwide; in iodine-sufficient countries, Hashimoto's and nodular goiter dominate) | Very high (no negative feedback, because no hormone can be made) | Hypothyroid — gland grows in response to TSH but lacks the iodine building block |
| **Graves' disease** (TSH-mimicking antibodies) | Not the driver; antibodies act in its place | Hyperthyroid |
| **TSH-secreting pituitary tumor** | Too high | Hyperthyroid |

## Adrenal Glands
An orange: the **cortex** (thick peel) and the **medulla** (flesh) are essentially separate glands wrapped together. The cortex has three zones, each making steroid hormones: ✅

| Layer (outer to inner) | Family | Mnemonic |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Outer | Mineralocorticoids | **Salt** |
| Middle | Glucocorticoids (cortisol) | **Sugar** |
| Inner (zona reticularis) | Sex hormones | **Sex** — becomes the main sex-hormone source in postmenopausal women |

### Cushing Syndrome — Too Much Cortisol
From an ACTH-driving pituitary tumor or a tumor of the adrenal cortex itself. ✅ The most common cause overall is glucocorticoid medication (Nieman 2008). Five impacts: ✅
- **Catecholamines:** does not raise epinephrine/norepinephrine but **amplifies sensitivity** to them (same epinephrine gives a bigger heart-rate response).
- **Sex hormones:** tells the gonads "too stressed to reproduce" — estrogens and androgens fall, ovulation can stop, sperm production can be compromised.
- **Fat:** selective — **lipolysis in the limbs, lipogenesis in the trunk, neck, and face** (truncal obesity, buffalo hump).
- **Protein:** strips protein from muscle, bone, and skin (collagen) to send amino acids to the liver for glucose — stretch marks, muscle loss, weak bones.
- **Glucose:** raises it at any cost — amino acids to glucose, liver glycogen breakdown, **muscle and liver made insulin resistant** — ending in insulin resistance and type 2 diabetes.

### Other Adrenal Disorders
- **Sex-hormone tumors of the cortex:** too much estrogen in a man gives **feminization**; too much androgen in a woman gives **virilization**. All sexes make both estrogens and androgens; only the ratio differs. ✅
- **Pheochromocytoma:** tumor of the adrenal **medulla** secreting epinephrine (and often norepinephrine; high blood pressure, often in spikes, is the hallmark; Lenders 2014) — constant panic-attack feeling, pounding heart, sweaty palms, elevated glucose from epinephrine driving liver glucose release. ✅

## The Pancreas — Outside the Axis
- The **endocrine pancreas** is the islets: **alpha cells make glucagon, beta cells make insulin.** ✅
- **Paracrine tug-of-war:** glucagon stimulates beta cells to release insulin; insulin tells alpha cells to stop releasing glucagon. **Insulin always wins.** ✅
- Insulin is stimulated when glucose is high and lowers it; glucagon is stimulated when glucose is low and raises it. ✅

## Insulin Resistance to Type 2 Diabetes
- Scale (Bikman's figures): about half of adults worldwide are overweight, **over half have insulin resistance**, and in the US "upwards of near 80%" have some degree of it. 🟡 Presented as his estimates; worse in Mexico, the Middle East, Asia, and the Pacific Islands. 🟡 Evidence check: the WHO puts overweight (including obesity) at 45% of adults in 2024 (WHO 2026). In NHANES, about 40–45% of non-diabetic US adults aged 18–44 had insulin resistance by HOMA-IR (Parcha 2022); the 80% figure is unverified and depends on the cutoff used.
- **Definition:** some cells (not all) respond less to insulin, and **blood insulin rises (hyperinsulinemia)** to compensate — polite knocking becomes an angry mob. ✅
- **Insulin's most famous effect is not its most important one.** It opens glucose doors in **muscle and fat** (other tissues do not need insulin to take up glucose). ✅
- **Missed because of a glucose-centric paradigm:** insulin climbs for years while glucose stays normal; type 2 diabetes is detected 10 to 20 years later, when compensation fails. Measuring insulin would catch it while it is still correctable. 🟢 Evidence check: in the Whitehall II cohort, insulin sensitivity fell steeply over the 5 years before diagnosis and glucose rose sharply only in the last 3 (Tabák 2009). 🟡 "10 to 20 years" and the value of routine insulin testing are his views; fasting insulin is not a standardized screening test in current guidelines.

| Cause of insulin resistance | Mechanism |
| --- | --- |
| **Stress** | Cortisol and epinephrine raise glucose, opposing insulin, so insulin works harder |
| **Inflammation** | Illness, autoimmune disease, and inhaled irritants such as cigarette smoke or diesel exhaust |
| **Too much insulin** | Constant refined starches and sugars; too much of something produces resistance to it. 🟡 Evidence check: experiments show high insulin can cause insulin resistance, but whether it is usually the cause or the consequence is debated (Shanik 2008) |

**The domino sequence (Bikman's view: fat-first):** fat cell becomes resistant first, then three more dominoes decide glucose control: 🟡 The fat-first order is his hypothesis; the standard model lists muscle, liver, fat, alpha cells and failing beta cells among eight contributors without a fixed order (DeFronzo 2009).
- **Muscle** takes up about **80%** of a starchy or sugary meal's glucose (🟢 this figure comes from insulin-infusion clamp studies; after an ordinary meal, the liver and other tissues take a larger share; DeFronzo 2009); resistant muscle does not open its glucose doors, so glucose stays in the blood.
- **Liver:** resistant liver breaks down glycogen instead of storing it — "a disobedient child."
- **Alpha cells:** resistant alpha cells ignore insulin's stop signal and pump out glucagon, which tells the liver to release even more glucose.
- Once all three are involved, glucose climbs dramatically — insulin resistance has matured into **type 2 diabetes**. There is no type 2 diabetes without insulin resistance first. 🟡 Evidence check: diabetes appears only when beta cells can no longer compensate, so failing insulin secretion is the other essential half; in some lean patients it is the dominant defect (DeFronzo 2009).

## Key Corrections to Carry Forward
- 🔴 Assuming a goiter means an overactive thyroid: it can be hypo (iodine deficiency) or hyper (Graves'). Enlargement and hormone output are two different TSH signals.
- 🔴 Assuming T4 is the working thyroid hormone: T4 is the inactive form; T3 is the active form, and conversion happens in tissues (brown fat especially, when cold-activated). 🔴 In humans, mainly skeletal muscle and liver (Maia 2005).
- 🔴 Assuming growth hormone only builds tissue: it also mobilizes fat and liver glycogen, and excess drives insulin resistance.
- 🔴 Assuming insulin resistance is detected by glucose: glucose stays normal for years while insulin rises.
- 🔴 Assuming the adrenal gland is one gland: the cortex (salt, sugar, sex) and medulla (epinephrine) are essentially separate.

## Practical Takeaway
When any hormone result looks odd, ask the negative-feedback question first: what signal would normally switch this off, and is it present? Iodine deficiency, Graves', and Cushing's all become predictable that way. For metabolic health, the lecture's practical message is to look at insulin, not only glucose.

## Questions to Learn From (Socratic) — with Answer Options
**1. A patient has an enlarged thyroid, very high TSH, and signs of hypothyroidism. What is the most likely cause, and why does the gland grow?**
A. Graves' disease, because TSH-mimicking antibodies push the gland to over-produce hormone
B. \* Iodine deficiency: without iodine no thyroid hormone can be made, so there is no negative feedback, TSH stays very high, and the gland still responds to TSH's growth signal even though it cannot make hormone
C. A cold-activated brown fat problem converting too much T4 to T3
*Why B: TSH has two separate effects (make hormone, grow gland). Without iodine, only the growth signal can be obeyed.*

**2. Why does a high cortisol state such as Cushing syndrome produce both muscle loss and central fat gain?**
A. Cortisol lowers glucose, so the body burns muscle to compensate
B. Cortisol selectively raises fat storage only, and the muscle loss is unrelated
C. \* Cortisol raises blood glucose at any cost, stripping amino acids from muscle, bone, and skin for liver glucose production, while selectively mobilizing limb fat and storing fat in the trunk, neck, and face
*Why C: the lecture's central cortisol principle is raising glucose, with selective fat redistribution on top.*

**3. Why is insulin resistance usually caught late?**
A. \* The usual screening focus is glucose, which stays normal while insulin climbs for years; type 2 diabetes is only recognized 10 to 20 years later when compensation fails
B. Insulin resistance produces no change in any blood marker until diabetes appears
C. Insulin levels fall early in insulin resistance, so they are hard to measure
*Why A: hyperinsulinemia is half the definition. The "glucose-centric paradigm" misses it.*

**4. In the insulin-glucagon relationship, what changes when the alpha cell becomes insulin resistant?**
A. Beta cells stop making insulin, so glucagon is no longer needed
B. The alpha cell makes less glucagon because it hears insulin more strongly
C. \* Insulin's stop signal no longer reaches the alpha cell, so it pumps out glucagon, which tells the liver to break down glycogen and release more glucose
*Why C: insulin normally wins by directly inhibiting the alpha cell. Losing that control is the third domino in the progression to type 2 diabetes.*
