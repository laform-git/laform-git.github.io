
# Bikman — Fundamentals of Physiology, Ch.6: Endocrine Physiology
*Hormone classes, the hypothalamic-pituitary axis (growth hormone, thyroid, adrenal), and the pancreas — ending on how insulin resistance becomes type 2 diabetes.*

*Source: Dr. Benjamin Bikman, Peterson Academy course "Fundamentals of Physiology," Lecture 6 (transcript). Same lecturer as the "Why We Get Sick" and "Essentials of Endocrinology" series — this is the compact, physiology-level version of the endocrine system, with disorders as the teaching device.*

**In plain words:** hormones are chemical messages that one part of the body sends through the blood to another part. Most of them work like a thermostat: when there is enough, the body turns production down. This lecture explains the main types of hormone, the control center in the brain, and what goes wrong with growth hormone, the thyroid, the adrenal glands and the pancreas. It ends with how insulin resistance slowly turns into type 2 diabetes, often years before blood sugar looks abnormal.

**Words in this note:**
- **TSH**: thyroid-stimulating hormone, the brain's signal to the thyroid
- **IGF-1**: insulin-like growth factor 1, the hormone growth hormone works through
- **TRH**: thyrotropin-releasing hormone, the brain's signal that starts TSH
- **WHO**: World Health Organization
- **NHANES**: a large U.S. government health and nutrition survey
- **HOMA-IR**: a score calculated from fasting sugar and fasting insulin to estimate insulin resistance
- **ApoB**: apolipoprotein B; counts all the cholesterol particles that can clog arteries
- **TRT**: testosterone replacement therapy
- **DHEA**: a hormone made by the adrenal glands that the body turns into sex hormones
- **ACTH**: adrenocorticotropic hormone, the brain's signal to the adrenal glands to make cortisol

## Why This Matters
This is the one "system" lecture in the course that is not a single organ. It is about signals moving between organs. Its two most useful ideas: (1) **negative feedback (the thermostat principle) lets you predict whether a hormone will go up or down**, and (2) **a hormone's chemistry (peptide, amine or steroid) decides how it travels and how it sends its message**. It also ends with the clearest short account of how insulin resistance grows into type 2 diabetes.

## What a Hormone Is
A hormone lets one type of cell in one tissue send a message to a different type of cell in a different tissue: "the telegram or the email." ✅ Example: there is no direct nerve from muscle to fat, so exercising muscle releases hormones that tell fat "we need energy," and fat breaks down its stores.

## Punchlines & Quotable Facts
- **"If you can remember negative feedback, then you can almost always anticipate the direction these hormones are going."**
- **Free vs. bound: roughly 1,000 times more fat-soluble hormone is attached to carriers (bound) than floating loose (free).** The bound pool is a protected reserve; only the free pool acts. 🟡 Evidence check: the ratio varies widely by hormone. About 0.03% of T4 (the main thyroid hormone) is free (several thousand to one), but roughly 2% of testosterone and 5–10% of cortisol (Guyton & Hall; Mendel 1989).
- **Growth hormone is a fuel hormone, not just a growth hormone.** He says it releases fat and stored liver sugar to fuel growth. 🟢 Evidence check: its most prominent fuel effect is fat release (lipolysis). It raises blood sugar mainly by making the liver and muscle resistant to insulin, and it tends to *spare* stored sugar (glycogen) rather than release it (Møller & Jørgensen 2009).
- **TSH, the thyroid-stimulating hormone, sends two different signals: make thyroid hormone AND grow the gland.** This is the key to understanding goiter (an enlarged thyroid).
- **Brown fat is the main producer of active T3 when switched on by cold**, he says, though the T4 still comes from the thyroid. 🔴 Evidence check: in rodents, cold-activated brown fat makes T3 for its own use. In humans, most T3 in the blood is made from T4 in skeletal muscle and the liver, and a role for human brown fat as the main source is not confirmed (Maia 2005; Bianco 2019).
- **"Salt, sugar, sex. The deeper you go, the sweeter it gets."** The three layers of the outer adrenal gland.
- **Insulin always wins the insulin-glucagon fight.** It directly shuts off the alpha cell that makes glucagon.
- **"The canary in the metabolic coal mine":** rising insulin, years before blood sugar changes.
- **"Too much of something will result in a resistance to that something."**

## The Three Hormone Types — Full Comparison

| Feature | Peptide | Amine | Steroid |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Built from** | A string of amino acids | One or two amino acids (tyrosine or tryptophan) | Cholesterol (the steroid core) |
| **Examples** | Hormones of the pancreas, hypothalamus, pituitary and gut (most hormones) | Thyroid hormone, adrenaline/noradrenaline (from tyrosine); melatonin (from tryptophan) | Cortisol and other adrenal hormones (corticoids); all estrogens and androgens |
| **Dissolves in water or fat** | Water | Catecholamines (adrenaline-type): water. Thyroid hormone: fat | Fat |
| **Travel in the blood** | Loose, unregulated (exceptions: IGF-1 and part of growth hormone travel attached) | Catecholamines: loose. Thyroid: needs a carrier | **Needs a carrier** (an escort protein) |
| **How it sends its message** | Knocks on a receptor on the cell surface; second messengers inside the cell do the work | Catecholamines: surface receptor. Thyroid: enters the cell | **Slips through the membrane**: attaches to a receptor inside or goes to the nucleus and switches genes on |

✅ The "knock on the door" vs. "thief in the night" picture: water-soluble hormones cannot cross the fatty membrane, so a chain of second messengers (the dog barks, the kids run, someone fetches me) delivers the message. Fat-soluble hormones cross the membrane and ARE the messenger; no second messenger is needed.

## Free vs. Bound Pools (Fat-Soluble Hormones)
- **Free pool:** tiny, but the only part that can act. ✅
- **Bound pool:** about 1,000 times larger. The carrier protein protects the hormone from being broken down and holds it as a reserve that can be released quickly when needed. ✅
- While a hormone is attached to its carrier, it cannot send a message. ✅

## Three Signaling Distances
- **Autocrine:** the cell releases a hormone that acts back on itself. ✅
- **Paracrine:** acts on a neighboring cell of a different type and never enters the blood. The pancreas's alpha and beta cells are the example. ✅
- **Endocrine:** released into the blood and acts throughout the body. ✅

## The Brain's Hormone Control Center (Hypothalamus-Pituitary Axis)
- The hypothalamus, a control area at the base of the brain, sends "releasing hormones" through a **portal system** (blood passes through capillaries, then a vessel, then capillaries again, the same design as the liver's portal system) a very short distance to the front part of the pituitary gland (the anterior pituitary). ✅
- **CEO / manager / line worker:** the hypothalamus is the CEO (tertiary gland), the pituitary the manager (secondary), and the end gland the line worker (primary). ✅
- **Growth hormone** is the exception: it comes straight from the pituitary and acts on its own. The **thyroid and adrenal** pathways add a third gland. ✅
- The pancreas sits **outside** this chain of command; it acts on its own. ✅

## Growth Hormone
**GHRH (from the hypothalamus) → growth hormone (from the pituitary) → direct effects plus IGF-1 from the liver.** Growth hormone is unusual among pituitary hormones because it acts directly, and it also tells the liver to release **somatomedins (growth factors called IGFs, mainly IGF-1)**. ✅
- **Growth effects:** muscle growth, growth of the long bones, and the body getting "bigger and thicker" through growth hormone plus IGF-1. ✅
- **Fuel effects:** switches on **fat release (lipolysis)**, raising free fatty acids in the blood, and, he says, tells the liver to release **stored glycogen**, raising blood sugar. This is energy to fuel growth. ✅ for fat release. 🟢 Evidence check: growth hormone raises blood sugar mainly by opposing insulin in the liver and muscle, while sparing the stored glycogen (Møller & Jørgensen 2009).

| Condition | When too much growth hormone starts | What happens |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Gigantism** | Before the long bones stop growing (adolescence) | Extreme height (over 7 feet in severe cases; the tallest recorded man reached about 8 ft 11 in); usually caused by a tumor that releases growth hormone |
| **Acromegaly** | After the long bones have stopped growing | Normal or slightly tall height; the bones of the face, jaw, hands and feet keep growing (coarse features); the heart thickens |

✅ The deadly part of acromegaly: **hypertrophic cardiomyopathy**, a thickening of the heart wall until the chamber is squeezed and cannot fill (the filling problem from the heart and lungs lecture). Constant release of stored sugar also keeps blood sugar and insulin high, pushing toward **insulin resistance and type 2 diabetes**. ✅ 🟢 Evidence check: the heart disease of acromegaly is usually called acromegalic cardiomyopathy (both ventricles thicken, filling is impaired, and heart failure can follow). It is a leading cause of death when the disease is not treated (Colao 2004). The diabetes risk comes from growth hormone's insulin-opposing effect (Møller & Jørgensen 2009).

## The Thyroid Chain
**TRH (hypothalamus) → TSH (front of the pituitary) → thyroid → mostly T4, some T3.**
- **T4 is the inactive form; T3 is the active form.** Tissues turn T4 into T3 after taking it up. ✅
- **Brown fat** (a fat that burns energy fast to make heat, scattered through the chest), he says, becomes the main T3 producer when switched on by cold. 🔴 Evidence check: shown in rodents; in humans, most T3 in the blood comes from skeletal muscle and the liver (Maia 2005; Bianco 2019). Bikman says he generally supports cold-water immersion.
- **TSH has two effects:** it switches on thyroid hormone production AND makes the thyroid grow. ✅
- **Thyroid hormone is the engine's idle speed (the "choke valve")** for every cell. High means each cell does what it does faster and more; low means slower. ✅

| System | Hyperthyroid (everything turned up) | Hypothyroid (everything slowed) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Weight / temperature** | Fast weight loss, can't stand heat, sweating, thirst | Weight gain, always cold |
| **Heart** | Fast pulse, irregular heartbeat, too much clotting | Too slow |
| **Nerves / muscles** | Spasms, tremors | Slow responses; delayed reflexes (the knee-jerk kicks out late) |
| **Gut** | Fast gut movement, diarrhea | Slow gut movement, constipation |
| **Eyes** | Bulging eyes (exophthalmos: the fat pads behind the eye swell; the eyelid lags behind when looking down). Bulging eyes are specific to Graves' disease, while eyelid lag can happen in any overactive thyroid | Puffy, swollen skin around the eyes (they look sunken) |

### Thyroid Disorders
- **Hashimoto's thyroiditis:** the most common thyroid problem in the US. The immune system attacks the thyroid (autoimmune). Early on it swings between overactive and underactive, trending toward a truly underactive thyroid. ✅ Bikman says most autoimmune diseases have a trigger from the environment, and the most common one here is **gluten**: people with gluten sensitivity or celiac disease are about **20 times more likely** to develop Hashimoto's. He admits this is likely to be controversial but says the research is solid. 🟡 Taken as his stated claim; no study is named in the lecture. 🔴 Evidence check: in a Swedish national study, people with celiac disease had about 4 times the risk of an underactive thyroid and 3.6 times the risk of thyroid inflammation (thyroiditis), not 20 times (Elfström 2008). That gluten is the most common trigger of Hashimoto's is not confirmed.
- **Graves' disease:** also autoimmune, but the antibodies **imitate TSH**. The gland is told both to over-produce hormone and to grow. Result: an overactive thyroid plus a goiter. ✅
- **A goiter (enlarged thyroid) does NOT tell you whether the thyroid is overactive or underactive.** ✅

| Cause of goiter | TSH | Thyroid hormone state |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Not enough iodine** (the most common cause worldwide; in countries with enough iodine, Hashimoto's and lumpy, nodular goiter are more common) | Very high (no negative feedback, because no hormone can be made) | Underactive: the gland grows in response to TSH but lacks iodine, the building block |
| **Graves' disease** (antibodies that imitate TSH) | Not the driver; the antibodies act in its place | Overactive |
| **A pituitary tumor that releases TSH** | Too high | Overactive |

## The Adrenal Glands
Picture an orange: the **cortex** (the thick peel) and the **medulla** (the flesh) are basically two separate glands wrapped together. The adrenal glands sit on top of the kidneys. The cortex has three zones, each making steroid hormones: ✅

| Layer (outer to inner) | Family | Memory trick |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Outer | Mineralocorticoids (salt-balancing hormones) | **Salt** |
| Middle | Glucocorticoids (cortisol, which raises blood sugar) | **Sugar** |
| Inner (zona reticularis) | Sex hormones | **Sex**: becomes the main source of sex hormones in women after menopause |

### Cushing Syndrome — Too Much Cortisol
Caused by a pituitary tumor that drives ACTH (the hormone that tells the adrenals to make cortisol) or by a tumor of the adrenal cortex itself. ✅ The most common cause overall is steroid medicine (glucocorticoids) (Nieman 2008). Five effects: ✅
- **Adrenaline-type hormones (catecholamines):** cortisol does not raise adrenaline or noradrenaline, but it **makes the body more sensitive** to them (the same adrenaline gives a bigger jump in heart rate).
- **Sex hormones:** it tells the ovaries and testes "too stressed to reproduce." Estrogens and androgens fall, ovulation can stop, and sperm production can suffer.
- **Fat:** selective. **Fat is released from the arms and legs and stored in the trunk, neck and face** (belly fat and a "buffalo hump" on the upper back).
- **Protein:** strips protein from muscle, bone and skin (collagen) to send amino acids to the liver to make glucose. The results are stretch marks, muscle loss and weak bones.
- **Glucose:** raises it at any cost: amino acids turned into glucose, stored liver sugar broken down, and **muscle and liver made insulin resistant**, ending in insulin resistance and type 2 diabetes.

### Other Adrenal Disorders
- **Sex-hormone tumors of the cortex:** too much estrogen in a man causes **feminization** (female traits); too much androgen in a woman causes **virilization** (male traits). Everyone makes both estrogens and androgens; only the balance differs. ✅
- **Pheochromocytoma:** a tumor of the adrenal **medulla** that releases adrenaline (and often noradrenaline; high blood pressure, often in spikes, is the hallmark; Lenders 2014). It feels like a constant panic attack: pounding heart, sweaty palms, and high blood sugar because adrenaline drives the liver to release glucose. ✅

## The Pancreas — Outside the Chain of Command
- The hormone-making part of the pancreas is the islets: **alpha cells make glucagon, beta cells make insulin.** ✅
- **A neighborly tug-of-war (paracrine):** glucagon prompts beta cells to release insulin; insulin tells alpha cells to stop releasing glucagon. **Insulin always wins.** ✅
- Insulin is released when blood sugar is high and lowers it; glucagon is released when blood sugar is low and raises it. ✅

## From Insulin Resistance to Type 2 Diabetes
- How common it is (Bikman's figures): about half of adults worldwide are overweight, **more than half have insulin resistance**, and in the US "upwards of near 80%" have some degree of it. 🟡 Given as his estimates; he says it is worse in Mexico, the Middle East, Asia and the Pacific Islands. 🟡 Evidence check: the WHO puts overweight (including obesity) at 45% of adults in 2024 (WHO 2026). In the US national health survey (NHANES), about 40–45% of adults aged 18–44 without diabetes had insulin resistance by a score called HOMA-IR (Parcha 2022). The 80% figure is not confirmed and depends on the cutoff used.
- **What it is:** some cells (not all) respond less to insulin, so **insulin in the blood rises (hyperinsulinemia)** to make up for it. Polite knocking becomes an angry mob. ✅
- **Insulin's most famous effect is not its most important one.** It opens the sugar doors in **muscle and fat** (other tissues do not need insulin to take in glucose). ✅
- **Missed because doctors focus on glucose:** insulin climbs for years while blood sugar stays normal. Type 2 diabetes is found 10 to 20 years later, when the body can no longer keep up. Measuring insulin would catch the problem while it can still be turned around. 🟢 Evidence check: in the Whitehall II study, insulin sensitivity fell steeply over the 5 years before diagnosis, and blood sugar rose sharply only in the last 3 (Tabák 2009). 🟡 "10 to 20 years" and the value of routine insulin testing are his views; fasting insulin is not a standard screening test in current guidelines.

| Cause of insulin resistance | How |
| --- | --- |
| **Stress** | Cortisol and adrenaline raise blood sugar, working against insulin, so insulin has to work harder |
| **Inflammation** | Illness, autoimmune disease, and breathed-in irritants such as cigarette smoke or diesel exhaust |
| **Too much insulin** | Constant refined starches and sugars; too much of something makes the body resistant to it. 🟡 Evidence check: experiments show high insulin can cause insulin resistance, but whether it is usually the cause or the result is debated (Shanik 2008) |

**The row of dominoes (Bikman's view: fat falls first):** the fat cell becomes resistant first, then three more dominoes decide how blood sugar is controlled. 🟡 The fat-first order is his idea; the standard model lists muscle, liver, fat, alpha cells and failing beta cells among eight contributors, with no fixed order (DeFronzo 2009).
- **Muscle** takes up about **80%** of the glucose from a starchy or sugary meal (🟢 this figure comes from lab studies where insulin is dripped in; after an ordinary meal, the liver and other tissues take a larger share; DeFronzo 2009). Resistant muscle does not open its sugar doors, so glucose stays in the blood.
- **Liver:** a resistant liver breaks down its stored sugar instead of storing more, "a disobedient child."
- **Alpha cells:** resistant alpha cells ignore insulin's stop signal and pump out glucagon, which tells the liver to release even more glucose.
- Once all three are involved, blood sugar climbs sharply: insulin resistance has grown into **type 2 diabetes**. There is no type 2 diabetes without insulin resistance first. 🟡 Evidence check: diabetes appears only when the beta cells can no longer keep up, so failing insulin production is the other essential half. In some lean patients it is the main problem (DeFronzo 2009).

## Key Corrections to Carry Forward
- 🔴 Thinking a goiter means an overactive thyroid: it can be underactive (lack of iodine) or overactive (Graves'). Growth of the gland and hormone output are two different TSH signals.
- 🔴 Thinking T4 is the working thyroid hormone: T4 is the inactive form, T3 is the active form, and the change happens in the tissues (brown fat especially, when switched on by cold). 🔴 In humans, mainly skeletal muscle and the liver (Maia 2005).
- 🔴 Thinking growth hormone only builds tissue: it also releases fat and stored liver sugar, and too much of it drives insulin resistance.
- 🔴 Thinking insulin resistance shows up in blood sugar: blood sugar stays normal for years while insulin rises.
- 🔴 Thinking the adrenal gland is one gland: the cortex (salt, sugar, sex) and the medulla (adrenaline) are basically separate.

## Practical Takeaway
When any hormone result looks odd, ask the negative-feedback question first: what signal would normally switch this off, and is it there? Lack of iodine, Graves' disease and Cushing syndrome all become predictable that way. For metabolic health, the lecture's practical message is to look at insulin, not only blood sugar.

## Questions to Learn From (Socratic) — with Answer Options
**1. A patient has an enlarged thyroid, very high TSH and signs of an underactive thyroid. What is the most likely cause, and why does the gland grow?**
A. Graves' disease, because antibodies that imitate TSH push the gland to over-produce hormone
B. \* Lack of iodine: without iodine no thyroid hormone can be made, so there is no negative feedback, TSH stays very high, and the gland still obeys TSH's growth signal even though it cannot make hormone
C. A cold-activated brown fat problem turning too much T4 into T3
*Why B: TSH has two separate effects (make hormone, grow the gland). Without iodine, only the growth signal can be obeyed.*

**2. Why does a high-cortisol state such as Cushing syndrome cause both muscle loss and fat gain around the middle?**
A. Cortisol lowers blood sugar, so the body burns muscle to make up for it
B. Cortisol only raises fat storage, and the muscle loss is unrelated
C. \* Cortisol raises blood sugar at any cost, stripping amino acids from muscle, bone and skin for the liver to make glucose, while selectively releasing fat from the limbs and storing it in the trunk, neck and face
*Why C: the lecture's central point about cortisol is raising blood sugar, with the selective moving of fat on top.*

**3. Why is insulin resistance usually caught late?**
A. \* Screening usually focuses on blood sugar, which stays normal while insulin climbs for years; type 2 diabetes is only recognized 10 to 20 years later, when the body can no longer keep up
B. Insulin resistance causes no change in any blood test until diabetes appears
C. Insulin levels fall early in insulin resistance, so they are hard to measure
*Why A: high insulin is half the definition. A "glucose-centric" approach misses it.*

**4. In the insulin-glucagon relationship, what changes when the alpha cell becomes insulin resistant?**
A. Beta cells stop making insulin, so glucagon is no longer needed
B. The alpha cell makes less glucagon because it hears insulin more strongly
C. \* Insulin's stop signal no longer reaches the alpha cell, so it pumps out glucagon, which tells the liver to break down stored sugar and release more glucose
*Why C: insulin normally wins by directly holding back the alpha cell. Losing that control is the third domino on the road to type 2 diabetes.*
