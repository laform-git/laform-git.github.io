
# Bikman — Fundamentals of Physiology, Ch.7: Adipose Physiology
*Frames fat as a regulated, hormone-secreting organ: how fat cells grow (hyperplasia vs. hypertrophy), why insulin is the signal that tells them to grow, and why fat-cell size, not total fat mass, drives metabolic risk.*

*Source: Dr. Benjamin Bikman, Peterson Academy course "Fundamentals of Physiology," Lecture 7 (transcript). The capstone of the course's organ-system tour, which Bikman deliberately gives its own chapter because physiology textbooks rarely do. Same lecturer as the "Why We Get Sick" and "Essentials of Endocrinology" series; this lecture is where his fat-cell research specialty shows up most directly.*

**In plain words:** body fat is not just stored energy; it is a living organ that sends out hormones. After the teenage years most people keep the same number of fat cells for life, so adults gain fat mainly by making each fat cell bigger. Bikman argues that fat cells only grow when two things are present, extra energy and a high insulin signal, and that the harm comes when individual fat cells get too big, not simply from how much fat a person carries. Several of these claims are debated, and the evidence checks below say where.

**Words in this note:**
- **ATP**: adenosine triphosphate, the cell's energy currency
- **LPL**: lipoprotein lipase, the enzyme that unloads fat from the blood into tissues
- **BMI**: body mass index, weight compared with height
- **TRT**: testosterone replacement therapy
- **ApoB**: apolipoprotein B; counts all the cholesterol particles that can clog arteries

## Why This Matters
This is the lecture that explains *why* fat grows, not just *that* it grows. Bikman's central claim is that fat gain needs two things, energy and an insulin signal, and that the metabolic damage of extra fat comes from how big each fat cell gets rather than how much fat the body carries in total. It ties together the whole "Why We Get Sick" series (insulin as a growth signal, inflammation as a cause of insulin resistance) and sets up the "Functions of Fat" chapter of the Endocrinology course.

## How Fat Grows — More Cells vs. Bigger Cells
- **Hyperplasia** (more fat cells): happens through infancy, childhood and the teenage years. ✅
- **At the end of the teenage years, adding fat cells generally stops.** For most people, the *number* of fat cells is then set for adult life. ✅
- **Fat cells are not immortal, but they live a long time:** up to roughly 10 years. When one dies it is replaced by another, so the count stays flat. ✅ About 10% of fat cells are renewed each year, and their number stays the same in adults, even after major weight loss (Spalding 2008).
- **Hypertrophy** (bigger fat cells): the way almost all adult fat gain happens. The number is fixed, but size keeps rising, so the *amount* of fat can still climb. ✅ 🟢 Nuance: in an 8-week overfeeding study of lean adults, lower-body fat grew by adding cells, while belly fat grew by making cells bigger (Tchoukalova 2010).
- **The exception:** some people have a genetic ability to keep making new fat cells, but Bikman puts it at only about 10% of people with obesity. 🟡 The 10% figure is not confirmed.

## Three Fat Stores, Two Colors

| Feature | Brown fat | Subcutaneous fat (white) | Visceral fat (white) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Where** | Small pockets woven through the chest area (near the rib cage and collarbones) | Under the skin: the fat you can pinch and jiggle | Inside the belly, around the organs: a swollen, hard belly rather than a pinchable one |
| **Share of body fat** | Least | Most | Large but varies; more in men than women |
| **Color** | Dark reddish-brown | Yellowish-white | Yellowish-white |
| **What the cell looks like** | Small fat droplets plus lots of mitochondria ("an engine that's just revving") | Almost entirely one big fat droplet | Almost entirely one big fat droplet |
| **Job** | Make heat | Store energy (a "miserly hoard") for fasting or times of little food | Store energy |
| **Effect on health** | More brown fat, or more active brown fat, goes with resistance to obesity and diabetes | The healthiest place to store fat | The more harmful place to store fat |

🟡 He says white fat cells are about **1,000 times larger** than brown fat cells, and that no other cell type can expand as much as a white fat cell. White fat cells are much larger and can expand enormously, but the 1,000-times figure is not confirmed.

## Brown Fat — Why Babies Have It
- Newborns are described as **the only land mammal born obese**: they are chubby but short on muscle. 🟢 Evidence check: human newborns are among the fattest mammals on record at birth, unlike most mammals including other primates, though they are not the only ones born with substantial fat (Kuzawa 1998).
- Adults warm up by shivering (muscle spasms make heat). A newborn does not have enough muscle, or enough nerve supply to the muscle, to shiver, so **brown fat is its main way of keeping warm.** ✅
- As muscle grows, brown fat shrinks back to the small patches in the chest that adults keep. ✅
- It still matters in adults: people with more brown fat, or more active brown fat, tend to stay leaner and healthier. 🟢 In a study of about 52,000 patients' scans, visible brown fat went with lower rates of type 2 diabetes and heart disease (Becher 2021). This is a link, not proof of cause.
- 🟢 **Bikman's own lab data (his claim, presented as his published work):** ketones push white fat to act a little more like brown fat, and in their human measurements the energy use of fat tissue rose about **three-fold** in ketosis or with raised ketones. In contrast, **insulin turns energy use down, even in brown fat cells.** Ketones turn the dial up; insulin turns it down. 🟡 Evidence check: in his lab's paper, the ketone BHB raised the oxygen use of mitochondria in human under-the-skin fat samples by about 128% (roughly 2.3-fold). This was measured in tissue outside the body, and ATP production did not rise to match (Walton 2020). In a small sub-study of a feeding trial, a high-carbohydrate diet lowered mitochondrial oxygen use in fat tissue (Bikman 2022). The "three-fold" figure and the claim about insulin in brown fat are not confirmed.

## Where Fat Goes vs. How Much — Two Different Signals
- **Insulin decides *how much* fat is stored.** ✅
- **Lipoprotein lipase (LPL) decides *where* it is stored.** LPL is an enzyme (the "-ase" ending means enzyme) that pulls fat out of the blood into tissue. Wherever more LPL is made, more fat is stored. ✅
- **Sex hormones steer where LPL is made:** at puberty, estrogens drive LPL in the typical female places (breasts, buttocks, hips). The much higher androgens (male-type hormones) in boys drive LPL in the middle, so a male gains fat around the belly. 🟡 Evidence check: in men, testosterone *reduced* fat uptake into belly fat and steered it to under-the-skin fat (Mårin 1996), and low testosterone goes with more belly fat, so the claim that androgens drive belly fat is disputed.
- Before puberty, boys and girls look basically the same in body shape. ✅
- **With no sex hormone signal** (in some diseases), he says the body falls back to a general female pattern, with more on the buttocks and hips, though not necessarily breast tissue in a man. 🟡 Not confirmed as stated.

## Fat as a Hormone Organ — Leptin
- Fat releases **more than 50 identified hormones.** ✅
- **Leptin** rises in step with the amount of fat: more body fat, more leptin. ✅
- **The discovery story:** a chance mutation in the leptin gene in a colony of mice produced hugely obese mice that were never full. Leptin was named for the Greek *leptos* (thin) because injecting it made them lean. ✅
- **The drug that failed:** a multi-million-dollar bet that leptin would cure obesity. It worked in people born with a faulty leptin gene (very rare; a near life-saving treatment for them) and, he says, did *nothing* in typical adults with obesity, who already have plenty of leptin. ✅ for leptin deficiency (Farooqi 1999). 🟢 Evidence check: in the main trial in typical adults with obesity, only the highest doses gave modest, varied weight loss (Heymsfield 1999), so "nothing" slightly overstates it.
- **Why:** too much leptin creates **leptin resistance**, the same logic as too much insulin creating insulin resistance. ✅
- **Leptin is more than a fullness signal:** it also triggers fat breakdown, increases glucose uptake by muscle, and **holds back the beta cells** so insulin stays down. Without leptin, insulin "goes through the roof." ✅

## Why Bigger Fat Cells Cause Metabolic Trouble
1. **The balloon problem.** A fat cell can only stretch so far before its membrane would break, and bursting is a messy death, not a tidy one. So at its maximum size the cell **becomes insulin resistant on purpose** to stop growing, protecting itself. 🔴 "On purpose" is his interpretation. 🟢 What is well supported: larger fat cells are more insulin resistant, and enlarged fat cells predicted type 2 diabetes regardless of obesity (Weyer 2000).
2. **The leak.** Once insulin resistant, the cell starts breaking down fat and **leaking free fatty acids** into the blood, even while insulin is high. ✅
3. **The suffocation problem.** Cells must sit within roughly 100–200 micrometers of a capillary, the distance oxygen can spread (Trayhurn 2013). Giant fat cells push each other away from the blood supply and become **starved of oxygen (hypoxic).** ✅
4. **The rescue that backfires.** Oxygen-starved fat cells release **inflammation signals (pro-inflammatory cytokines)** calling for new blood vessels to grow. This saves the cell but causes low-grade, body-wide **inflammation** (blood inflammation markers above a lean person's, with no obvious illness). ✅ (Trayhurn 2013)
5. **The knock-on effect.** Inflammation is a main cause of insulin resistance (previous lecture), so the rest of the body gets an extra push toward insulin resistance. ✅

**The proof from injection sites:** people with type 1 diabetes are told to rotate where they inject insulin. Repeated insulin in one spot produces lumps of fat that can look like tumors. He says biopsies show fat cells at a long-used injection site differing in size from cells a few centimeters away by **100 to 1,000 times**. 🟢 Fat lumps at repeated injection sites (lipohypertrophy) are well documented, but the 100- to 1,000-fold figure is not confirmed. *Insulin makes fat cells bigger, and substantially so.*

## The Personal Fat Threshold
- The real job of fat tissue is to store energy safely so it does not end up in muscle or the liver (fat in the wrong place, called **ectopic fat**). ✅
- The **personal fat threshold** is the point where a person's fat cells are too big to keep storing fat in a healthy way. Past it, more storage pushes them into insulin resistance. It depends on how many new fat cells a person can make. ✅ The idea comes from Taylor & Holman 2015.
- **The ethnicity study:** European white and South Asian men matched for age, habits and total amount of fat. The South Asian men had far more insulin resistance and the problems that follow (prediabetes, high blood pressure, fatty liver). Biopsies showed the white men had **more, smaller** fat cells and the South Asian men **fewer, larger** ones. So the white men had the higher threshold. 🟢 Evidence check: the closest published study (Anand 2011; men and women compared within body-mass-index bands) found larger fat cells in South Asians, which explained much of their higher insulin and liver fat. The South Asians also had more body fat at the same BMI, so they were not matched for amount of fat.
- **The threshold is set by the size of each fat cell, not by the total amount of fat.** ✅ Bikman presents this as the way to judge fat's effect on health.

## What Tells Fat to Grow — Two Signals
- **Signal 1: more energy coming in than going out** (calories in vs. out). Needed, but only half the answer. ✅
- **Signal 2: raised insulin.** Bikman's words: **"There is no fat growth unless insulin is elevated."** 🟡 Disputed: this is the carbohydrate-insulin model (Ludwig 2021), which the energy-balance model challenges (Hall 2022).
- **The petri-dish experiment (his lab):** fat cells sitting in a liquid full of glucose and fat stay small until insulin is added. Then they are visibly bigger at 24 hours, bigger again after that, and keep growing over days. *A cell does not know what to do with energy until a hormone tells it.* ✅
- **Insulin tells the cell to grow; calories make the growth possible.**

## Where Does the Energy Go When Insulin Is Low?
The physics objection (energy cannot just disappear), answered: without an insulin signal, **the body finds ways to waste the energy.**
- **Joslin and Benedict** (whom he calls the godfather of modern endocrinology and the godfather of metabolic-rate science): people with severe (type 1) diabetes burned energy at a rate **about 20% above** what their body size predicted. Giving insulin brought it back to the expected level. 🟡 Evidence check: Benedict and Joslin (1910–1912) reported energy use about 15% above normal in severe diabetes, linked to the blood becoming acidic. But insulin was not available until 1922, so the part about insulin reversing it is not from their work (Benedict & Joslin 1912). Later studies confirm that stopping insulin in type 1 diabetes raises resting energy use, largely through high glucagon (Charlton & Nair 1998). (Joslin is better described as a pioneer of diabetes care.)
- He says a similar slowing happens when insulin is raised in some people with type 2 diabetes, and in people without diabetes. 🟡 Not confirmed as stated; no study named.
- **Meals with the same calories:** the higher-carb meal gives a bigger insulin spike and a bigger drop in energy use. He says the low-carb group burned roughly **300 more calories per day.** 🟡 Evidence check: in the main trial, a low-carb diet raised energy use by about 209 kcal/day compared with high-carb (278 in those who finished; 308 only in the people who released the most insulin) (Ebbeling 2018). A study in a locked research ward found a much smaller rise, about 57–151 kcal/day, on a ketogenic diet, with no faster fat loss (Hall 2016). Disputed.
- **Wasted ketones:** with low insulin, lots of ketones are made and lost in the breath and urine. Each ketone carries calories, roughly like glucose, so that energy is thrown away. 🟡 True in principle, but in diet-induced ketosis this loss is probably small; no figure is given.
- **Diabulimia:** people with type 1 diabetes who deliberately take too little insulin to stay thin while eating freely, at deadly risk (blood sugar roughly 10 times too high, and dangerously acidic ketones). Bikman uses it as the "ultimate proof" that insulin directs where energy goes. ✅ for the danger: women with type 1 diabetes who cut back on insulin had about three times the risk of death over 11 years (Goebel-Fabbri 2008). 🔴 "Ultimate proof" is his interpretation.
- At low enough insulin, he says, a fat cell **must shrink and cannot grow**, whatever the calories.

## Liposuction — Why It Does Not Fix the Problem
- It removes only **subcutaneous** (under-the-skin) fat, the healthiest store, and leaves the belly (visceral) fat untouched. ✅
- It removes fat *amount* but does not change the **size** of the cells left behind. ✅
- If diet and insulin signals stay the same, the body regains fat elsewhere (his example: from the buttocks and hips to the belly). ✅
- **No health outcome improves.** Because fat cells are not replaced, the ones left carry a bigger load and grow bigger more easily, so, he says, risk can go *up.* ✅ for no improvement: removing about 10 kg of belly fat under the skin did not improve insulin sensitivity or heart-risk factors (Klein 2004), and fat came back within a year, moved to the belly (Hernandez 2011). 🟡 That risk goes *up* is not confirmed.
- Women are described as fatter than men on average yet healthier, because they store fat almost only under the skin (buttocks and hips). Men store more around the organs. ✅

## Punchlines & Quotable Facts (Lecture 7)
- **"There is no fat growth unless insulin is elevated."** 🟡 Disputed (Ludwig 2021; Hall 2022).
- **Humans are the only land mammal born obese.** A baby's fat is a heating system, not just a reserve.
- **White fat cells expand up to 1,000 times** the size of a brown fat cell. No other cell comes close.
- **Insulin controls how much fat; lipoprotein lipase controls where.**
- **Fat cells live up to ~10 years**, and for most people their number is set after the teenage years.
- **Low-carb vs. high-carb at the same calories: roughly 300 extra calories burned per day** (as Bikman reports it). 🟡 About 209 kcal/day in the main trial (Ebbeling 2018); smaller in a locked-ward study (Hall 2016).
- **Type 1 diabetes without insulin: energy use about 20% above predicted.** 🟡 Benedict and Joslin reported about 15% (Benedict & Joslin 1912).
- **Fat cells become insulin resistant to avoid bursting.** Here insulin resistance is a survival move.
- **"The problem is the size of the fat cells. It's not the total mass of fat."**

## Key Corrections to Carry Forward
- 🔴 Thinking adults keep making fat cells: after the teenage years the number is mostly fixed, and adult gain happens by cells getting bigger (apart from the ~10% genetic exception).
- 🔴 Thinking fat cells never die: they last roughly 10 years and are replaced one for one.
- 🔴 Thinking leptin is only a hunger-off switch, or that more leptin means less obesity: typical obesity involves leptin resistance, and leptin also acts on fat breakdown, muscle glucose uptake and beta cells.
- 🔴 Thinking fat gain is purely calories in vs. out: Bikman's position is that it needs both a surplus and raised insulin, and that low insulin raises energy use and wastes energy as ketones.
- 🔴 Thinking the total amount of fat is the risk: his claim is that cell size relative to a personal threshold is what matters, and the same amount of fat can be healthy or unhealthy.
- 🔴 Thinking liposuction is a metabolic fix: it removes the healthiest store and leaves health outcomes the same or worse.

## Practical Takeaway
In this lecture's framework, the lever on fat growth is the insulin signal, not calories alone. The goal is to keep fat cells small and under their personal threshold, rather than simply chasing a lower total amount of fat. Belly (visceral) fat and under-the-skin (subcutaneous) fat are different problems, and storing fat under the skin is the protective one. 🟡 The lecture gives the mechanisms and Bikman's lab findings; it does not give a plan, so how to act on this belongs with the "Why We Get Sick" solutions chapters and your doctor.

## Questions to Learn From (Socratic) — with Answer Options
**1. The number of fat cells in adults is mostly fixed after the teenage years. How does a typical adult keep gaining fat?**
A. By making new fat cells all the time, since fat cells die every few weeks
B. By turning brown fat into more white fat cells
C. \* By hypertrophy: the existing fat cells get bigger, so the amount of fat rises even though the number of fat cells stays about the same
*Why C: the lecture says adding fat cells generally stops at the end of the teenage years, fat cells live up to about 10 years and are replaced one for one, and adult gain is cells getting bigger (apart from the ~10% with a genetic ability to keep making fat cells).*

**2. In Bikman's petri-dish experiment, fat cells sit in a liquid full of glucose and fat, yet stay small until one thing is added. What does this show?**
A. \* Available energy alone does not grow a fat cell; a hormone signal (insulin) is needed to tell it to store that energy
B. Fat cells need more calories than other cells, so the liquid simply was not rich enough
C. Glucose is the true growth signal and fat plays no part
*Why A: the cells stayed thin until insulin was added, then grew over the next days. His conclusion: insulin tells the cell to grow, and calories make the growth possible.*

**3. Insulin is high and a fat cell has reached its maximum size. Why does the cell become insulin resistant, and what is the downside?**
A. Insulin damages the cell, and the downside is that it can no longer burn fat
B. \* The cell stops listening to insulin to avoid bursting, protecting itself, but it then leaks free fatty acids, and the inflammation signals from its oxygen-starved neighbors push the body further into insulin resistance
C. Insulin resistance in fat cells helps the body lose fat, so there is no downside
*Why B: Bikman frames it as the cell's survival move (the balloon picture), followed by the leak and the low-grade inflammation from suffocating cells.*

**4. Why does liposuction fail to improve health outcomes, and possibly even raise metabolic risk?**
A. It removes belly fat, which makes fat grow back faster
B. It lowers leptin, which makes the remaining fat cells larger
C. \* It removes under-the-skin fat (the healthiest store) without changing the size of the remaining cells, and since fat cells are not replaced, the ones left carry a bigger load and can grow more easily
*Why C: the lecture says liposuction only reaches under-the-skin fat, that the problem is cell size and not the total amount, and that the remaining cells grow bigger when they cannot share the load.*

## Open Threads
- **Insulin turning down energy use in brown fat:** Bikman says he will "revisit" this with other researchers' work; no further detail in this lecture. 🟡
- **Why white and South Asian people differ in making new fat cells:** the lecture reports the finding but not the reason. 🟡
