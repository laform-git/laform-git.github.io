
# Bikman — Fundamentals of Physiology, Ch.2: Nervous System Physiology
*Maps the peripheral nervous system (afferent in, efferent out), how pain signals are amplified and silenced, and how the sympathetic and parasympathetic branches use different ganglia, receptors, and transmitters to run every organ except skeletal muscle.*

*Source: Dr. Benjamin Bikman, Peterson Academy course "Fundamentals of Physiology," Lecture 2 (transcript). Builds directly on Lecture 1 (cell physiology, including the action potential and synapse). Same lecturer as the Endocrinology and Why We Get Sick series; this is the first systems-level lecture of the course.*

## Why This Matters
This lecture moves from the cell to the first organ system, and it supplies the wiring diagram that every later lecture in the course (skeletal muscle, cardiopulmonary, GI, endocrine, adipose) will plug into. Two ideas carry the most weight forward: (1) the autonomic nervous system runs everything except skeletal muscle through two opposing but cooperating branches, and (2) the same signal can be amplified or suppressed at the synapse, which is why pain can be turned up by prostaglandins and turned down by endorphins. Bikman also closes on fat tissue, the part most relevant to his metabolic focus: sympathetic signaling is what tells fat cells to break down stored fat.

## Scope-Setting Claim — The Peripheral Nervous System Is a Two-Way Loop
The lecture deliberately sets the brain (central nervous system) aside and looks at the **peripheral** nervous system: the nerves interacting with the rest of the body. The loop is simple. A stimulus is sensed by a sensory receptor, travels **up** to the brain on **afferent** neurons, the brain decides what to do, and the response travels **out** on **efferent** neurons to an **effector organ** (his example: muscles moving because one foot is uncomfortable). ✅ Memory hook: Afferent = Arrives at the brain; Efferent = Exits it.

## Punchlines & Quotable Facts (Lecture 2)
- **Afferent carries the signal to the brain; efferent carries it away** — stimulus up, response out.
- **Everything except skeletal muscle falls under the autonomic nervous system** — heart, lungs, kidneys, and digestion all run on it, while skeletal muscle alone is the voluntary (somatic) side.
- **Substance P, "P for pain"** — the neurotransmitter that carries the pain signal across the synapse; Bikman jokes it is "not too clever after all, but very obvious." 🟢 Evidence check: nociceptors release substance P together with glutamate, which carries most of the fast pain signal (Basbaum 2009).
- **Prostaglandins turn pain "from a 5 up to an 11"** — they make nociceptors more sensitive to the same amount of substance P. 🟢 Evidence check: prostaglandins (mainly PGE2) sensitize the nociceptor endings themselves, lowering their firing threshold to heat, pressure and inflammatory chemicals, rather than acting on substance P specifically (Basbaum 2009).
- **Both autonomic branches innervate the same organs and have opposite effects** — "two different instruments within the same band," not a tug-of-war.
- **Sympathetic ganglion sits next to the spine; parasympathetic ganglion sits next to the organ.**
- **Both branches use cholinergic receptors at the ganglion; only the parasympathetic is cholinergic all the way to the organ.** The sympathetic switches to adrenergic at the target.
- **Fat cells respond to norepinephrine through the beta-3 adrenergic receptor, triggering lipolysis.** 🟡 Evidence check: beta-3 dominates in rodent fat; in human white fat, lipolysis runs mainly through beta-1 and beta-2 receptors (Lafontan & Berlan 1993), and human brown-fat heat production is driven mainly by beta-2 (Blondin 2020), although the beta-3 drug mirabegron does activate human brown fat (Cypess 2015).

## Divisions of the Nervous System — The Map
**Afferent (sensory, up to brain)**
- **Somatosensory:** touch on skin or in the mouth, plus joint movement and muscle contraction (his example: with eyes closed you still know where your arms and legs are). ✅
- **Visceral:** what is happening deeper, in the gut, kidneys, and other internal organs, also signaling up to the brain. ✅

**Efferent (response, out from brain)**
- **Somatic:** the part we consciously control. Motor neurons from the brain drive skeletal muscle and move the skeleton (motor neurons are deferred to the skeletal muscle lecture). ✅
- **Autonomic:** "everything else," splitting into **sympathetic** and **parasympathetic**. ✅

| **Mechanoreceptor** | Stretch, bending, contraction, movement | Skin stretch, but also muscle stretch and contraction |

## The Pain Pathway — Turn It Up, Turn It Down
**The signal itself (a re-run of Lecture 1's synapse).** An electrical signal runs down the neuron, opens **voltage-gated calcium channels**, calcium enters, and vesicles loaded with **substance P** release across the synaptic cleft. The next neuron fires and the brain interprets it as pain. ✅ Bikman's point on purpose: the brain needs this signal to know something is harming the system so it can move or adjust.

**Amplifier: prostaglandins.** Tissue damage brings immune cells, especially **macrophages** ("the poster child of the immune system"), which release **prostaglandins**. These enhance the cell's sensitivity to substance P, so the same signal is felt much more strongly. ✅ **Aspirin and ibuprofen** work partly by reducing prostaglandin production (they block the COX enzymes; Vane 1971), giving a more modest response to the same substance P. He stresses "part of the mechanism," and that pain is sometimes needed to get the body to act. ✅

**Brake: endorphins.** The body has endogenous analgesics: **endorphins, enkephalins, and dynorphin**. They block the calcium channel, so substance P is not released across the cleft and the signal is dampened. ✅ They also open potassium channels on the receiving neuron, making it harder to fire (Corder 2018). Bikman suggests this may be why exercise or cold immersion, which raise endorphins, can reduce pain sensitivity in some people. 🟢 Offered as a possibility with "in some people," not a firm claim. 🟡 Evidence check: exercise-induced pain relief is well documented in pain-free people but varies in chronic pain, and its mechanism (endogenous opioids, endocannabinoids and others) is not settled (Rice 2019); evidence for cold immersion is thinner.

## Lateral Inhibition — The General Principle
Endorphins are an example of **lateral inhibition**: a neighboring neuron comes in from the side and disrupts the signal at the synaptic cleft between a presynaptic and a postsynaptic cell. ✅ 🟡 Evidence check: neuroscience calls this side-on brake **presynaptic inhibition**; "lateral inhibition" usually means neighboring neurons suppressing each other to sharpen a signal (Guyton & Hall). For pain, the blocker is the endorphin family. For essentially all non-pain neurons, the blocker is **GABA** (gamma-aminobutyric acid), which likewise blocks the calcium channel. ✅ 🟢 Evidence check: GABA works mostly by opening chloride channels (GABA-A receptors) that make the receiving neuron harder to fire; its GABA-B receptors on nerve endings are the ones that reduce calcium entry (Guyton & Hall). Takeaway: the nervous system has a built-in mechanism for turning any signal down, and GABA is among the most common molecules doing it.

## Autonomic Nervous System — Sympathetic vs. Parasympathetic
Both branches **innervate the same organs with opposite effects**: sympathetic is "fight or flight" (amp everything up), parasympathetic is "rest and digest" (calm down, digest after a meal). ✅ Neither is good or bad; both are always running and constantly seeking **homeostasis**. Bikman's framing: not a combative tug-of-war but "a concerted effort," two instruments in the same band.

**Anatomy: the autonomic ganglion.** Between the central nervous system and the organ is a relay station, the **autonomic ganglion**, where the **pre-ganglionic** neuron meets the **post-ganglionic** neuron. One presynaptic neuron can connect to multiple postsynaptic neurons here, so the signal can be amplified. ✅

| Feature | Sympathetic | Parasympathetic |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Nickname** | Fight or flight | Rest and digest |
| **Ganglion location** | Right next to the spinal column | Close to the target organ |
| **Pre-ganglionic fiber** | Short | Long, reaching nearly to the organ |
| **Post-ganglionic fiber** | Long, runs to the organ | Short |
| **Receptor at the ganglion** | Cholinergic | Cholinergic |
| **Receptor at the organ** | **Adrenergic** (switches) | **Cholinergic** (all the way through) |
| **Transmitter at the organ** | Norepinephrine | Acetylcholine |

✅ This table is the most reusable reference in the lecture. The cholinergic-then-adrenergic switch in the sympathetic chain is the one detail most easily forgotten. ✅ Two textbook exceptions: sympathetic nerves to sweat glands release acetylcholine, and the adrenal medulla acts as the post-ganglionic cell, releasing epinephrine into the blood (Guyton & Hall).

## The Two Receptor Families

| Feature | Cholinergic | Adrenergic |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Responds to** | Acetylcholine (the predominant neurotransmitter in the body, most receptors respond to it simply because it is so abundant). 🟡 Evidence check: ACh dominates the peripheral nervous system, but in the brain glutamate is the most abundant transmitter (Zhou & Danbolt 2014) | Norepinephrine (from nerve endings) and epinephrine (from the adrenal glands) |
| **Subtypes** | **Nicotinic**: always excitatory, opens sodium and potassium channels, also responds to nicotine. **Muscarinic**: named for muscarine from mushrooms, can be excitatory or inhibitory | **Alpha** (1, 2) and **beta** (1, 2, 3); each can be excitatory or inhibitory |
| **Built from** | Not discussed in this lecture | Catecholamines, built on the amino acid **tyrosine** |

- Epinephrine = adrenaline; norepinephrine = noradrenaline. ✅
- Norepinephrine acts as a neurotransmitter at a synapse; epinephrine from the adrenal glands floods the system as a hormone and activates some of the same receptors, a bridge to the endocrine lecture. ✅
- 🟢 Bikman keeps the alpha and beta subclasses "superficial" on purpose; only the beta-3 receptor in fat is named for a specific job here.

## Organ-by-Organ — Table

| Organ | Sympathetic (norepinephrine) | Parasympathetic (acetylcholine) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Heart** | Faster and harder contraction | Slower rate, softer contraction |
| **Blood vessels** | Vasoconstriction: narrowing, faster blood movement, higher blood pressure | Vasodilation: blood flow slows, blood pressure comes down |
| **GI tract** | Slows peristalsis (no time to digest or take a bathroom break while running) | Increases GI movement ("we have time, we're safe enough") |
| **Airways** | Bronchodilation: opens the airway, easier oxygenation and CO₂ clearance | Bronchoconstriction: air does not need to move as fast |
| **Fat tissue** | Beta-3 receptor activation: **lipolysis** | 🟡 Not covered in this lecture |

✅ Bikman flags the vessel-versus-airway contrast: under sympathetic drive vessels constrict while airways dilate, and the parasympathetic reverses both. He explains vasoconstriction in the sympathetic case as a way to move energy, oxygen, and waste clearance faster, "it doesn't occlude it, it narrows it." 🟡 The lecture presents this as the general pattern and does not discuss exceptions. 🟡 Evidence check: most blood vessels have no parasympathetic nerve supply (exceptions include the genitals and salivary glands), so dilation at rest mostly reflects *less* sympathetic tone; and sympathetic constriction mainly diverts blood away from skin and gut, while working muscle dilates its own vessels through local metabolites (Joyner & Casey 2015; Guyton & Hall).

## Fat Tissue — The Metabolic Payoff
Adipose tissue is "uniquely responsive" to the sympathetic nervous system. Norepinephrine released from the post-ganglionic fiber binds the **beta-3 adrenergic receptor** on fat cells and activates **lipolysis**, the breakdown of stored fat: triglycerides are broken down and released as **free fatty acids** (plus glycerol). ✅ 🟡 In humans the main lipolytic receptors are beta-1 and beta-2 rather than beta-3 (see the Punchlines check; Lafontan & Berlan 1993). Anything that switches on the sympathetic nervous system, Bikman names **exercise and cold therapy**, drives more of this process. 🟢 His stated link between nervous system physiology and "things as precise as fat-burning."

## Key Corrections to Carry Forward
- 🔴 Assuming the sympathetic and parasympathetic branches are opposing teams in a tug-of-war: they are complementary and always both active, jointly maintaining homeostasis.
- 🔴 Assuming both autonomic branches use the same chemistry throughout: both use cholinergic receptors at the ganglion, but at the organ the sympathetic switches to adrenergic (norepinephrine) while the parasympathetic stays cholinergic (acetylcholine).
- 🔴 Assuming the sympathetic ganglion and parasympathetic ganglion sit in the same place: sympathetic ganglia are beside the spine (short pre-, long post-ganglionic fiber), parasympathetic ganglia are near the organ (long pre-, short post-ganglionic fiber).
- 🔴 Assuming pain is a fixed, one-way signal: substance P carries it, prostaglandins amplify it, and endorphins (for pain) and GABA (for other signals) block it at the calcium channel.
- 🔴 Assuming vasoconstriction is inherently a bad sign: in fight or flight it is the way to move blood faster and clear waste from working muscle. 🟡 More precisely, it redirects blood toward working muscle, whose own vessels dilate (Joyner & Casey 2015).

## Practical Takeaway
The wiring diagram to keep: sensory receptor → afferent neuron → brain → efferent neuron → effector. On the output side, the somatic branch is voluntary skeletal muscle and the autonomic branch is everything else, with sympathetic and parasympathetic pulling each organ in opposite directions through norepinephrine and acetylcholine. For fat, the sympathetic signal via beta-3 receptors is the one that mobilizes stored triglyceride as free fatty acids, and Bikman names exercise and cold therapy as things that turn it on.

## Questions to Learn From (Socratic) — with Answer Options
**1. A runner is sprinting from danger. Which pattern of autonomic effects does Bikman's framework predict?**
A. Slower heart rate, vasodilation, bronchoconstriction, and faster peristalsis
B. \* Faster and harder heartbeat, vasoconstriction, bronchodilation, and slowed peristalsis
C. Faster heart rate and vasodilation, with unchanged airways and gut
*Why B: sympathetic activation (norepinephrine) amps the heart, narrows vessels to move blood faster, opens the airways, and pauses digestion. Option A describes the parasympathetic pattern.*

**2. A signal travels from the spinal cord to the heart through a parasympathetic pathway. Which description fits?**
A. Short pre-ganglionic fiber, ganglion beside the spine, adrenergic receptor at the heart
B. Long post-ganglionic fiber, ganglion beside the spine, cholinergic receptors only at the ganglion
C. \* Long pre-ganglionic fiber, ganglion near the heart, short post-ganglionic fiber, cholinergic receptors at both the ganglion and the heart
*Why C: the parasympathetic ganglion sits near the target organ, and the chain is cholinergic all the way through (acetylcholine). Option A describes the sympathetic anatomy.*

**3. Someone takes ibuprofen after an injury and feels the same injury as less painful. Based on this lecture, what is one part of the mechanism?**
A. \* Reduced prostaglandin production, so nociceptors are less sensitized to substance P
B. Direct blockade of voltage-gated calcium channels by endorphins
C. Increased GABA release at every synapse in the body
*Why A: prostaglandins from macrophages at damaged tissue amplify the response to substance P, and aspirin and ibuprofen act partly by lowering them. Option B is the endogenous endorphin brake, a separate mechanism.*

**4. Why is it mechanistically sensible that exercise or cold exposure might increase fat breakdown?**
A. They activate the parasympathetic nervous system, which stimulates acetylcholine release in fat
B. They block GABA, removing lateral inhibition on fat cells
C. \* They turn on the sympathetic nervous system, releasing norepinephrine that binds beta-3 adrenergic receptors on fat cells and triggers lipolysis
*Why C: Bikman states that anything turning on the sympathetic nervous system, such as exercise or cold therapy, activates lipolysis, releasing stored triglyceride as free fatty acids.*
