
# Bikman — Fundamentals of Physiology, Ch.2: Nervous System Physiology
*Maps the peripheral nervous system (afferent in, efferent out), how pain signals are amplified and silenced, and how the sympathetic and parasympathetic branches use different ganglia, receptors, and transmitters to run every organ except skeletal muscle.*

*Source: Dr. Benjamin Bikman, Peterson Academy course "Fundamentals of Physiology," Lecture 2 (transcript). Builds directly on Lecture 1 (cell physiology, including the action potential and synapse). Same lecturer as the Endocrinology and Why We Get Sick series; this is the first systems-level lecture of the course.*

**In plain words:** the nerves outside the brain carry messages in two directions: from the body up to the brain (what you feel) and from the brain out to the body (what you do). An automatic part of this system runs the heart, lungs, gut and fat without you thinking about it. It has two branches: one that revs the body up ("fight or flight") and one that calms it down ("rest and digest"). This lecture also explains how pain is turned up and turned down, and how stress signals tell fat cells to release stored fat.

**Words in this note:**
- **GABA**: the brain's main calming chemical messenger
- **GABA-A**: the receptor for GABA that sleeping pills and alcohol act on
- **DHEA**: a hormone made by the adrenal glands that the body turns into sex hormones
- **TRT**: testosterone replacement therapy
- **ApoB**: apolipoprotein B; counts all the cholesterol particles that can clog arteries

## Why This Matters
This lecture moves from the single cell to the first organ system. It gives the wiring diagram that every later lecture in the course (muscle, heart and lungs, gut, hormones, fat) plugs into. Two ideas matter most going forward. First, the automatic (autonomic) nervous system runs everything except the muscles that move the skeleton, through two opposite but cooperating branches. Second, the same signal can be turned up or turned down at the junction between nerves (the synapse). That is why pain can be made worse by prostaglandins and eased by endorphins. Bikman also ends on fat tissue, the part closest to his metabolic focus: the "fight or flight" (sympathetic) signal is what tells fat cells to break down stored fat.

## The Big Idea — The Peripheral Nervous System Is a Two-Way Loop
The lecture sets the brain and spinal cord (the central nervous system) aside and looks at the **peripheral** nervous system: the nerves that connect with the rest of the body. The loop is simple. Something is sensed by a sensory receptor. The message travels **up** to the brain on **afferent** nerves. The brain decides what to do. The response travels **out** on **efferent** nerves to an **effector organ**, the part that acts (his example: you shift your muscles because one foot is uncomfortable). ✅ Memory trick: Afferent = Arrives at the brain; Efferent = Exits it.

## Punchlines & Quotable Facts (Lecture 2)
- **Afferent nerves carry the message to the brain; efferent nerves carry it away.** Sensation up, response out.
- **Everything except skeletal muscle falls under the autonomic (automatic) nervous system.** The heart, lungs, kidneys and digestion all run on it. Skeletal muscle alone is the voluntary (somatic) side.
- **Substance P, "P for pain":** the nerve chemical (neurotransmitter) that carries the pain signal across the gap between nerves. Bikman jokes it is "not too clever after all, but very obvious." 🟢 Evidence check: pain-sensing nerves (nociceptors) release substance P together with glutamate, which carries most of the fast pain signal (Basbaum 2009).
- **Prostaglandins turn pain "from a 5 up to an 11."** They make pain nerves more sensitive to the same amount of substance P. 🟢 Evidence check: prostaglandins (mainly one called PGE2) make the pain-nerve endings themselves more sensitive, lowering the level at which they fire in response to heat, pressure and inflammation chemicals, rather than acting on substance P specifically (Basbaum 2009).
- **Both automatic branches reach the same organs and have opposite effects:** "two different instruments within the same band," not a tug-of-war.
- **The relay station (ganglion) of the fight-or-flight branch sits next to the spine; the relay station of the rest-and-digest branch sits next to the organ.**
- **Both branches use cholinergic receptors (ones that respond to acetylcholine) at the relay station; only the rest-and-digest branch is cholinergic all the way to the organ.** The fight-or-flight branch switches to adrenergic receptors (ones that respond to adrenaline-type chemicals) at the target.
- **Fat cells respond to noradrenaline (norepinephrine) through the beta-3 adrenergic receptor, which triggers fat release (lipolysis).** 🟡 Evidence check: beta-3 dominates in rodent fat. In human white fat, fat release runs mainly through beta-1 and beta-2 receptors (Lafontan & Berlan 1993), and heat production in human brown fat is driven mainly by beta-2 (Blondin 2020), although the beta-3 drug mirabegron does switch on human brown fat (Cypess 2015).

## Divisions of the Nervous System — The Map
**Afferent (sensory, up to the brain)**
- **Somatosensory:** touch on the skin or in the mouth, plus joint movement and muscle contraction (his example: with your eyes closed you still know where your arms and legs are). ✅
- **Visceral:** what is happening deeper inside, in the gut, kidneys and other internal organs, also signaling up to the brain. ✅

**Efferent (response, out from the brain)**
- **Somatic:** the part we control on purpose. Motor nerves from the brain drive skeletal muscle and move the skeleton (motor nerves are saved for the skeletal muscle lecture). ✅
- **Autonomic (automatic):** "everything else." It splits into the **sympathetic** (fight or flight) and **parasympathetic** (rest and digest) branches. ✅

## Types of Sensory Receptor — Table

| Receptor | What it senses | Lecture note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Photoreceptor** | Light | Found in the eye |
| **Mechanoreceptor** | Stretch, bending, contraction, movement | Skin stretch, but also muscle stretch and contraction |
| **Thermoreceptor** | Hot and cold | Especially in the skin |
| **Chemoreceptor** | Chemical changes in the blood | The body "sampling the blood"; saved for the heart and lungs lecture |
| **Nociceptor** | Pain | Detects things that damage tissue and releases the nerve chemical substance P; covered in detail below |

## The Pain Pathway — Turning It Up, Turning It Down
**The signal itself (a replay of Lecture 1's synapse).** An electrical signal runs down the nerve and opens **calcium channels that respond to voltage**. Calcium enters, and tiny bubbles (vesicles) loaded with **substance P** release it across the gap between nerves (the synaptic cleft). The next nerve fires, and the brain reads it as pain. ✅ Bikman's point about its purpose: the brain needs this signal to know something is harming the body, so it can move or adjust.

**The amplifier: prostaglandins.** Damaged tissue draws in immune cells, especially **macrophages** ("the poster child of the immune system"), which release **prostaglandins**. These make the cell more sensitive to substance P, so the same signal is felt much more strongly. ✅ **Aspirin and ibuprofen** work partly by lowering prostaglandin production (they block enzymes called COX; Vane 1971), giving a milder response to the same substance P. He stresses that this is "part of the mechanism," and that pain is sometimes needed to make the body act. ✅

**The brake: endorphins.** The body makes its own painkillers: **endorphins, enkephalins and dynorphin**. They block the calcium channel, so substance P is not released across the gap and the signal is muffled. ✅ They also open potassium channels on the receiving nerve, making it harder to fire (Corder 2018). Bikman suggests this may be why exercise or cold-water immersion, which raise endorphins, can lower pain sensitivity in some people. 🟢 Offered as a possibility, "in some people," not a firm claim. 🟡 Evidence check: pain relief after exercise is well documented in people without pain but varies in people with long-term pain, and how it works (the body's own opioids, its own cannabis-like chemicals, and others) is not settled (Rice 2019). The evidence for cold immersion is thinner.

## Lateral Inhibition — The General Principle
Endorphins are an example of what Bikman calls **lateral inhibition**: a neighboring nerve comes in from the side and interrupts the signal at the gap between the sending and receiving cells. ✅ 🟡 Evidence check: neuroscience calls this side-on brake **presynaptic inhibition**. "Lateral inhibition" usually means neighboring nerves quieting each other to sharpen a signal (Guyton & Hall). For pain, the blocker is the endorphin family. For basically all other nerves, the blocker is **GABA** (gamma-aminobutyric acid), which, he says, likewise blocks the calcium channel. ✅ 🟢 Evidence check: GABA works mostly by opening chloride channels (called GABA-A receptors) that make the receiving nerve harder to fire. Its GABA-B receptors on nerve endings are the ones that reduce calcium entry (Guyton & Hall). Takeaway: the nervous system has a built-in way to turn any signal down, and GABA is one of the most common molecules doing it.

## The Automatic Nervous System — Fight or Flight vs. Rest and Digest
Both branches **reach the same organs and have opposite effects**. The sympathetic branch is "fight or flight" (turn everything up). The parasympathetic branch is "rest and digest" (calm down, digest after a meal). ✅ Neither is good or bad. Both are always running and always working toward **homeostasis**, the body's steady normal. Bikman's view: not a combative tug-of-war but "a concerted effort," two instruments in the same band.

**The anatomy: the relay station (autonomic ganglion).** Between the spinal cord and the organ there is a relay station, the **autonomic ganglion**, where the nerve before the relay (**pre-ganglionic**) meets the nerve after it (**post-ganglionic**). One incoming nerve can connect to several outgoing nerves here, so the signal can be amplified. ✅

| Feature | Sympathetic | Parasympathetic |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Nickname** | Fight or flight | Rest and digest |
| **Where the relay station is** | Right next to the spine | Close to the target organ |
| **Nerve before the relay** | Short | Long, reaching nearly to the organ |
| **Nerve after the relay** | Long, runs to the organ | Short |
| **Receptor at the relay station** | Cholinergic | Cholinergic |
| **Receptor at the organ** | **Adrenergic** (switches) | **Cholinergic** (all the way through) |
| **Chemical messenger at the organ** | Noradrenaline (norepinephrine) | Acetylcholine |

✅ This table is the most useful reference in the lecture. The switch from cholinergic to adrenergic in the fight-or-flight chain is the detail most easily forgotten. ✅ Two textbook exceptions: fight-or-flight nerves to the sweat glands release acetylcholine, and the inner part of the adrenal gland (the adrenal medulla) acts as the after-relay cell, releasing adrenaline (epinephrine) into the blood (Guyton & Hall).

## The Two Receptor Families

| Feature | Cholinergic | Adrenergic |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Responds to** | Acetylcholine (the main nerve chemical in the body; most receptors respond to it simply because there is so much of it). 🟡 Evidence check: acetylcholine dominates the nerves outside the brain, but in the brain glutamate is the most plentiful messenger (Zhou & Danbolt 2014) | Noradrenaline (norepinephrine, from nerve endings) and adrenaline (epinephrine, from the adrenal glands) |
| **Subtypes** | **Nicotinic:** always switches the cell on, opens sodium and potassium channels, and also responds to nicotine. **Muscarinic:** named for muscarine, a chemical from mushrooms; can switch cells on or off | **Alpha** (1, 2) and **beta** (1, 2, 3); each can switch cells on or off |
| **Built from** | Not discussed in this lecture | Catecholamines, built from the amino acid **tyrosine** |

- Epinephrine = adrenaline; norepinephrine = noradrenaline. ✅
- Noradrenaline works as a nerve chemical at a synapse. Adrenaline from the adrenal glands floods the whole body as a hormone and switches on some of the same receptors, a bridge to the hormone lecture. ✅
- 🟢 Bikman keeps the alpha and beta subtypes "superficial" on purpose. Only the beta-3 receptor in fat is named for a specific job here.

## Organ by Organ — Table

| Organ | Fight or flight (noradrenaline) | Rest and digest (acetylcholine) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Heart** | Beats faster and harder | Slower rate, softer beat |
| **Blood vessels** | Vasoconstriction: vessels narrow, blood moves faster, blood pressure rises | Vasodilation: vessels widen, blood flow slows, blood pressure comes down |
| **Gut** | Slows the squeezing waves that move food along (peristalsis); no time to digest or stop for the bathroom while running | More gut movement ("we have time, we're safe enough") |
| **Airways** | Bronchodilation: airways open, easier to take in oxygen and clear carbon dioxide | Bronchoconstriction: airways narrow, since air does not need to move as fast |
| **Fat tissue** | Beta-3 receptor switched on: **fat release (lipolysis)** | 🟡 Not covered in this lecture |

✅ Bikman points out the contrast between vessels and airways: under fight or flight, vessels narrow while airways widen, and rest and digest reverses both. He explains the narrowing of vessels in fight or flight as a way to move energy and oxygen in and waste out faster: "it doesn't occlude it, it narrows it." 🟡 The lecture presents this as the general pattern and does not discuss exceptions. 🟡 Evidence check: most blood vessels have no rest-and-digest nerve supply (exceptions include the genitals and salivary glands), so widening at rest mostly reflects *less* fight-or-flight tone. And fight-or-flight narrowing mainly steers blood away from the skin and gut, while working muscle widens its own vessels through local chemicals (Joyner & Casey 2015; Guyton & Hall).

## Fat Tissue — The Metabolic Payoff
Fat tissue (adipose tissue) is "uniquely responsive" to the fight-or-flight system. Noradrenaline released from the after-relay nerve attaches to the **beta-3 adrenergic receptor** on fat cells and switches on **lipolysis**, the breakdown of stored fat. Triglycerides (stored fat) are broken down and released as **free fatty acids** (plus glycerol). ✅ 🟡 In humans the main fat-releasing receptors are beta-1 and beta-2 rather than beta-3 (see the Punchlines check; Lafontan & Berlan 1993). Anything that switches on the fight-or-flight system drives more of this process; Bikman names **exercise and cold therapy**. 🟢 This is his stated link between how the nervous system works and "things as precise as fat-burning."

## Key Corrections to Carry Forward
- 🔴 Thinking the fight-or-flight and rest-and-digest branches are opposing teams in a tug-of-war: they work together, are always both active, and jointly keep the body steady.
- 🔴 Thinking both branches use the same chemistry throughout: both use cholinergic receptors at the relay station, but at the organ the fight-or-flight branch switches to adrenergic (noradrenaline) while rest-and-digest stays cholinergic (acetylcholine).
- 🔴 Thinking the two branches' relay stations are in the same place: fight-or-flight relay stations sit beside the spine (short nerve before, long nerve after); rest-and-digest relay stations sit near the organ (long nerve before, short nerve after).
- 🔴 Thinking pain is a fixed, one-way signal: substance P carries it, prostaglandins amplify it, and endorphins (for pain) and GABA (for other signals) block it at the calcium channel.
- 🔴 Thinking narrowed blood vessels are always a bad sign: in fight or flight it is a way to move blood faster and clear waste from working muscle. 🟡 More exactly, it steers blood toward working muscle, whose own vessels widen (Joyner & Casey 2015).

## Practical Takeaway
The wiring diagram to keep: sensory receptor → afferent nerve → brain → efferent nerve → effector. On the output side, the somatic branch is voluntary skeletal muscle, and the automatic branch is everything else, with fight-or-flight and rest-and-digest pulling each organ in opposite directions through noradrenaline and acetylcholine. For fat, the fight-or-flight signal through beta-3 receptors is the one that releases stored fat as free fatty acids, and Bikman names exercise and cold therapy as things that switch it on.

## Questions to Learn From (Socratic) — with Answer Options
**1. A runner is sprinting away from danger. Which pattern does Bikman's framework predict?**
A. Slower heart rate, wider vessels, narrower airways and faster gut movement
B. \* Faster and harder heartbeat, narrower vessels, wider airways and slower gut movement
C. Faster heart rate and wider vessels, with unchanged airways and gut
*Why B: the fight-or-flight branch (noradrenaline) revs up the heart, narrows vessels to move blood faster, opens the airways and pauses digestion. Option A describes the rest-and-digest pattern.*

**2. A signal travels from the spinal cord to the heart through a rest-and-digest pathway. Which description fits?**
A. Short nerve before the relay, relay station beside the spine, adrenergic receptor at the heart
B. Long nerve after the relay, relay station beside the spine, cholinergic receptors only at the relay station
C. \* Long nerve before the relay, relay station near the heart, short nerve after the relay, cholinergic receptors at both the relay station and the heart
*Why C: the rest-and-digest relay station sits near the target organ, and the chain is cholinergic all the way through (acetylcholine). Option A describes the fight-or-flight anatomy.*

**3. Someone takes ibuprofen after an injury and feels the same injury as less painful. Based on this lecture, what is one part of how it works?**
A. \* Fewer prostaglandins are made, so pain nerves are less sensitized to substance P
B. Endorphins directly block the voltage-gated calcium channels
C. More GABA is released at every synapse in the body
*Why A: prostaglandins from macrophages at the damaged tissue amplify the response to substance P, and aspirin and ibuprofen work partly by lowering them. Option B is the body's own endorphin brake, a separate mechanism.*

**4. Why does it make sense that exercise or cold exposure might increase fat breakdown?**
A. They switch on the rest-and-digest system, which releases acetylcholine in fat
B. They block GABA, removing the brake on fat cells
C. \* They switch on the fight-or-flight system, releasing noradrenaline that attaches to beta-3 adrenergic receptors on fat cells and triggers fat release
*Why C: Bikman says anything that switches on the fight-or-flight system, such as exercise or cold therapy, triggers fat release, freeing stored triglyceride as free fatty acids.*
