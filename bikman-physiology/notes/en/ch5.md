
# Bikman — Fundamentals of Physiology, Ch.5: Gastrointestinal Physiology
*A mouth-to-anus tour of the GI tract (plus liver and pancreas) through four recurring themes — motility, secretion, digestion, absorption — ending with how gut hormones (GLP-1) explain gastric bypass and weight-loss drugs.*

*Source: Dr. Benjamin Bikman, Peterson Academy course "Fundamentals of Physiology," Lecture 5 (transcript). Follows Cardiopulmonary Physiology (Ch.4); the pancreas and glucagon are explicitly handed off to the Endocrine lecture (Ch.6).*

## Why This Matters
This is the "how does food actually become fuel" lecture. Every macronutrient has a different route into the body — carbohydrates and protein go straight to the blood, long-chain fat detours through the lymph, and short- and medium-chain fats go straight to the liver. That routing is the mechanistic backbone for later topics: insulin and glucagon (Ch.6), adipose tissue (Ch.7), ketones, and the gut-hormone drugs now in the news. It also explains why fat, bile and protein digestion are linked, which cuts against a lot of low-fat folk wisdom.

## The Framework — Four Themes at Every Stop
Bikman walks the tube top to bottom and asks the same four questions at each segment: **motility** (movement), **secretion** (what the wall releases), **digestion** (breaking big molecules into small ones), **absorption** (moving small molecules into the body). ✅ Digestion and absorption are different events — you can cut a molecule up without moving anything into the blood.

## Punchlines & Quotable Facts (Lecture 5)
- **"Most digestion... is happening in the small intestine, not because of the small intestine itself, but because of what's connected to it."** — the intestinal wall releases no digestive enzymes of its own (only enterokinase); the pancreas and liver do the work.
- **Human stomach pH is about 2** — Bikman places it near a wolf's, just short of scavengers, and reads that as a clue about what humans are built to eat. 🟡 Interpretive aside, not a measured result.
- **"You will not release bile salts unless you eat fat."** — and bile also enhances protease activity, so protein plus fat is digested better than protein alone.
- **Everything absorbed from the intestine goes to the liver first** — the hepatic portal system makes the liver "the detoxifier of the bloodstream."
- **Fat is the one macronutrient that does not enter the blood first** — long-chain fat rides a chylomicron ("a big bus") into the lymph.
- **Short- and medium-chain fats "must be burned, they cannot be stored"** — the reason coconut oil or vinegar can push the liver toward ketones.
- **Incretins were discovered in gastric-bypass patients** — their diabetes improved almost immediately, even though they remained very overweight.

## The Journey — Segment by Segment

| Segment | Motility | Secretion | Digestion | Absorption |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Mouth** | Chewing (mechanical breakdown) | Saliva, salivary amylase | Starch snipped into smaller pieces | None |
| **Esophagus** | Peristalsis; upper third skeletal (voluntary), lower two-thirds smooth (involuntary) | — | — | — |
| **Stomach** | Stores food ~3–5 hours; churns food into chyme; expands ~4–5x | HCl and intrinsic factor (parietal cells); pepsinogen (chief cells); mucus | Pepsin starts protein digestion; salivary amylase keeps working | None |
| **Small intestine** | Peristalsis | Enterokinase only (plus bicarbonate, enzymes and bile from accessory organs) | Main site of digestion | Main site of absorption |
| **Large intestine** | Slow mass movement (peristalsis) | None | None | Water only, to dry the stool |
| **Rectum / anus** | Internal sphincter (smooth, autonomic) plus external sphincter (skeletal, voluntary) | — | — | — |

✅ The sphincter logic: upper esophageal sphincter and external anal sphincter are skeletal muscle (you control them); lower esophageal and internal anal sphincters are smooth muscle (autonomic). Sympathetic activation closes the internal anal sphincter; parasympathetic opens it. The lower esophageal sphincter passes through the diaphragm.

## The Stomach — Acid, Pepsin and B12
- **Parietal cells** pump out hydrochloric acid using active transport (H+ against its gradient, costing ATP — the "pushing the stone uphill" idea from the cell-physiology lecture). ✅
- **Mucus** protects the stomach's own lining from the acid. ✅
- **Three jobs of the acid:** kill pathogens, denature (unfold) proteins so they can be cut, and convert pepsinogen into active pepsin. ✅
- **Chief cells** secrete inactive **pepsinogen**; acid snips off a piece to make **pepsin**, a protease that makes the first cuts in protein, producing peptides. ✅
- **Intrinsic factor** (also from parietal cells) hitches onto vitamin B12 and escorts it into the blood. B12 comes only from animal foods; without intrinsic factor a person can eat it and still not absorb it. ✅
- **Chyme** is the term for the churned, mushy food-and-gastric-juice mix. ✅

## The Small Intestine — Where the Work Happens
- **Three sections:** duodenum (first, most important), jejunum, ileum. ✅
- **Surface area:** the wall has villi, and the villi carry microvilli ("fingers on fingers," the brush border), massively increasing absorptive area. Each villus holds **capillaries** (to blood) and a **lacteal** (the smallest unit of the lymph system, built for fat). ✅
- **Roux-en-Y gastric bypass as a teaching case:** the stomach is stapled into a small pouch and the duodenum is bypassed. Result: far less eaten, and what is eaten is poorly digested and absorbed (diarrhea and GI distress), plus loss of intrinsic factor, so **lifelong B12 injections**. ✅

## The Accessory Organs
### Pancreas
- **Exocrine pancreas** (digestive enzymes, via a duct into the top of the duodenum) vs. **endocrine pancreas** (hormones into blood — deferred to Ch.6). ✅
- **Bicarbonate:** the small intestine has only a thin mucus layer (needed for absorption), so the pancreas floods in bicarbonate right at the duodenum to neutralize stomach acid. ✅
- **Enzymes arrive inactive** and are switched on in the intestine, which protects the pancreas itself from digesting its own tissue. 🟡 The protective rationale is my inference; the lecture only states they are released inactive.

| Enzyme family | Substrate | What it does | End product |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Proteases** (trypsinogen to trypsin; procarboxypeptidase to carboxypeptidase; activated by enterokinase) | Protein | Trypsin is an endopeptidase (cuts from the middle); carboxypeptidase is an exopeptidase (cuts from the ends) | Amino acids, dipeptides, tripeptides (never longer) |
| **Amylase** | Carbohydrate | Finishes what salivary amylase started | Mostly glucose (some fructose) |
| **Lipase** | Triglyceride | Removes two fatty acids from the glycerol backbone | Monoglyceride plus free fatty acids |

### Liver and the Biliary System
- **Multi-tasker:** clears old red blood cells, makes most blood proteins (albumin is the majority protein), secretes hormones, clears waste and toxins, and makes bile. ✅
- **Hepatic portal system:** intestinal capillaries merge into the portal vein, which splits into capillaries again inside the liver before returning to the heart. One of only two portal systems in the body (the other is deferred to the endocrine lecture). ✅
- **Bile** is made continuously by the liver, stored in the gallbladder, and ejected when fat arrives. Bile salts act like dish detergent: **emulsification** carves a fat globule into droplets small enough for lipase to reach. ✅
- **Bile also boosts protease activity**, so protein eaten with fat is digested more completely. Bikman suggests this could partly explain studies showing more muscle protein synthesis when protein is eaten with fat. 🟡 He frames it as "could be," not established.
- **Gallstones:** if the gallbladder rarely contracts, bile becomes supersaturated and crystallizes. Bikman states that fat avoidance is one of the most common causes of stones and gallbladder removal. ✅ as lecturer's claim; 🟡 no data cited in the lecture.

## Absorption — Three Macronutrients, Three Routes

| Nutrient | Form absorbed | Mechanism | Destination |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Glucose** | Glucose | SGLT sodium-glucose symport (glucose piggybacks on sodium's gradient) | Blood capillary, then portal vein, then liver |
| **Fructose** | Fructose | GLUT5 (fructose-specific; glucose does not use it despite the name) | Blood capillary |
| **Protein** | Amino acids, di- and tripeptides | Symport with sodium or hydrogen; larger pieces by transcytosis (vesicle) | Blood capillary |
| **Long-chain fat** | Fatty acids plus monoglyceride | Diffuse freely through the membrane, **re-esterify** into triglyceride inside the cell, packaged with cholesterol and protein into a **chylomicron** | **Lacteal (lymph)**, then blood at lymph entry points |
| **Medium- and short-chain fat** | Fatty acids | Absorbed directly | **Blood, then portal vein, then liver**, which must burn them (cannot be stored) |

- **SGLT2 inhibitors** (a diabetes drug class) block this sodium-glucose route; glucose stays in the gut and pulls water with it, causing GI distress. Bikman's pointed alternative: eat less glucose. ✅ 🟡 The editorial comment is his opinion.
- **Medium-chain fats** come from coconut oil and goat dairy (caprylic, capric and caproic acids share a root meaning "goat"). **Short-chain fats** include acetic acid (vinegar) and butyric acid (butter) and are "almost only a product of fermentation." ✅
- **Fermentation logic:** bacteria eat sugars and starches and leave short-chain fatty acids behind, which Bikman says have outsized metabolic benefits (not detailed). 🟡
- **Ketone link:** because medium- and short-chain fats go straight to the liver and cannot be stored, the liver burns them fast enough to make ketones. ✅

## Incretins and GLP-1 — Why Gastric Bypass Cured Diabetes Fast
- **Discovery:** in Roux-en-Y patients, type 2 diabetes improved almost immediately, even while patients were still obese, and blood work revealed big changes in small-intestine hormones called **incretins**. ✅
- **GLP-1 (glucagon-like peptide-1) effects:** increases fat oxidation and lipolysis; activates brain satiety centers; **slows peristalsis**; and **inhibits glucagon**, which lowers blood glucose. ✅
- **Weight-loss drugs (GLP-1 agonists):** first used at lower doses for the glucagon effect and diabetes; weight loss was noticed, so doses were raised roughly five-fold. ✅
- **The cost Bikman highlights:** intestinal motility can be nearly paralyzed; food may sit in the stomach up to ~24 hours (versus a normal 4–5), producing foul belching; in rare cases the gut "freezes" completely, which he calls potentially lethal within days. 🟡 Strong claims with no rates or sources given in the lecture; verify before relying on them.

## Key Corrections to Carry Forward
- 🔴 Assuming digestive enzymes come from the intestinal wall: the small intestine releases only enterokinase; the pancreas, liver (bile) and saliva/stomach supply the digestive machinery.
- 🔴 Assuming digestion and absorption are the same thing: the mouth and stomach digest but absorb nothing; absorption is essentially a small-intestine event (water in the colon).
- 🔴 Assuming all dietary fat enters the blood like sugar and protein: long-chain fat goes via chylomicron into the lymph; only medium- and short-chain fat takes the direct blood-to-liver route.
- 🔴 Assuming fat is the enemy of digestion: fat triggers bile release, bile helps digest protein too, and (per Bikman) regular gallbladder emptying guards against gallstones.
- 🔴 Assuming GLUT5 absorbs glucose: despite the name it is fructose-specific; glucose enters via the sodium-glucose symport.

## Practical Takeaway
Eat protein with its natural fat, treat bile as a feature of fat digestion rather than a nuisance, and understand that the intestine routes nutrients differently: sugar and protein hit the liver via the portal vein, long-chain fat arrives later through lymph. For any gut-hormone drug or bariatric procedure, the key question is what happens to motility and to the duodenum, since that is where digestion and absorption concentrate.

## Questions to Learn From (Socratic) — with Answer Options
**1. A person eats a pure lean-protein meal versus the same protein eaten with natural fat. According to this lecture, what might differ and why?**
A. Nothing differs; bile has no role in protein digestion
B. Fat is required for pepsin to be activated in the stomach
C. \* Fat triggers bile release, and bile salts enhance protease activity, so protein may be digested more completely with fat
*Why C: the lecture states bile "activates and enhances" the proteases and that bile is released only when fat is eaten. Pepsin activation is due to stomach acid, not fat, so B is wrong. The muscle-protein-synthesis link is offered tentatively ("could be").*

**2. Why does the pancreas release bicarbonate at the top of the duodenum?**
A. \* The small intestine has only a thin mucus layer and must be protected from acidic chyme arriving from the stomach
B. Bicarbonate carries CO2 in the blood, as it does in respiration
C. Bicarbonate activates pepsinogen into pepsin
*Why A: Bikman explicitly separates this role from bicarbonate's CO2-transport role in the blood. Pepsinogen is activated by acid, the opposite of what bicarbonate does.*

**3. A supplement of medium-chain fat (coconut oil) is swallowed. Which path best describes it, and what is the consequence?**
A. Packaged into chylomicrons, taken up by the lacteals, and stored in adipose tissue
B. \* Absorbed directly into capillaries, carried by the portal vein to the liver, which must burn it quickly, favoring ketone production
C. Absorbed only in the large intestine, where bacteria convert it into short-chain fats
*Why B: only long-chain fats use chylomicrons and lymph; medium- and short-chain fats go straight to the blood, then the liver, and cannot be stored as fat, so they are oxidized (and generate ketones).*

**4. Which statement correctly links gastric bypass, B12 and GLP-1 in this lecture?**
A. Bypass patients absorb more glucose, which raises GLP-1 and cures diabetes
B. GLP-1 drugs speed up peristalsis, which is why they cause diarrhea
C. \* Bypass removes the intrinsic-factor-producing stomach (so B12 injections are needed) and rerouted the gut, revealing incretin changes such as GLP-1, which slows motility, boosts satiety and lowers glucagon
*Why C: the lecture ties together (1) loss of parietal cells and intrinsic factor, and (2) the discovery of incretins in these patients. GLP-1 slows peristalsis rather than speeding it, and nothing in the lecture says bypass patients absorb more glucose.*
