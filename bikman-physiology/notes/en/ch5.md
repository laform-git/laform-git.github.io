
# Bikman — Fundamentals of Physiology, Ch.5: Gastrointestinal Physiology
*A mouth-to-anus tour of the GI tract (plus liver and pancreas) through four recurring themes — motility, secretion, digestion, absorption — ending with how gut hormones (GLP-1) explain gastric bypass and weight-loss drugs.*

*Source: Dr. Benjamin Bikman, Peterson Academy course "Fundamentals of Physiology," Lecture 5 (transcript). Follows Cardiopulmonary Physiology (Ch.4); the pancreas and glucagon are explicitly handed off to the Endocrine lecture (Ch.6).*

**In plain words:** the gut is a long tube that moves food along, adds juices, breaks food into tiny pieces, and lets those pieces into the body. Most of that work happens in the small intestine, with help from the pancreas and the liver. Each type of food takes a different road in: sugar and protein go into the blood and straight to the liver, while most fat takes a side road through the lymph. This lecture also explains why gastric bypass surgery and the new weight-loss drugs affect diabetes and digestion.

**Words in this note:**
- **GLP-1**: a gut hormone that lowers appetite and blood sugar; also a class of drugs (such as semaglutide)
- **ATP**: adenosine triphosphate, the cell's energy currency
- **NIH**: U.S. National Institutes of Health
- **SGLT1**: a pump that pulls sugar in from the gut
- **GLUT5**: the gut's door for fructose
- **SGLT2**: a pump in the kidney that takes sugar back into the blood; also a class of diabetes drugs that block it
- **FDA**: U.S. Food and Drug Administration
- **ApoB**: apolipoprotein B; counts all the cholesterol particles that can clog arteries
- **DHEA**: a hormone made by the adrenal glands that the body turns into sex hormones

## Why This Matters
This is the "how does food actually become fuel" lecture. Each of the three main food types (macronutrients) takes a different route into the body. Carbohydrates and protein go straight into the blood. Long-chain fat (most dietary fat) takes a detour through the lymph, the body's second drainage system. Short- and medium-chain fats go straight to the liver. That routing is the groundwork for later topics: insulin and glucagon (Ch.6), fat tissue (Ch.7), ketones, and the gut-hormone drugs now in the news. It also explains why digesting fat, bile and protein are linked, which goes against a lot of low-fat folk wisdom.

## The Framework — Four Questions at Every Stop
Bikman walks down the tube from top to bottom and asks the same four questions at each part: **motility** (how it moves food), **secretion** (what the wall releases), **digestion** (breaking big molecules into small ones), and **absorption** (moving small molecules into the body). ✅ Digestion and absorption are different events: you can cut a molecule up without moving anything into the blood.

## Punchlines & Quotable Facts (Lecture 5)
- **"Most digestion... is happening in the small intestine, not because of the small intestine itself, but because of what's connected to it."** He says the intestinal wall releases no digestive enzymes of its own (only enterokinase); the pancreas and liver do the work. ✅ Correction: the pancreas and bile do most of the work, but the intestine's brush border (its finger-like surface) also carries its own enzymes (lactase, sucrase-isomaltase, maltase and peptidases) that finish digesting carbohydrate and protein (Guyton & Hall).
- **The human stomach's acidity is about pH 2.** Bikman places it near a wolf's, just short of scavengers, and reads that as a clue about what humans are built to eat. 🟡 His interpretation, not a measured result. 🟢 Evidence check: a review comparing animals puts the human fasting stomach at about pH 1.5, more acidic than most meat-eaters and closest to animals that eat carrion. Its authors read the high acidity as a filter against germs in food (Beasley 2015).
- **"You will not release bile salts unless you eat fat."** Bile also boosts the protein-cutting enzymes (proteases), so protein eaten with fat is digested better than protein alone. 🟢 Evidence check: fat is the strongest trigger for the gallbladder to empty (through a gut hormone called CCK), but protein also releases CCK and empties the gallbladder (Guyton & Hall). The effect on proteases was shown in lab experiments (Gass 2007).
- **Everything absorbed from the intestine goes to the liver first** (except long-chain fat, which goes through the lymph, as below). This setup, the hepatic portal system, makes the liver "the detoxifier of the bloodstream."
- **Fat is the one food type that does not enter the blood first.** Long-chain fat rides a chylomicron ("a big bus") into the lymph.
- **Short- and medium-chain fats "must be burned, they cannot be stored."** That is why coconut oil or vinegar can push the liver toward making ketones. 🟡 Evidence check: pure medium-chain (C8) oil raises ketones clearly, but coconut oil is mostly lauric acid (C12) and raised ketones only about a quarter as much (Vandenberghe 2017). Evidence that vinegar meaningfully raises ketones is lacking.
- **Incretins (gut hormones that affect blood sugar) were discovered in gastric-bypass patients**, whose diabetes improved almost immediately even though they were still very overweight. 🔴 Evidence check: incretins were known decades earlier (GIP in the early 1970s, GLP-1 in the 1980s; Drucker 2018). The rapid improvement of diabetes in bypass patients was reported in 1995 (Pories 1995) and later linked to large rises in GLP-1 after surgery (le Roux 2006).

## The Journey — Part by Part

| Part | How it moves food | What is released | Digestion | Absorption |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Mouth** | Chewing (breaking food down physically) | Saliva, salivary amylase (a starch-cutting enzyme) | Starch snipped into smaller pieces | None |
| **Esophagus** (food pipe) | Squeezing waves (peristalsis); top third is skeletal muscle (voluntary), bottom two-thirds smooth muscle (automatic) | — | — | — |
| **Stomach** | Stores food ~3–5 hours; churns it into a mush called chyme; stretches ~4–5 times (🟡 textbooks: holds about 0.8–1.5 L, many times its empty size; Guyton & Hall) | Hydrochloric acid (HCl) and intrinsic factor (from parietal cells); pepsinogen (from chief cells); mucus | Pepsin starts digesting protein; salivary amylase keeps working | None |
| **Small intestine** | Squeezing waves | Enterokinase plus brush-border enzymes (lactase, sucrase, peptidases), mucus; plus bicarbonate, enzymes and bile from the helper organs | Main place of digestion | Main place of absorption |
| **Large intestine** (colon) | Slow mass movement (squeezing waves) | Mucus and bicarbonate | Bacteria ferment fiber | Mainly water and salts (sodium, chloride), plus short-chain fatty acids made by bacteria |
| **Rectum / anus** | Inner ring muscle (internal sphincter: smooth, automatic) plus outer ring muscle (external sphincter: skeletal, voluntary) | — | — | — |

✅ The logic of the ring muscles (sphincters): the upper esophageal sphincter and the outer anal sphincter are skeletal muscle (you control them). The lower esophageal sphincter and the inner anal sphincter are smooth muscle (automatic). The fight-or-flight system closes the inner anal sphincter; the rest-and-digest system opens it. The lower esophageal sphincter passes through the diaphragm.

## The Stomach — Acid, Pepsin and B12
- **Parietal cells** pump out hydrochloric acid using active transport: they push hydrogen ions against their gradient, which costs ATP (the "pushing the stone uphill" idea from the cell lecture). ✅
- **Mucus** protects the stomach's own lining from the acid. ✅
- **The acid has three jobs:** kill germs, unfold (denature) proteins so they can be cut, and turn pepsinogen into active pepsin. ✅
- **Chief cells** release inactive **pepsinogen**. Acid snips off a piece to make **pepsin**, a protein-cutting enzyme (protease) that makes the first cuts in protein, producing short chains called peptides. ✅
- **Intrinsic factor** (also from parietal cells) latches onto vitamin B12 and escorts it into the blood. Bikman says B12 comes only from animal foods. Without intrinsic factor, a person can eat B12 and still not absorb it. ✅ (Fortified foods and supplements are the other reliable sources; NIH ODS.)
- **Chyme** is the word for the churned, mushy mix of food and stomach juices. ✅

## The Small Intestine — Where the Work Happens
- **Three sections:** duodenum (first and most important), jejunum, ileum. ✅
- **Surface area:** the wall is covered in finger-like villi, and the villi carry even smaller microvilli ("fingers on fingers," called the brush border). This hugely increases the area for absorbing. Each villus holds **capillaries** (leading to the blood) and a **lacteal** (the smallest unit of the lymph system, built for fat). ✅
- **Roux-en-Y gastric bypass as a teaching case:** the stomach is stapled into a small pouch and the duodenum is skipped. Result: far less food is eaten, and what is eaten is poorly digested and absorbed (diarrhea and gut upset). Intrinsic factor is also lost, so patients need **lifelong B12 injections**. 🟢 Evidence check: lifelong B12 is needed, but guidelines allow high-dose pills, nasal sprays or injections (Mechanick 2019).

## The Helper Organs
### Pancreas
- The **exocrine pancreas** makes digestive enzymes and sends them through a duct into the top of the duodenum. The **endocrine pancreas** makes hormones that go into the blood (saved for Ch.6). ✅
- **Bicarbonate:** the small intestine has only a thin layer of mucus (thin enough to let food through), so the pancreas floods bicarbonate, an acid neutralizer, into the duodenum to neutralize stomach acid. ✅
- **The enzymes arrive switched off** and are switched on in the intestine, which stops the pancreas from digesting itself. ✅ This protective reason is the textbook one. It applies to the protein-cutting enzymes (and phospholipase), while amylase and lipase are released already active (Guyton & Hall).

| Enzyme family | What it works on | What it does | What comes out |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Proteases** (trypsinogen to trypsin; procarboxypeptidase to carboxypeptidase; switched on by enterokinase) | Protein | Trypsin cuts from the middle of the chain (endopeptidase); carboxypeptidase cuts from the ends (exopeptidase) | Single amino acids and pieces of two or three (dipeptides, tripeptides), never longer |
| **Amylase** | Carbohydrate | Finishes what salivary amylase started | Maltose, maltotriose and short starch pieces; brush-border enzymes then release glucose (fructose comes from sucrose, via sucrase) |
| **Lipase** | Triglyceride (fat) | Takes two fatty acids off the glycerol backbone | One-armed fat (monoglyceride) plus free fatty acids |

### The Liver and Bile
- **A multi-tasker:** clears old red blood cells, makes most blood proteins (albumin is the main one), releases hormones, clears waste and toxins, and makes bile. ✅
- **The hepatic portal system:** the capillaries of the intestine join into the portal vein, which splits into capillaries again inside the liver before blood goes back to the heart. It is one of only two portal systems in the body (the other is saved for the hormone lecture). ✅
- **Bile** is made all the time by the liver, stored in the gallbladder, and squirted out when fat arrives. Bile salts act like dish soap: they **emulsify** fat, breaking a big blob into droplets small enough for lipase to reach. ✅
- **Bile also boosts the protein-cutting enzymes**, so protein eaten with fat is digested more completely. Bikman suggests this could partly explain studies showing more muscle-building when protein is eaten with fat. 🟡 He presents it as "could be," not established.
- **Gallstones:** if the gallbladder rarely squeezes, bile becomes too concentrated and forms crystals. Bikman says avoiding fat is one of the most common causes of gallstones and gallbladder removal. ✅ as the lecturer's claim; 🟡 no data cited in the lecture. 🟢 Evidence check: during rapid weight loss, very-low-fat diets raise the risk of gallstones and higher-fat diets lower it (Festi 1998; Stokes 2014). Outside that setting, the main risk factors are being female, age, obesity, pregnancy and genes, so "one of the most common causes" is not confirmed.

## Absorption — Three Food Types, Three Routes

| Nutrient | Form absorbed | How | Where it goes |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Glucose** | Glucose | SGLT1, a door that moves sodium and glucose together (glucose rides along with sodium) | Blood capillary, then portal vein, then liver |
| **Fructose** (fruit sugar) | Fructose | GLUT5 (only for fructose; glucose does not use it despite the name) | Blood capillary |
| **Protein** | Amino acids, pieces of two or three | Moves together with sodium or hydrogen; larger pieces carried through in bubbles (transcytosis) | Blood capillary |
| **Long-chain fat** | Fatty acids plus monoglyceride | Pass freely through the membrane, are **rebuilt into triglyceride** inside the cell, then packed with cholesterol and protein into a **chylomicron** (a fat-carrying bubble) | **Lacteal (lymph)**, then the blood where the lymph joins it |
| **Medium- and short-chain fat** | Fatty acids | Absorbed directly | **Blood, then portal vein, then liver**, which must burn them (he says they cannot be stored) |

- **SGLT2 inhibitors** (a class of diabetes drugs), Bikman says, block this sodium-glucose route; glucose stays in the gut and pulls water with it, causing gut upset. His pointed alternative: eat less glucose. 🟡 The editorial comment is his opinion. ✅ Correction: SGLT2 inhibitors work in the **kidney**, making glucose spill into the urine. The gut transporter is SGLT1, and the usual side effects of these drugs are genital yeast infections and fluid loss, not gut upset (Vallon 2015).
- **Medium-chain fats** come from coconut oil and goat's milk products (caprylic, capric and caproic acids share a root meaning "goat"). **Short-chain fats** include acetic acid (vinegar) and butyric acid (butter) and are "almost only a product of fermentation." ✅
- **How fermentation fits in:** bacteria eat sugars and starches and leave short-chain fatty acids behind. Bikman says these have outsized metabolic benefits (not detailed). 🟡
- **The ketone link:** because medium- and short-chain fats go straight to the liver and cannot be stored, the liver burns them fast enough to make ketones. ✅ for medium-chain fats (St-Onge & Jones 2002; Vandenberghe 2017). 🟡 "Cannot be stored" overstates it: they are mostly burned quickly, but not only burned.

## Incretins and GLP-1 — Why Gastric Bypass Improved Diabetes So Fast
- **The discovery:** in Roux-en-Y patients, type 2 diabetes improved almost immediately, even while the patients were still obese, and blood tests showed big changes in small-intestine hormones called **incretins**. ✅ for what doctors observed (Pories 1995; le Roux 2006). 🔴 The incretins themselves were discovered earlier, outside bypass patients (see the Punchlines check).
- **What GLP-1 (glucagon-like peptide-1) does:** increases fat burning and fat release; switches on the brain's fullness centers; **slows the gut's squeezing waves**; and **blocks glucagon**, which lowers blood sugar. ✅ 🟢 Evidence check: GLP-1's defining job, not named here, is boosting insulin release when blood sugar is high. Direct effects on fat burning and fat release in humans are uncertain (Drucker 2018).
- **Weight-loss drugs (GLP-1 agonists, drugs that copy GLP-1):** first used at lower doses for the glucagon effect and diabetes. Weight loss was noticed, so, he says, doses were raised roughly five-fold. 🟡 Evidence check: the first obesity doses were about 1.2–2.4 times the diabetes doses (liraglutide 1.8 → 3.0 mg daily, Pi-Sunyer 2015; semaglutide 1–2 → 2.4 mg weekly, Wilding 2021). Only the 7.2 mg weekly semaglutide dose approved by the FDA in March 2026 is several times higher (AP 2026).
- **The downside Bikman highlights:** the gut's movement can be nearly paralyzed. Food may sit in the stomach up to ~24 hours (against a normal 4–5), causing foul burping. In rare cases the gut "freezes" completely, which he calls potentially deadly within days. 🟡 Strong claims with no rates or sources given in the lecture; check before relying on them. 🟢 Evidence check: a large database study found higher risks of a paralyzed stomach (gastroparesis), bowel blockage and inflamed pancreas (pancreatitis) with GLP-1 drugs used for weight loss, though the actual risks were low (Sodhi 2023). The 24-hour figure and "deadly within days" are not confirmed.

## Key Corrections to Carry Forward
- 🔴 Thinking digestive enzymes come from the intestinal wall: the small intestine releases only enterokinase (✅ correction: it also has brush-border enzymes that finish digestion). The pancreas, the liver (bile) and the saliva and stomach supply the digestive machinery.
- 🔴 Thinking digestion and absorption are the same thing: the mouth and stomach digest but absorb nothing; absorption is basically a small-intestine event (plus water in the colon).
- 🔴 Thinking all fat in food enters the blood like sugar and protein: long-chain fat goes by chylomicron into the lymph; only medium- and short-chain fat takes the direct blood-to-liver route.
- 🔴 Thinking fat is the enemy of digestion: fat triggers bile release, bile helps digest protein too, and (according to Bikman) regular emptying of the gallbladder guards against gallstones.
- 🔴 Thinking GLUT5 absorbs glucose: despite the name it only carries fructose; glucose enters through the sodium-glucose door.

## Practical Takeaway
Eat protein with its natural fat, see bile as part of how fat is digested rather than a nuisance, and understand that the intestine sends nutrients by different roads: sugar and protein reach the liver through the portal vein, while long-chain fat arrives later through the lymph. For any gut-hormone drug or weight-loss surgery, the key question is what happens to the gut's movement and to the duodenum, since that is where digestion and absorption are concentrated.

## Questions to Learn From (Socratic) — with Answer Options
**1. A person eats a meal of pure lean protein, or the same protein with its natural fat. According to this lecture, what might differ and why?**
A. Nothing differs; bile plays no part in digesting protein
B. Fat is needed to switch on pepsin in the stomach
C. \* Fat triggers bile release, and bile salts boost the protein-cutting enzymes, so protein may be digested more completely with fat
*Why C: the lecture says bile "activates and enhances" the protein-cutting enzymes and that bile is only released when fat is eaten. Pepsin is switched on by stomach acid, not fat, so B is wrong. The link to muscle-building is offered tentatively ("could be").*

**2. Why does the pancreas release bicarbonate at the top of the duodenum?**
A. \* The small intestine has only a thin mucus layer and must be protected from the acidic mush arriving from the stomach
B. Bicarbonate carries carbon dioxide in the blood, as it does in breathing
C. Bicarbonate turns pepsinogen into pepsin
*Why A: Bikman clearly separates this job from bicarbonate's job of carrying carbon dioxide in the blood. Pepsinogen is switched on by acid, the opposite of what bicarbonate does.*

**3. A medium-chain fat supplement (coconut oil) is swallowed. Which route best describes it, and what follows?**
A. Packed into chylomicrons, taken up by the lacteals, and stored in fat tissue
B. \* Absorbed directly into capillaries, carried by the portal vein to the liver, which must burn it quickly, favoring ketone production
C. Absorbed only in the large intestine, where bacteria turn it into short-chain fats
*Why B: only long-chain fats use chylomicrons and the lymph. Medium- and short-chain fats go straight to the blood, then the liver, and cannot be stored as fat, so they are burned (and make ketones).*

**4. Which statement correctly links gastric bypass, B12 and GLP-1 in this lecture?**
A. Bypass patients absorb more glucose, which raises GLP-1 and cures diabetes
B. GLP-1 drugs speed up the gut's squeezing waves, which is why they cause diarrhea
C. \* Bypass removes the part of the stomach that makes intrinsic factor (so B12 injections are needed) and reroutes the gut, revealing gut-hormone changes such as GLP-1, which slows gut movement, boosts fullness and lowers glucagon
*Why C: the lecture links (1) the loss of parietal cells and intrinsic factor, and (2) the discovery of incretins in these patients. GLP-1 slows the gut rather than speeding it up, and nothing in the lecture says bypass patients absorb more glucose.*
