
# Bikman — Fundamentals of Physiology, Ch.4: Cardiopulmonary Physiology
*Why the heart and lungs are one system: a self-starting pump, a two-circuit loop, the pressure physics of breathing, and how blood hands off oxygen and CO₂ where it is needed.*

*Source: Dr. Benjamin Bikman, Peterson Academy course "Fundamentals of Physiology," Lecture 4 (transcript). Same lecturer and same course thread as Ch.1 to Ch.3; this lecture builds on the ion/calcium and skeletal-muscle machinery from earlier chapters and hands off to the endocrine lecture next.*

**In plain words:** the heart is a pump with two sides: the right side sends blood to the lungs to pick up oxygen, and the left side sends that fresh blood to the whole body. The lungs work like bellows, pulling air in by changing pressure, and the blood carries oxygen out and carbon dioxide back. This lecture explains heart sounds, blood pressure numbers, what makes us breathe faster, and how a breathing test (spirometry) tells two kinds of lung problems apart.

**Words in this note:**
- **SERCA**: a pump that pulls calcium back so a muscle can relax
- **ATPase**: an enzyme that uses ATP for energy
- **ATP**: adenosine triphosphate, the cell's energy currency
- **FEV1**: how much air you can blow out in one second
- **VEGF**: a signal that makes new blood vessels grow
- **ApoB**: apolipoprotein B; counts all the cholesterol particles that can clog arteries
- **CAC**: coronary artery calcium score, a heart scan for hardened plaque

## Why This Matters
The heart's only job is to move blood, and the blood is only worth moving because of what the lungs just did to it. Bikman's view: you cannot understand one without the other. This chapter explains the mechanics behind blood pressure numbers, heart sounds, heart murmurs, breathing rate and a breathing-test report. It also shows a theme that runs through the course: the body controls itself through chemistry (carbon dioxide, hydrogen ions, stretch, pressure), not through any central awareness.

## The Two-Loop "Figure 8" — How Blood Flows
The heart has four chambers: two atria on top (right and left) and two ventricles below. Each atrium empties into the ventricle beneath it. Blood moves in a never-ending loop made of two circuits, each powered by one side of the heart. ✅

| Step | Where blood goes | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | **Left ventricle** → aortic valve → body (the body circuit, or systemic circuit) | Biggest, strongest chamber; must drive blood through the whole body |
| 2 | Vena cava (the big vein back to the heart) → **right atrium** → right AV (tricuspid) valve → **right ventricle** | The atria are weak; they just contract and move blood down into the chamber below |
| 3 | Right ventricle → pulmonary arteries → **lungs** (the lung circuit, or pulmonary circuit) | An easy, low-pressure job, so a lighter ventricle |
| 4 | Lungs → **left atrium** → left AV (bicuspid) valve → **left ventricle** | The loop closes; both atria contract at the same moment |

The ventricles are thicker and more muscular than the atria because they must pump blood further. ✅

## Heart Sounds and Murmurs
- The classic "lub-dub" is made by **valves snapping shut**, not by blood moving. ✅
- **Lub (sound 1):** Bikman says it is the left AV (bicuspid) valve closing as the left atrium empties into the ventricle. **Dub (sound 2):** the aortic valve closing after the left ventricle pushes blood out. ✅ for the valve-closing idea. ✅ Correction on timing: the first sound is **both** AV valves (mitral and tricuspid) closing at the start of the ventricles' squeeze, not while the atrium is emptying. The second sound is the aortic **and** pulmonary valves closing (Guyton & Hall).
- He says the right-side valves (tricuspid and pulmonary) are too quiet to hear with a stethoscope, because pressure there is low and flow is easy. The left side makes far more force, so its valves are the ones you hear. 🟡 Evidence check: the left-side sounds are louder, but the right-side closures can be heard too. Doctors listen at a special spot for the tricuspid valve, and they hear the pulmonary valve as the normal "split" of the second sound when you breathe in (Guyton & Hall).
- **Murmur** means the lub-dub is stretched or smeared. Two causes are named: **stenosis** (the valve will not open well, giving a drawn-out "luuub-dub") or **regurgitation** (the valve's shape has changed and lets blood squirt backward). Usually one of the two left-side valves is involved. ✅

## The Smallest Vessels — Where the Real Work Happens
Heart → **arteries** (thick, muscular) → **arterioles** (small arteries) → **capillaries** (the tiniest vessels) → **venules** (small veins) → **veins** → heart. The capillary is the "business part": the only place where blood hands things to cells and takes things back. ✅ Capillaries in the body (muscle, liver, kidney) give up oxygen and take up carbon dioxide (CO₂). Capillaries in the lungs do the exact opposite.

## The Heartbeat Cycle and How Much the Heart Pumps
- **Diastole** = the *ventricles* are relaxed and filling. **Systole** = the *ventricles* squeeze and push blood out. Diastolic and systolic blood pressure (the bottom and top numbers) are the pressures in those two states. ✅
- Bikman stresses being exact: it is the ventricles, not "the heart," that define these phases. The atria actually contract at the end of diastole, adding the last bit of filling, and that starts systole. ✅
- **End-diastolic volume (EDV)** = the blood in the ventricle just before it squeezes. **End-systolic volume (ESV)** = the blood left right after it squeezes.
- **Stroke volume = EDV − ESV** (what leaves with each beat). **Cardiac output = stroke volume × heart rate** (liters per minute), the most useful measure of how well the heart works. ✅

## Why the Heart Beats on Its Own — Its Built-In Wiring
Skeletal muscle needs a nerve for every fiber; the heart makes its own rhythm. That is why it can respond instantly to the automatic nervous system: the fight-or-flight branch (sympathetic) speeds it up and strengthens it, and the rest-and-digest branch (parasympathetic) slows it. ✅

| Feature | Skeletal muscle (Ch.3) | Heart muscle |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **What starts it** | A motor nerve, one per fiber | **Pacemaker (P) cells** in the SA node, the heart's natural pacemaker; no nerve needed |
| **How the signal spreads** | Each fiber separate; some can be used and not others | From cell to cell through tiny connecting channels (**gap junctions**) at the joints between cells (**intercalated discs**); all or nothing |
| **T-tubules** (tunnels that carry the signal inside) | Present | Present in ventricle cells (wider, at the Z lines); few in atrium cells (Bers 2002) |
| **Where calcium comes from** | Only the cell's own calcium store (the sarcoplasmic reticulum, SR) | **From outside the cell first** (through L-type channels), which then opens the SR's ryanodine receptor for the big release |
| **How calcium is cleared** | SERCA pump back into the SR | SERCA **plus two transporters** that pump calcium out of the cell (the sodium-calcium exchanger and the membrane calcium pump), for fast relaxing (Bers 2002) |
| **Grab-and-pull cycle** | Same | Same; only the way calcium arrives is different |

**The wiring sequence:** SA node → walls of the atria (the atria squeeze downward) → **AV node (a deliberate short pause so the ventricles can fill)** → **Purkinje fibers** down to the bottom of the heart → the ventricles squeeze from the bottom up, pushing blood out the exits at the top. One-way valves stop blood flowing backward, because the pressure itself slams them shut. ✅

## The Frank-Starling Law
How much the heart pumps depends on **how strongly the ventricles squeeze** and **how full they are before squeezing** (end-diastolic volume). The Frank-Starling law: **the more the ventricles fill and stretch, the stronger they contract**. On a graph, more filling gives a bigger stroke volume. In practice: when you run and blood comes back to the heart faster, the heart fills more and automatically squeezes harder to push it all out. ✅

## The Lungs — Layout and the Two Things That Control Airflow
- Windpipe (trachea) → bronchi → bronchioles, like an upside-down root system, ending in **alveoli** (tiny balloons). Each alveolus is wrapped in a net of capillaries. Red blood cells arrive loaded with CO₂, drop it into the alveolus, and pick up oxygen. ✅
- **Airflow depends on (1) the pressure difference between the outside air and the alveoli and (2) how wide the airways are.** ✅

**Pressure (Boyle's law: when volume goes up, pressure goes down, and the reverse):**
- The outside air is at about 760 mmHg. The thin space between the lungs and the chest wall (the intrapleural space) is at about 756 mmHg. This slightly negative **intrapleural pressure** (about −4 mmHg compared with the outside air) creates a positive **transpulmonary pressure** (the pressure in the alveoli minus the intrapleural pressure, about +4 mmHg) that keeps the lungs pulled open against the chest wall. Bikman's picture: a fist pushed into a balloon. ✅
- **Breathing in:** the diaphragm pulls down and the muscles between the ribs (intercostals) widen the rib cage → lung volume goes up → pressure falls below the outside air → air flows in. **Breathing out:** volume goes down → pressure rises above the outside air → air flows out. Gravity and body position do not matter to this basic pressure mechanism. ✅ (Body position does change lung volumes and where air goes in the lungs; Guyton & Hall.)

**Resistance (how wide the airways are):** the fight-or-flight system **widens the airways (bronchodilation)**, giving more airflow when you need to run. The rest-and-digest system narrows them slightly (bronchoconstriction), since less is needed. 🟡 In the transcript Bikman says bronchodilation is "reducing the radius"; that is a slip. Dilation widens the airway, and the end of his own sentence (improved airflow) confirms it.

## Stretch, Spring-Back and Surfactant
- **Compliance** = how easily the lung stretches. **Elasticity** = how well it springs back. The lung needs both: elastin (springy connective tissue) plus **surfactant**. ✅
- **Type II alveolar cells** make surfactant, a slightly oily, watery film. Water is sticky (his demonstration: a plate stuck on a wet counter), and without surfactant a normal breath could not inflate the alveoli. ✅
- **Respiratory distress syndrome (RDS)** in newborns: the lungs are the last organ to finish developing, because the unborn baby gets oxygen through the placenta. A premature baby may lack surfactant. The fix is to squirt surfactant into the airway to buy time for the lungs to finish. ✅ (Plus steroids given to the mother before birth; McGoldrick 2020.)

## Carrying the Gases — Oxygen, CO₂ and Hemoglobin
**Oxygen:** about 3% is dissolved in the liquid part of the blood (plasma) (Guyton & Hall). The vast majority (about 97%) is attached to the iron (heme) spots of **hemoglobin**, the oxygen-carrying protein inside red blood cells. ✅ Whether hemoglobin holds on to oxygen or lets it go follows the **oxygen-hemoglobin dissociation curve**. ✅ Correction: it works the other way round from how it is sometimes put: where oxygen is high (the lungs), hemoglobin loads up; where oxygen is low (working tissue), it lets go (Guyton & Hall).

| Signal | Effect on hemoglobin | Where it makes sense |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **More CO₂** | Lets go of oxygen more easily | Working muscle burning glucose or fat makes CO₂ |
| **More hydrogen ions** | Lets go of oxygen more easily | Splitting ATP in a busy muscle makes hydrogen ions (H⁺) |
| **More 2,3-DPG** (a small molecule in red blood cells) | Lets go of oxygen more easily | He says the placenta signals the mother's red blood cells to raise 2,3-DPG so the baby gets plenty of oxygen. 🟡 Evidence check: 2,3-DPG rises in pregnancy, at high altitude and in anemia, but the placental signal is not confirmed. The main reason the unborn baby gets oxygen is that its own (fetal) hemoglobin barely binds 2,3-DPG and so holds oxygen more tightly (Guyton & Hall) |

✅ The first two are what Bikman's learning goals call the **Bohr effect**. Both rise with exercise, so the muscle effectively "asks" for oxygen without any conscious signal.

**CO₂:** some is carried on hemoglobin and some is dissolved in plasma, but by far most travels as **bicarbonate (HCO₃)**, a harmless storage form. At the lungs, bicarbonate splits back into water and CO₂, which is breathed out. ✅

## What Makes Us Breathe — Control of Breathing
- **Central chemoreceptors (chemical sensors in the brain, behind the blood-brain barrier):** CO₂ crosses into the brain, turns toward bicarbonate, and releases a free hydrogen ion. The sensor detects the **hydrogen (pH)**, not the CO₂ itself. More hydrogen ions mean faster, deeper breathing. ✅
- **Peripheral chemoreceptors:** the **carotid bodies** and **aortic bodies**, small sensors on the carotid arteries in the neck and on the aorta just above the heart. They sense both hydrogen ions and CO₂. ✅ They are also the body's main sensors of **low oxygen**, the drive that matters at high altitude and in lung disease (Guyton & Hall).
- So, Bikman says, faster breathing during exercise is not muscles sending a message along a nerve: working muscle changes the CO₂ and hydrogen ions in the blood, and the chemical sensors respond. 🔴 Evidence check: in moderate exercise, CO₂ and pH in the arteries stay close to normal. The current view is that the chemical sensors are not the main drive, and that signals from the brain's own command to move (central command) and from nerves in the working limbs contribute, though no single mechanism fully explains it (Forster 2012).
- **Lung sensors:** irritant receptors (smoke triggers coughing or shallow breathing) and **stretch receptors** (stop the breath in when the lungs are fully stretched). ✅

## Spirometry — Reading a Breathing Problem
Spirometry is a breathing test. It produces a **spirogram**, a trace that rises as you breathe in and falls as you breathe out. Two measures are emphasized (about a dozen exist):

| Measure | How it is taken | A low value points to |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Vital capacity** | Breathe out everything you can, then breathe in as far as you can (some air, the residual volume, always stays in) | A **restrictive** disorder: a scarred, stiff, "leathery" lung (for example from asbestos, dust or smoke) that has lost its stretch |
| **FEV1** (forced expiratory volume in one second) | After a full breath in, blow out as hard and fast as possible; the volume in the first second | An **obstructive** disorder: a narrowed airway, like "breathing through a straw"; breathing in is fine, breathing out is slow |

✅ Note on current standards: obstruction is defined by a low **FEV1/FVC ratio** (how much of the full breath comes out in the first second), not by a low FEV1 alone, because FEV1 also falls in restrictive disease. And a low vital capacity suggests restriction only until the total lung size is measured (Stanojevic 2022).

## Q&A Appendix — Preeclampsia as a Case Study
- Preeclampsia is one of the most common disorders of pregnancy. It shows up as high blood pressure and a growing kidney failure. Doctors check for **protein in the urine** at every visit as the sign of kidney damage. ✅ 🟢 Evidence check: it affects a few percent of pregnancies. Protein in the urine is the classic sign, but current rules allow the diagnosis without it when other organs are damaged, and kidney failure is a severe feature, not the usual picture (ACOG 2020).
- The placenta (the baby's lung, so it is full of blood vessels) uses **VEGF**, a vessel-growth signal, to grow its own vessels by **autocrine** signaling (a cell signaling to itself). The kidney's filter (the glomerulus) also needs VEGF to stay intact. ✅
- In some women, for an unknown reason, the placenta releases **free-floating VEGF receptors** that soak up VEGF. VEGF falls, the kidney filter leaks protein, and the kidneys fail. 🟢 This free-floating receptor is called sFlt-1 (Maynard 2003; Levine 2004). This, not the placenta itself, is what threatens the mother. 🟡 He says the cause of the receptor release is unknown.
- Once seizures begin, it is **eclampsia**, which he says is usually fatal. The only cure is delivering the placenta (and so the baby); he says the kidneys and blood pressure recover within hours. 🔴 Evidence check: eclampsia is a dangerous emergency, but with prompt care most women survive. Magnesium sulfate roughly halves the risk of seizures (Magpie 2002), and the risk of death is far higher where medical care is scarce (Fishel Bartal & Sibai 2022). After delivery, blood pressure often takes days to weeks to settle, and preeclampsia can even first appear after birth (ACOG 2020). The medical balancing act is buying the baby time, often by giving the mother **cortisol-type steroids (anti-inflammatory, not muscle-building)** to speed up the baby's lung development. ✅ (betamethasone or dexamethasone; McGoldrick 2020)

## Punchlines & Quotable Facts (Lecture 4)
- **"You can't really appreciate one without the other."** Heart and lungs as one system.
- **Lub-dub is valves closing, not blood moving.**
- **Diastole and systole belong to the ventricles, not "the heart."** The atria actually contract during diastole.
- **Cardiac output = stroke volume × heart rate; stroke volume = EDV − ESV.**
- **The AV node pause** lets the ventricles fill before the bottom-up squeeze.
- **Heart cells bring in calcium from outside first;** skeletal muscle uses only the calcium stored in its own SR.
- **Frank-Starling: more filling, harder squeeze.**
- **Without surfactant, the stickiness of water would keep the alveoli collapsed.**
- **The brain senses hydrogen, not CO₂,** yet CO₂ is how the hydrogen gets there.
- **Low vital capacity = restrictive; low FEV1 = obstructive.**

## Key Corrections to Carry Forward
- 🔴 "Diastole is the heart relaxing, systole the heart contracting": correct to the ventricles relaxing and filling versus the ventricles squeezing; the atria contract at the end of diastole.
- 🔴 "The heart sounds are blood rushing": they are valves snapping shut, mainly the left-side ones.
- 🔴 "Heart muscle works like skeletal muscle": the same grab-and-pull cycle, but it starts itself, spreads cell to cell, has no T-tubules, and takes calcium from outside first. ✅ Correction: ventricle cells do have T-tubules (Bers 2002).
- 🔴 "CO₂ is carried mostly on hemoglobin": the main carrier is bicarbonate.
- 🔴 "The brain makes us breathe in response to low oxygen": the central drive is the hydrogen ion (pH) produced from CO₂; this lecture does not describe oxygen as the main central signal. ✅ The peripheral sensors do drive breathing when oxygen is low (Guyton & Hall).
- 🔴 "Breathing out happens because the lungs squeeze themselves": it is a pressure change from smaller lung volume (Boyle's law).

## Practical Takeaway
The heart adjusts automatically to demand (Frank-Starling, the automatic nervous system), and breathing adjusts automatically to the body's fuel use (the CO₂ and hydrogen-ion sensors). When something goes wrong, the pattern is usually mechanical: a valve that will not open or close (murmur), a lung that will not stretch (restrictive, low vital capacity), or an airway that will not empty (obstructive, low FEV1).

## Questions to Learn From (Socratic) — with Answer Options
**1. A doctor hears the usual lub-dub stretched into a "luuub-dub." Since heart sounds come from valves closing, what does Bikman say this suggests?**
A. The atria are contracting too weakly to fill the ventricles
B. The capillaries in the lungs are failing to swap gases
C. \* A valve problem, either stenosis (will not open well) or regurgitation (leaks backward), usually in one of the two left-side valves: the left AV (bicuspid) or the aortic valve
*Why C: the sounds are valves closing, so a distorted sound points to a valve that opens or closes badly. The right-side valves are too quiet to be the ones heard.*

**2. When you start running, your breathing speeds up almost immediately. According to this lecture, what drives that?**
A. \* Working muscle raises the CO₂ and hydrogen ions in the blood, which stimulate the central chemical sensors (which sense hydrogen after CO₂ crosses into the brain) and the peripheral carotid and aortic sensors
B. Muscle fibers send a direct nerve signal to the lungs announcing that they are working
C. Stretch sensors in the lungs detect that the muscles need more oxygen
*Why A: Bikman says clearly that it is not the muscle stimulating a nerve; the body detects the chemistry (CO₂ and hydrogen ions) that working muscle produces.* 🔴 This is the lecture's answer; the research view is that the almost immediate rise is mainly driven by nerves (the brain's command to move and feedback from nerves in the limbs), not by the chemical sensors (Forster 2012).

**3. A patient breathes in fully with a normal vital capacity but, when told to blow out as hard as possible, takes many extra seconds to empty the lungs (low FEV1). What type of disorder does this fit, and where is the problem?**
A. Restrictive, in the air sacs, because the lung has lost its stretch
B. \* Obstructive, in the airway, because the lung holds air normally but cannot move it out quickly
C. Lack of surfactant, because the alveoli will not inflate
*Why B: a low vital capacity marks a restrictive problem (a scarred, stiff lung); a low FEV1 with normal capacity marks an obstructive one (a narrowed airway).*

**4. You are jogging, more blood is returning to the heart, and the ventricles fill more each beat. Using Frank-Starling, what happens, and what does it do to cardiac output?**
A. Stroke volume falls because the ventricle is overfilled, so cardiac output drops
B. Only heart rate changes; stroke volume is fixed by the valves
C. \* The extra stretch produces a stronger squeeze, so stroke volume rises, and with a higher heart rate, cardiac output (stroke volume × heart rate) goes up
*Why C: Frank-Starling says "the more the filling, the stronger the squeeze," which automatically matches what goes out to what comes in.*
