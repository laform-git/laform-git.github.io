# The Mass, Return Home — Holy Ground — Read This First

You were baptized. Maybe confirmed. Then life happened, and you drifted.

You don't need to have everything figured out to come back. You only need to come.

Bring the dust of the road. Bring the years of silence. The Father isn't waiting for a polished version of you. **He's waiting for you.** When the prodigal son finally turned for home, he didn't get a lecture at the door. His father saw him far off, and **ran** to him (Luke 15:20).

Guilt says you're bad. Grace says you're wanted.

But first, know what you walked away from. **It isn't just another church service.**

## An auditorium or a temple

Three thousand years ago, King Solomon finished the Temple in Jerusalem. It had taken seven years, and all the cedar and gold a kingdom could gather. On the day of its dedication, the priests carried in the Ark of the Covenant and set it in the innermost room.

Then something happened that no builder had planned. A cloud filled the house. The glory of the Lord came down so heavily that the priests could not stand to do their work (1 Kings 8:10–11). Men had raised a building. God had come to live in it.

Solomon stood before the altar, stretched out his hands, and asked the question every honest heart asks: *"But will God indeed dwell on the earth? Behold, heaven and the highest heaven cannot contain you; how much less this house which I have built!"* (1 Kings 8:27).

The answer came about a thousand years later: *"The Word became flesh and dwelt among us"* (John 1:14). God did come to dwell on earth, and he has not left.

That is the difference between an auditorium and a temple. Walk into most Protestant churches and look at the front: a stage, a pulpit, a band, screens. The room is built for the sermon, and good things happen there. The Bible is preached, and people pray and sing with real faith. But when the people leave, the room is empty. **An auditorium is filled by a crowd. A temple is filled by God.**

## A stage or an altar

Now walk into a Catholic church and look at the front. There is no stage. There is an **altar**.

An altar is not a stage. It's not a podium. **An altar is a place of sacrifice.** Abel, Noah and Abraham built altars. At the Temple in Jerusalem, lambs were offered on one. On Calvary, the Lamb of God was offered once, for all.

The Mass doesn't repeat Calvary. It doesn't act it out like a play: a play is a new performance of an old event. **The Mass is the same sacrifice, made present.** It is not a copy and not a sequel. It is the one sacrifice of Calvary, reaching across the centuries into your Sunday morning.

> ✅ "By a single offering he has perfected for all time those who are sanctified" (Hebrews 10:14). The sacrifice of the Cross and the sacrifice of the Mass are one single sacrifice (Catechism 1367; Council of Trent, 1562).

Hundreds of years before Christ, God promised through the prophet Malachi: *"From the rising of the sun to its setting… in every place… a pure offering"* (Malachi 1:11). Somewhere on earth, right now, a Mass is being offered. That promise is being kept.

The pulpit is for hearing about Jesus. **The altar is where he comes.**

## Holy ground

A shepherd in the desert sees a bush on fire, and it isn't burning up. He walks closer. A voice comes out of the flames and stops him cold:

*"Remove the sandals from your feet, for the place on which you are standing is holy ground"* (Exodus 3:5).

Same dirt. Same desert. But God is there, and that changes everything.

Now look up the aisle of a Catholic church. Those steps lead to the same kind of ground. The altar has been anointed with holy oil. The bones of saints may rest inside it. The priest climbs the steps and kisses it. A red lamp burns beside the tabernacle day and night, for one reason: **he is there.**

In the old Temple, only the high priest could enter the Holy of Holies, once a year, trembling. Today the Holy of Holies is a few steps from your pew, and the door is open every day.

When you bend your knee, you're doing what Moses did. You're taking off your sandals.

**You are not in an auditorium. You are standing before God.** And even with nobody inside, a Catholic church is never empty.

## A pastor or a priest

A **pastor** preaches, teaches and cares for his people, often with real love. But he is a believer chosen and set apart by his community. He leads worship. **He does not offer a sacrifice.**

A **priest** is different. A bishop lays hands on him, in a line of hands on heads that reaches all the way back to the Apostles. The sacrament of Holy Orders changes him forever. At the altar, he doesn't speak for himself: **Christ speaks through him.**

When he says, "This is my Body," it is. When he says, "I absolve you," you are forgiven.

A preacher can tell you about Calvary. **A priest stands at it.**

## A symbol or the real thing

For most Protestants, communion is a symbol: bread and juice, a reminder of a meal long ago. Even Lutherans, who believe Christ is present, say the bread is still bread, and they reject the Mass as a sacrifice.

Catholics believe something far bolder: **after the Consecration, it isn't bread anymore. It is Jesus.** Body, Blood, soul and divinity. And when Mass ends, he doesn't leave. He stays, in the tabernacle.

> 🟢 Protestant views differ by church: Baptists and most evangelicals hold a memorial (symbolic) view; Reformed churches a spiritual presence; Lutherans a real presence without transubstantiation. The Orthodox Churches, like Catholics, believe the bread and wine truly become Christ. The Catholic teaching was defined at the Council of Trent (1551).

Jesus didn't say, "This represents my body." He said, **"This is my body."**

When he taught it in John 6, many of his followers were shocked. They said, "This is a hard saying." And they walked away. He let them go. **He didn't soften one word.**

A symbol can't give you life. Only the real thing can.

> **"Truly, truly, I say to you, unless you eat the flesh of the Son of Man and drink his blood, you have no life in you."**
> — John 6:53
