# The Mass, Return Home — 2 — God Speaks

## Sit down and listen

**What happens:** Everyone sits. A reader goes to the ambo (the stand where the Word is read) and reads from the Bible: usually the Old Testament first, then a Psalm, then a letter of the Apostles.

**Why:** This isn't a history lesson. The Church teaches that when Scripture is read at Mass, **God himself is speaking to his people.** Somewhere in those readings there may be one line meant just for you this week. Listen for it.

> ✅ Church teaching: Christ "is present in his word, since it is he himself who speaks when the holy Scriptures are read in the Church" (Vatican II, *Sacrosanctum Concilium* 7).

At the end, the reader says: "The word of the Lord." You answer: **"Thanks be to God."**

## The Psalm: God's own songbook

**What happens:** Between the readings, a cantor sings a Psalm and everyone repeats a short refrain.

**Why:** The Psalms are the prayers Jesus himself prayed as a boy and as a man. Joy, fear, anger, grief: they're all in there. When you can't find words of your own, the Psalms lend you theirs.

## Stand up: the Alleluia

**What happens:** Everyone stands, and the Alleluia is sung.

**Why:** *Alleluia* is Hebrew for "Praise the Lord!" It's the word heaven sings (Revelation 19). We stand because the King is about to speak. In Lent it disappears completely, so that at Easter it comes back like a shout.

## Three small crosses: mind, lips, heart

**What happens:** The priest or deacon says, "A reading from the holy Gospel according to…" and everyone traces three small crosses with the thumb: **on the forehead, on the lips, and on the heart.**

**Why:** It's a silent prayer:

- **Forehead:** *May the Word of God be in my mind.* Help me understand it.
- **Lips:** *May it be on my lips.* Give me courage to speak it.
- **Heart:** *May it be in my heart.* Let me love it, and let it change me.

Christians have been making this gesture for more than fifteen hundred years. Make it slowly. Mean it.

> 🟢 Ancient tradition of the Latin Church, already known in St. Augustine's time (5th century).

## The Gospel: Jesus speaks

**What happens:** The priest or deacon reads from one of the four Gospels: Matthew, Mark, Luke or John. At the end: "The Gospel of the Lord." You answer: **"Praise to you, Lord Jesus Christ."** The priest then kisses the book.

**Why:** The other readings were God speaking *through* prophets and apostles. In the Gospel, you hear **the words and deeds of Jesus himself.** That's why we stand, why there may be candles and incense, and why the book is kissed. Everything is saying: *listen closely now.*

Try this: take home just one sentence from the Gospel. Repeat it during the week.

## The homily

**What happens:** Everyone sits. The priest (or deacon) explains the readings and what they mean for our lives.

**Why:** God's Word isn't meant to stay on the page. The homily breaks it open, like bread, so it can feed you on Monday morning. Some homilies are better than others. Listen anyway; God can speak through any of them.

## The Creed: why do we say "I believe"?

**What happens:** Everyone stands and says the Creed together: *"I believe in one God, the Father almighty, maker of heaven and earth…"*

Listen to how it starts: not "we believe," but **"I believe."**

Each person in that church is saying it for themselves. Nobody can believe for you. It's your own yes to God, like a promise made out loud.

But you're not saying it alone. The person beside you is saying the same words. So is every Catholic on earth this Sunday, in every language. So did the martyrs and the saints for more than sixteen hundred years. Each "I believe" joins one great voice: **the faith of the whole Church.**

So when you stand and say "I believe," you're doing two things at once: **making the faith your own, and joining your voice to everyone who ever believed.**

**One moment to watch for:** at the words *"and by the Holy Spirit was incarnate of the Virgin Mary, and became man,"* everyone bows. That's the moment God became one of us. (On Christmas, we kneel.)

> ✅ The Nicene Creed comes from the Councils of Nicaea (325) and Constantinople (381).

If you've been away, try this: say each line slowly, and notice which one you're not sure about anymore. That's not failure. That's the place to start asking.

## The Prayer of the Faithful

**What happens:** A reader names intentions: for the Church, the world, the sick, the dead, the parish. After each one, everyone answers: **"Lord, hear our prayer."**

**Why:** Having heard God speak, we speak back, and we don't just pray for ourselves. Silently add your own names: the person you're worried about, the one you can't forgive yet. This is your moment to bring them to the altar.
