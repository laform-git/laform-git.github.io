# On Narcissism — L3 — Clinical Narcissism

**Source:** W. Keith Campbell, *On Narcissism*, Lecture 3: "Clinical Narcissism" (eight-lecture course, about 9.5 hours) · [Peterson Academy](https://petersonacademy.com/courses/on-narcissism)

## Pocket Version

Narcissistic personality disorder is normal narcissism made extreme, rigid across situations and damaging to life. Unlike most disorders it feels good to the person who has it, so the harm falls on spouse, children and coworkers while the narcissist blames them and avoids treatment. Diagnosis needs five of nine DSM criteria and a full history, never a guess from a distance. Shame ("I am bad") turns to rage, while guilt ("I did wrong") can repair, and that is why the narcissist rarely makes amends.

## The Insight

Narcissistic personality disorder (NPD) is not a different kind of person. It is normal narcissism turned extreme, rigid across situations, and damaging. Unlike most disorders, it usually feels *good* to the one who has it. The damage falls on spouses, children and coworkers, and the narcissist blames them and avoids treatment. Two classic theories explain its depths: Kernberg sees a defense against early rage; Kohut sees a defense against early emptiness from missing love.

## Why It Matters

It corrects casual diagnosis: "my ex was a narcissist" usually means he was self-centered, not clinically disordered. It explains why narcissism is so hard to treat: the patient feels fine. And it separates shame from guilt, which shows why the narcissist cannot repair what he breaks.

## Key Points

### 1. What makes something a mental disorder?

A pattern of thoughts, feelings and behaviors that causes significant problems in life, goes beyond the normal, and isn't explained by cultural norms (in some cultures talking to the dead is ordinary). Where "clinically significant" begins is a line drawn by professionals: real but fuzzy. Most disorders lie on a continuum. Disliking public speaking is normal; a performer whose stage fright ends her career has a disorder. Feeling down after a loss is different from major depression: two weeks unable to leave bed, not eating, thoughts of suicide.

> ✅ Consistent with the DSM-5 general definition of mental disorder.

### 2. When personality becomes a disorder: extreme, rigid, damaging

Take normal personality, make it extreme, and take away its flexibility; then it wrecks a life. The point of personality is that you turn it up or down: an extravert should dial it back at a funeral. A man who acts like a big deal on TV is applauded; the same act at home with his wife and children, and at the taco stand, ruins his life.

The damage can be in thinking (distorted thoughts), feelings (too much negative emotion), relationships (damaged ties) or impulses (binge drinking, addiction). About 1 in 10 people meet the criteria for some personality disorder. It must be long-standing, traceable back to adolescence, so a sudden change at 55 points to a stroke, infection or drug, not personality. It must not be better explained by another condition: a manic phase of bipolar disorder can look narcissistic, and so can heavy cocaine or methamphetamine use. The old name was "character disorders."

> 🟢 Prevalence ~9–12% in community samples. ✅ DSM-5 general criteria for personality disorder.

### 3. The three clusters: "weird, wild and worried"

Any trait could in principle become a disorder; which ones get a name is partly a matter of professional politics. (Psychopathy, the public's favorite, isn't in the DSM; the antisocial and narcissistic disorders cover its ground.)

- **Cluster A, odd or eccentric:** *paranoid* (the world is plotting against me; often overlaps with narcissism), *schizoid* (emotionally cut off, little joy, few ties beyond close family), *schizotypal* (social isolation plus odd thinking, such as secret signs and messages; "like baby schizophrenia").
- **Cluster B, dramatic or emotional ("the reality-TV cluster"):** *antisocial* (breaking rules, exploiting others, callousness, impulsiveness); *borderline* (the most studied; named for the border between neurosis and psychosis; love then hate in the same relationship, intense anxiety, feeling that things aren't real, self-harm, suicidal behavior; at its core, an inability to manage emotions, close to vulnerable narcissism); *histrionic* (extreme extraversion and attention-seeking, the "diva," without the meanness); and *narcissistic*.
- **Cluster C, anxious or fearful:** *avoidant* (wants relationships but is too afraid); *dependent* (cannot make decisions without a strong figure; a good predictor of depression. As a resort bellhop, Campbell once helped a panicked guest pack her clothes into tied-up towels because the man she relied on was away); *obsessive-compulsive personality disorder* (so focused on order that one never acts: "bad conscientiousness"). This is different from OCD, which is obsessions plus rituals (roughly 30+ minutes a day).

Many patients get "personality disorder not otherwise specified": something is wrong with their character, but it doesn't fit a box. The big debate: normal personality is mapped by the Big Five, disorder by the clusters. That is two maps where there should be one.

> ✅ DSM-5 cluster structure. ✅ "Borderline" origin: Adolph Stern (1938). 🟢 Psychopathy's absence from the DSM is accurate.

### 4. NPD: mostly grandiose, with vulnerable features

Campbell's estimate: about two-thirds grandiose, one-third vulnerable. Research is hard because large samples of diagnosed patients are rare; much of it comes from big hospital systems in places like Italy.

What defines the disorder is that it is everywhere and inflexible. A little narcissism suits a cocktail party, a job interview or a performance, but not a friend in pain. A graduate-school acquaintance, now a therapist, joked that while clients poured out their feelings he would think, "How do you like my tie?" Being unable to set yourself aside even for a minute is what makes it a disorder.

> 🟡 The two-thirds estimate is Campbell's judgment.

### 5. Four kinds of damage

- **Thinking:** overconfidence and, above all, *not learning from negative feedback*. His made-up boat-wrap businessman insists on his own colors despite every customer's objection and sells nothing. In finance, ego plus risk can sink an institution; a lone rogue trader famously brought down Barings Bank.
- **Impulses:** addiction, especially to stimulants like cocaine (energized, talkative, high on dopamine), alcohol, sex and gambling. Drugs are used to manage the ego.
- **Relationships, the biggest:** most disorders make the sufferer feel bad, so he looks for help. Narcissism feels good: "I'm crushing it; my boss doesn't see it; my wife is lucky to have me." The problem is always other people, so the narcissist wrecks his spouse, children and coworkers and never goes to therapy: "What are you going to tell me? That I'm an 8, not a 10?" (A depressed person who thinks he's a 4 is glad to hear he's an 8.)
- **Feelings:** anger aimed outward and **narcissistic rage** (the restaurant meltdown over a wrong drink), hostility stewing inside ("I'm going to show them all"), and now and then shame.

> ✅ Barings collapsed in 1995 after Nick Leeson's unauthorized trading. 🟢 Narcissists' poor learning from feedback and low treatment-seeking are documented.

### 6. Shame versus guilt

**Guilt:** "I did something wrong and can make amends." I ate your soup; I'll replace it. **Shame:** "I *am* bad." It covers the whole self, with nothing to repair. Narcissistic shame is quickly turned into rage (the classic shame–rage spiral) and shows up only for a second or two when pushed.

> ✅ The shame/guilt distinction is well established (June Tangney). Catholic note: the sacrament of confession works on the logic of guilt — a specific act, owned and repaired — rather than shame about one's whole being.

### 7. The nine DSM criteria (five needed, each causing damage)

The DSM describes a pattern, present everywhere, of grandiosity (in fantasy or behavior), need for admiration and lack of empathy. It begins by early adulthood, shows up in many settings, and is shown by at least five of nine features. In paraphrase:

1. **Grandiose self-importance:** exaggerating achievements; expecting recognition without matching accomplishments.
2. **Fantasies of unlimited success, power, brilliance, beauty or ideal love.** "In fantasy or behavior" covers both the vulnerable dreamer and the grandiose actor.
3. **Believing oneself special,** understood only by, or fit only to mix with, high-status people or institutions: "above-the-line" people versus those below, whose ordinariness might rub off.
4. **Requiring excessive admiration.** What counts as excessive is a judgment call. Among truly high-status people, nobody announces "I'm an alpha male"; the one who does isn't.
5. **Entitlement:** an unreasonable expectation of special treatment or automatic obedience ("Lord Keith would like his mochaccino"). Campbell adds that a *reasonable* expectation of being treated with respect, as a person with a soul, is not entitlement.
6. **Using people:** people as tools or props. "Would you trade your girlfriend for a Porsche?" "Sure, they do the same thing for me."
7. **Lack of empathy:** not blindness to others' feelings, but counting oneself as heavier: "I just matter a little more."
8. **Envy, or believing others envy him.** It is worded with two sides on purpose, to catch both the vulnerable ("why do *you* get that?") and the grandiose ("everyone wishes they were me").
9. **Arrogant, haughty behavior:** the man at a resort party in an ascot claiming unlikely wartime service and dismissing Yale as overrated. His wife later confided that he might be a little narcissistic.

In practice, a full diagnosis happens mainly in hospitals, research studies or expensive private assessments.

> ✅ DSM-5 NPD criteria (301.81), paraphrased.

### 8. Don't diagnose from a distance

When a celebrity has a public breakdown, onlookers call it narcissism, mania, psychosis, cocaine, or narcissism brought on by fame. Without a history, nobody knows; mania and drugs especially can look like narcissism. Campbell warns against labeling public figures.

> 🟢 Consistent with the professional norm against diagnosing people one hasn't examined.

### 9. The psychodynamic models: rage or emptiness

The classic idea, from Freud: early in development, a child comes to identify not with the real self (who makes mistakes, fails his children, gets things wrong) but with an idealized, false self.

- **Kernberg:** narcissism defends against intense early rage, a baby's fury at a parent organized into a grandiose self. Pop the bubble and you meet that rage. This model fits vulnerable narcissism and links it to borderline personality: deep, defensive, about relationships.
- **Kohut:** some narcissism is normal and healthy. A mother's "you're the most beautiful baby" is *mirroring*. A child denied it feels empty and builds a grandiose identity to win the love missing at home: the child actor, the popular academic. Break through it and the result is a crisis of meaning, "I've been doing this my whole life to feel something," a grief that can then heal.

Campbell, a personality researcher, isn't sure either is true, since a baby's trauma can't be studied in a lab. But these traditions shape how expert therapists treat NPD. His own research finds some narcissists *do* describe their narcissism as a mask over vulnerability; those tend to be more neurotic.

> ✅ Kernberg (1975) and Kohut (1971) accurately summarized. 🟡 The psychodynamic etiologies are largely untested empirically.

### 10. Where the field is going

Newer add-on labels: **malignant narcissism** (an old term for a toxic, sadistic, hostile form) and a possible "vulnerable features" label. The DSM-5's alternative model uses a pathological Big Five (the PID-5) in which narcissism shows up as grandiosity and attention-seeking. Some argue that certain disorders, narcissism included, are best understood as about *relationships* rather than the individual. Campbell expects much of today's system to change within a decade toward one combined map.

> ✅ "Malignant narcissism" coined by Erich Fromm (1964). ✅ PID-5 and DSM-5 Section III Alternative Model: NPD = grandiosity + attention-seeking.

## Key Corrections

- **NPD always feels good to its owner.** True of the grandiose presentation; many diagnosed patients, especially with vulnerable features, do suffer and seek therapy, often for depression. 🟡
- **Two-thirds grandiose, one-third vulnerable.** Campbell's estimate; clinical studies often find vulnerable features common in treatment settings. 🟡
- **Psychodynamic origins (Kernberg, Kohut).** Clinically influential but largely untested; twin studies point to substantial heritability. 🟡
- **About 1 in 10 have a personality disorder.** Community estimates range from about 6% to 12% depending on method. 🟢
- **Barings Bank.** Leeson's losses broke the bank, but weak internal controls were equally to blame. ✅

## Meditations

- A disorder that feels good to its owner is paid for by everyone else.
- Guilt says "I did wrong" and can be repaired; shame says "I am wrong" and turns to rage.
- Not learning from correction is the most costly symptom of pride.

## Socratic Question

"If the narcissist feels well while those around him suffer, is a disorder best defined by the pain it causes its bearer or by the harm it does to others, and what does that imply about moral versus medical categories?"
