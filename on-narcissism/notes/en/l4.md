# On Narcissism — L4 — Narcissism in Love

**Source:** W. Keith Campbell, *On Narcissism*, Lecture 4: "Narcissism in Love" (eight-lecture course, about 9.5 hours) · [Peterson Academy](https://petersonacademy.com/courses/on-narcissism)

## Pocket Version

In love the narcissist pursues ego, not the beloved: a trophy partner for status, head games for power, and new options through cheating. Narcissists really are attractive at first, because they try harder with their appearance and radiate confidence, but closeness never deepens while control, lies and conflict grow. This is the chocolate cake pattern: delicious early, sickening later. Ordinary love trades a little ego for closeness. The narcissist refuses that trade and burns people as fuel.

## The Insight

In relationships, the narcissist looks for ego, not for the beloved: status through a "trophy" partner, power through head games, and options through cheating. Narcissists are truly attractive at first (confident, energetic, well-groomed), but closeness never deepens, while control, lies and conflict grow. This is the "chocolate cake" pattern: delicious at first, sickening later. Ordinary love gives up some ego to gain closeness; narcissism refuses that trade and ends up burning people as fuel.

## Why It Matters

It answers the painful question victims ask afterward, "How was I so stupid?", by showing that the attraction was real and predictable. It names the manipulation tactics (game playing, gaslighting, breadcrumbing, ghosting) and what protects against them. And it shows that love depends on being willing to lose a little self-importance for someone else.

## Key Points

### 1. What narcissists want: a boost, not closeness

Narcissism works the same way in friendships, parenting and work, but most research is on romance, where people also choose least carefully. Campbell's dissertation (*Narcissism and Romantic Attraction*) found that narcissistic students preferred partners who were high-status and attractive. Emotional closeness wasn't unimportant, just ranked lower. The "trophy spouse" is a partner used as a tool to display success. The same logic applies to bragging about children, rich friends or distinguished ancestors: relationships as status tools.

> ✅ Campbell (1999), *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*.

### 2. Short-term mating and power

Especially among men, narcissists seek many short-term sexual partners as a status marker: people as a means to an end. They also seek power over a partner, which brings status and control. Sociology's **principle of least interest** says the partner who cares least holds the most power. Narcissists set this up through **game playing**: "I love you; no, I don't; you're good enough; no one's good enough." They keep the partner unsure of where they stand, like Narcissus with Echo.

> ✅ Principle of least interest: Willard Waller (1938). 🟢 Campbell, Foster & Finkel (2002): narcissism predicts game-playing love style.

### 3. Gaslighting, breadcrumbing, ghosting

**Gaslighting** comes from a 1938 British play, later filmed. A husband secretly dims the gas lamps and makes noises in the attic, then insists his wife is imagining it, until she doubts her own grip on reality. Once people can't trust what they see, they can be controlled; cults use this too. Campbell's image is the billfish (marlin, swordfish), which slash through a school of fish to confuse it, then feed: knock them off balance, then prey on them. Little formal research exists yet.

Two newer online patterns his students describe: **breadcrumbing** (giving out just enough interest to keep someone hooked without a real relationship) and **ghosting** (vanishing without a word after what seemed like mutual care).

Campbell, who jokes about everything, stresses that these relationships can be devastating. His advice: find allies, someone you trust who can keep confirming what is real.

> ✅ *Gas Light*, Patrick Hamilton (1938); films 1940 and 1944. 🟡 Gaslighting, breadcrumbing and ghosting are not yet well researched.

### 4. Cheating and an eye on alternatives

Committed partners normally have a protective habit: they mark down attractive alternatives, so other people seem less appealing. Grandiose narcissists don't. They show high **attention to alternatives**, and more cheating. Part of it is ego (being desired by someone new is a boost), part is the reward-seeking of high extraversion. The narcissist explains it away: "It doesn't change how I feel about my wife, so it's a win-win." A win for him.

> ✅ Campbell & Foster (2002): narcissism, commitment and attention to alternatives. 🟢 Narcissism predicts infidelity in multiple studies.

### 5. Trading narcissism for love

Next to a stranger, "Who's better-looking?" "Me." Next to one's wife: "My wife." In close relationships, the bias in our own favor turns into a bias in the *other's* favor: credit to the children, blame shared with the friend after a team failure. Normally we trade ego for love, giving up something to gain something better. This is what marriage and children are.

Narcissists don't make that trade; after a shared failure they blame others. People usually rate their partners as about equal to themselves. Narcissists still rate themselves superior on status traits (intelligence, success, creativity) even while valuing a partner's looks, which strains any relationship. They also want to be the best *and* to be surrounded by the best. Facing someone of truly higher status, they flatter that person while belittling everyone else. Campbell's measure of having "made it": being the dumbest person at the table.

> 🟢 Self-serving bias reverses in close relationships (Sedikides, Campbell et al., 1998). Catholic link: love "does not insist on its own way" (1 Cor 13:5).

### 6. Self-protection: rejection, control and violence

The triggers are ego threat ("you're not as attractive as you think"), rejection ("I'm leaving"), and limits on freedom ("you can't do that"). Responses range from defiance to the deadly logic "If I can't have you, no one can." Campbell mentions well-known cases in which a celebrity was accused of killing a partner who tried to leave, as examples of control driven by status, noting that he doesn't know all the facts.

As a postdoc, he and Jean Twenge noticed that the writings of one of the Columbine shooters read like items from the Narcissistic Personality Inventory. Their lab studies on social rejection supported a model for some (not all) school shootings: rejection, then reasserting dominance through violence, often with a manifesto and a hunger for fame, which the media too often supplies. His rule: "If you're writing a manifesto, seek help."

> 🟢 Twenge & Campbell (2003): narcissists respond to rejection with aggression. 🟡 School-shooting motives vary widely; this is one pattern.

### 7. Why narcissists are attractive

They look better, but because they try harder. In one study, photos taken "as you normally look" showed narcissists rated as more attractive; after removing makeup, jewelry and styling, the difference disappeared. On dating apps they choose more flattering photos. (Campbell's joke: ordinary men's photos all show them holding a fish, the only time a normal man wants his picture taken.)

Above all, confidence and energy are attractive at a first meeting. In "thin slices" research, people rate grandiose narcissists as more likable after only 30 seconds of video. Vulnerable narcissists aren't attractive in this way; partners often enter those relationships as caregivers and discover the ego later.

> ✅ Holtzman & Strube (2010) adorned/unadorned study. ✅ Back, Schmukle & Egloff (2010) on narcissists' first-impression popularity.

### 8. Why they're bad partners

Control (of money, friends, access), the common kind of couple violence, cheating and **mate poaching** (luring people away from their current relationships), and lying. Narcissism and psychopathy are among the top predictors of dishonesty. Not white lies ("you look great," "you've got this"), but useful lies that make the narcissist look better or get him what he wants.

And little **accommodation**. When one partner acts badly ("Why is there no food in the fridge?"), the other can make it worse or calm it down. Narcissists make it worse, and the exchange spirals; Campbell's comic version ends with "your mother paid me to marry you."

> 🟢 Accommodation: Rusbult et al. (1991). Mate poaching: Schmitt & Buss (2001). Narcissism and dishonesty are well documented.

### 9. The chocolate cake model

We choose partners by passion and attraction, but long-term happiness depends on companionate love: warmth, care, reliability. Chocolate cake: a thrilling first bite and energy, then 20 minutes later tiredness and self-disgust. Salad: nothing special at first, then steady well-being.

A narcissistic relationship follows the cake:

1. early excitement ("I'm dating someone in finance!");
2. a request for closeness ("meet my family?") goes nowhere;
3. suspicions, lies, control (even choosing your clothes so you make him look good);
4. self-blame and head games ("You're the crazy one");
5. collapse, followed by two years of asking, "How did I fall for that?"

The answer: attractiveness, charm, and the foot-in-the-door slide into toxicity.

The data: people drawn to narcissistic partners mentioned ambition, status, charm, sexual attraction and confidence; people drawn to non-narcissists mentioned similarity and niceness. The worst features of narcissistic partners: self-centeredness, game playing, dishonesty, control, feeling like a trophy, materialism. People rarely regret ending a relationship with a narcissist; they more often regret ending one with a kind partner. When people drew their relationships over time, the narcissist was more satisfying and exciting at first, then the lines crossed. Emotional closeness never developed, while with others it kept rising. The trade: excitement and an ego boost in exchange for closeness and deep connection.

On "women don't like nice guys": people love kindness. What isn't attractive is passivity dressed up as niceness. Kindness joined to ambition, like wanting to end hunger somewhere, is very attractive.

> ✅ Campbell (2005), "chocolate cake" model; Brunell & Campbell research on relationship trajectories.

### 10. People as firewood

The bigger picture (his "extended agency" model): the grandiose narcissist sees every person as fuel: an attractive partner, flattering employees, a parent who buys a car. He draws them in, burns them for self-esteem, and throws them away, like an aging star who drops partners once they pass 25. It is predatory, shark-like, and in the end empty, like daily cocaine: a rush each time, a deepening emptiness. In the end: "Nobody actually loves me. Nobody knows who I am." Isolated, perhaps forgiven at the grave.

> 🔴 Campbell's interpretive model.

### 11. Highlights from the Q&A

- **Trapped with children, money and friendships tangled together:** Campbell won't tell anyone what to choose, but advises protection first: a good family lawyer, a network of friends to keep you grounded, safety for the children, and, if there's violence, the police. In safer situations, set firm boundaries and reward caring behavior by tying it to his ego (go to the game; be seen and praised as a good father). Watch for manipulation in divorce; he has heard of men provoking a wife into an outburst, then calling the police.
- **Men versus women:** both look for a boost to the self. The clearest difference is that men gain status among men from many sexual partners, while women generally don't.
- **Does culture matter?** Yes. A culture of personal branding, and of choosing partners from curated photos as if shopping online, pushes people toward shallow, materialistic, using relationships.

> 🟢 The general safety advice reflects standard domestic-violence guidance (National Domestic Violence Hotline, 1-800-799-7233).

## Key Corrections

- **Celebrity partner killings.** Campbell's examples involve accusations, not always convictions; at least one famous defendant was acquitted in criminal court. Use them only as illustrations of the "if I can't have you" logic. 🟡
- **Columbine and narcissism.** Rejection-then-violence is one supported pattern, but investigators have also described one shooter as psychopathic and the other as depressed; motives in school shootings vary widely. 🟡
- **Gaslighting, breadcrumbing, ghosting.** Popular terms with limited formal research; useful labels, not established clinical constructs. 🟡
- **Regret after leaving a narcissist versus a kind partner.** Based mainly on Campbell's own survey research; not widely replicated. 🟡
- **Men gain status from many partners, women don't.** A common finding, but the size of this sexual double standard varies by culture and recent studies find it smaller than once thought. 🟡

## Meditations

- Ordinary love trades a little ego for a great deal of closeness.
- Whatever is used to feed the self is eventually thrown away.
- Thrilling at first is not the same as good over time.

## Socratic Question

"If love requires giving up some self-importance for the sake of another, can a person who will not surrender ego ever truly love, or only be loved?"
