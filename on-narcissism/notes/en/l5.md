# On Narcissism — L5 — Narcissism at Work

**Source:** W. Keith Campbell, *On Narcissism*, Lecture 5: "Narcissism at Work" (eight-lecture course, about 9.5 hours) · [Peterson Academy](https://petersonacademy.com/courses/on-narcissism)

## Pocket Version

At work and in politics, narcissism is a real trade-off. On one side: confidence, boldness and stage presence. On the other: hogging credit, poor ethics, needless risk and using up shared resources. The benefits show early and the costs later, so organizations keep hiring narcissists, especially in unstable times, and the narcissist usually wins even when the institution loses. In games over a shared resource, narcissists take first and fastest and drag everyone into running it dry. Leadership by respect rather than by fear is possible, as Washington showed by giving up power, but it is rare.

## The Insight

At work and in politics, narcissism is a real trade-off. It has a bright side (confidence, boldness, public performance, a willingness to lead) and a dark side (bias in its own favor, poor ethics, costly risks, using up shared resources). The benefits show early and the costs later, so organizations keep choosing narcissists, especially in unstable times, and the narcissist usually comes out ahead even when the institution doesn't. Leadership earned through respect (prestige) rather than fear (dominance) is possible, but historically rare.

## Why It Matters

It explains why intelligent boards and voters keep picking self-absorbed leaders, and why that isn't always a mistake. It shows how one selfish person can drag a whole group into destroying a common good. And it separates two paths to authority, fear and respect, which bear directly on what makes a leader good rather than merely effective.

## Key Points

### 1. Bright side and dark side

Workplace psychologists such as Robert Hogan have long described narcissism as both an asset and a liability in leadership. The bright side shows at a first meeting and when things are calm; the dark side shows later or under stress. Unlike a daughter's narcissistic boyfriend ("get him out of here"), a narcissistic leader might be exactly what an organization needs for a while.

> ✅ Hogan's bright-side/dark-side framework (Hogan Development Survey).

### 2. The problem employee: harmful behavior, no extra effort

**Counterproductive work behaviors** (taking office supplies, fraud, cooking the books for a bigger bonus, harassment) are predicted by narcissism, especially when the narcissist feels unfairly treated and "rebalances the scales" by taking 42 reams of paper. **Organizational citizenship behaviors** are the unscripted extras, like organizing a coworker's birthday or the softball league, and that is where narcissists are missing: "It's not in my job description; why are you wasting your time?" Others resent carrying that load.

> 🟢 Grijalva & Newman (2015) meta-analysis: narcissism predicts counterproductive work behavior, weakly related to citizenship.

### 3. Hogging credit and entitlement

The **self-serving bias** ("I succeeded because I'm awesome; we failed because of a colleague") eats away at teams. The person thrown under the bus eventually strikes back, and the system breaks down. Like a ball hog in sports, it's tolerated only from the very talented, and most narcissists aren't. Organizations often call Campbell about a "rainmaker" they want to fire but can't.

**Entitlement** cuts both ways. It is unpleasant to work with, yet entitled people demand raises and threaten to leave, and on average they get paid more.

> 🟡 The pay advantage of entitlement is suggested, not firmly established.

### 4. Why narcissists get hired

Job interviews reward the same traits as first dates: confidence, energy, poise. Most newly promoted people feel the **impostor phenomenon** for a while (the fear of being found out). Narcissists don't: "I should be the boss; I deserve more." That reads as readiness. They also signal boldness and a taste for risk, prized in "move fast and break things" industries. Structured interviews help, but even executive assessment centers admit they partly select for narcissism because they want confidence. In sales, quick one-time selling may benefit; long-term relationship sales probably suffers.

> ✅ Impostor phenomenon (Clance & Imes, 1978). 🟢 Narcissists perform well in job interviews (Paulhus et al., 2013).

### 5. Where narcissists go

Toward status, prominence and money: leadership, finance, law, consulting, sales, some performing arts (especially reality TV), surgery rather than family medicine. Away from helping professions such as teaching. Veterinarians (very skilled, often modestly paid, "an act of love") score low.

Even in religion: a Canadian study found that more narcissistic pastors led bigger congregations; the pull is toward status and power. Campbell, a casual churchgoer, remembers a Southern California megachurch with a band, a coffee shop, a skate park and a basketball court ("Disneyland"), which felt to him more ego-centered than a traditional parish Mass.

> 🟢 Business students score higher than psychology students on average. 🟡 The pastor study is reported but its details are unverified here. Catholic note: the Church warns explicitly against seeking ecclesiastical office for prestige (Mt 23:5–12).

### 6. Spotting the narcissistic leader

CEOs rarely fill out questionnaires, so researchers use signs they can observe from outside: attention to looks and dress; private rage at staff that is hidden in public; rushing toward any camera (the old Washington joke that the most dangerous place in town was between a certain senator and a camera); cheating and a trail of personal wreckage. Business-school studies found that narcissistic CEOs had larger photos in annual reports and bigger signatures.

Predator nicknames: a CEO famous for mass layoffs was called "Neutron Jack" (the people gone, the buildings still standing); another corporate raider was "Chainsaw Al," photographed among stuffed predators. A biotech founder took on a black turtleneck and a deeper voice to copy Steve Jobs, before her company was exposed as a fraud.

> ✅ Chatterjee & Hambrick (2007): CEO narcissism measured by photo prominence and pronoun use; Jack Welch ("Neutron Jack"), Al Dunlap ("Chainsaw Al"); Elizabeth Holmes was convicted of fraud in 2022.

### 7. The trade-offs: confidence, risk, starting businesses, performing, ethics

- **Confidence vs. overconfidence:** willing to take on the job, but not learning from mistakes or asking for help. We judge by the outcome: if a fraudulent product had worked, its founder would be a business-book hero.
- **Taking risks:** narcissistic tech CEOs make big, splashy public acquisitions. Some succeed brilliantly, others fail. Sometimes a fired founder is brought back (Apple and Steve Jobs).
- **Starting businesses:** predicted by narcissism (confidence, comfort with risk when it's other people's money). Yet a large Silicon Valley study found that *agreeable* founders raised money more easily.
- **Performing in public:** narcissists shine on stage, on TV and in fundraising; much of a university president's job is ego work, not management. In a classic study, narcissists threw darts better when told they were being watched and worse in private.
- **Ethics:** decisions that serve themselves; leaders who run companies into the ground but still collect payouts.

> ✅ Wallace & Baumeister (2002) public-performance study. 🟢 Chatterjee & Hambrick on acquisitions; Jobs was ousted in 1985 and returned in 1997.

### 8. Early benefits, late costs, and the narcissist usually wins

As in romance, the benefits arrive early (confidence, charm, performance, being liked at first) and the costs later: bad decisions, needless risks ("leaders lead, so they have to *do* something"), growing dislike, self-destructive acts like trashing a hotel on the company's tab. Yet the narcissist usually does well even when the company fails. Campbell recalls a home-improvement retailer whose cooperative culture was wrecked by a CEO brought in from a cutthroat corporate system. He left with a large payout and was hired to run a carmaker. "Wreck a place, get paid, move on."

> 🟢 Golden-parachute patterns are widely documented. 🔴 The retailer story is Campbell's retelling.

### 9. The tragedy of the commons

A shared pasture grows back if each herder grazes five sheep. One grazes ten; the others notice and follow; the pasture dies. The same happens with fisheries and forests. In Campbell's lab, narcissists harvested more at the start of these shared-resource games and dragged everyone into running out faster. Yet *they* ended up with the most, having taken "firster and faster."

Real fisheries: a fish becomes fashionable (Patagonian toothfish, sold as Chilean sea bass), gets overfished, and the market moves on. The only defense is strong rules that are enforced.

> ✅ Garrett Hardin, "The Tragedy of the Commons," *Science* (1968). ✅ Campbell, Bush, Brunell & Shelton (2005). 🟢 Patagonian toothfish overfishing and poaching are documented. Catholic link: the universal destination of goods and care for the common good (CCC 2402–2406; *Laudato Si'*).

### 10. Is a moderate amount of narcissism best?

Some argue for an upside-down U: too little narcissism hurts leadership, a moderate amount helps, too much hurts again. Campbell sees this found about half the time.

> 🟡 Grijalva et al. (2015) meta-analysis supports a curvilinear relationship for leadership *effectiveness*; replication is mixed.

### 11. Why we choose them: first movers and unstable times

Leadership means moving a group toward a goal. Leaders are often simply the people who want it most and act first: "Who wants to be in charge?" "I will." We are especially drawn to confident, strong figures when things feel unstable; those who stir up fear and chaos prepare people to accept strong authority. Narcissism also thrives where ethics are weak, like the high-pressure stock shops shown in films such as *The Wolf of Wall Street*.

The usual arc: a honeymoon, then risky self-centered decisions and aggression toward enemies, then hostility and removal. The occasional deeply decent leader with little narcissism may be admired as a person but judged "not tough enough" for the job.

> 🟢 Threat increases preference for dominant leaders (Laustsen & Petersen, 2015).

### 12. Political leaders and U.S. presidents

A political-science study of tens of thousands of serious U.S. candidates found that the single-question narcissism score went along with political ambition. Historians and biographers rated every president's personality before office on 30 Big Five facets, and researchers estimated narcissism from these.

The results: narcissism has risen across U.S. history (with early exceptions like Andrew Jackson and Theodore Roosevelt), especially since television made the presidency a media job; the 1960 Kennedy–Nixon debate is the symbolic turning point. Lyndon Johnson scored highest, and he passed Medicare and a great deal of legislation. More narcissistic presidents showed better leadership in a crisis, more laws passed, more popular votes, more public persuasiveness, higher "greatness" ratings from historians and more risk-taking. But they also showed more abuse of power, more unethical behavior, more impeachment resolutions, and more putting political success before policy.

Campbell's own study (described without taking sides): supporters and opponents of a recent president agreed he was very extraverted and impulsive, but disagreed sharply on agreeableness and conscientiousness. Opponents saw an extremely disagreeable man; supporters saw a "straight shooter." The same figure can look narcissistic or just tough depending on which side you're on, like rival fans judging the same famous football coach.

> ✅ Watts, Lilienfeld et al. (2013), *Psychological Science*: presidential narcissism, with Johnson highest; data from Rubenzer & Faschingbauer. ⚠️ The greatness ratings are Dean *Simonton*'s. 🟡 The candidate study is reported but unverified here.

### 13. The "energy clash" model: throwing a cat into the bag

Boards sometimes put in a disruptive narcissist on purpose to shake up a stuck organization. With his advisor Constantine Sedikides, Campbell describes three stages:

1. **Shake-up:** the newcomer makes changes right away, with no six-month listening tour.
2. **Conflict:** after a honeymoon, departments push back; academics hold votes of no confidence.
3. **Resolution:** the leader purges opponents or is pushed out, and the system resets.

Recent shake-ups at tech companies (a takeover with mass departures, a CEO fired and rehired within days) fit the pattern. You'd never do this to a family; organizations do it on purpose.

> ✅ Sedikides & Campbell (2017), *Perspectives on Psychological Science*.

### 14. Fear versus respect, and the rare good leader

There are two routes to leadership: **dominance** (feared, fights to the top) and **prestige** (respected, lifted to the top). A World War II tank commander told Campbell that his men hated Patton, who ordered them to strip off the sandbags they'd added for protection because it "made them look weak," and loved Bradley, "the soldier's general," who treated them as human beings. Dominance leaders are more narcissistic, aggressive and neurotic; prestige leaders are more sociable, agreeable and ethical.

Through history, most rulers seized power and never let go. When George Washington gave up power of his own will, people of his time could only compare him to Cincinnatus, the Roman who saved the republic and went back to his farm. Shakespeare's *Henry V* shows the ideal at Agincourt: the king tells his common soldiers that whoever fights beside him today is his brother: "we few, we happy few, we band of brothers." Noble leadership based on courage, strength and ethics is possible without narcissism. It's just rare.

> ✅ Henrich & Gil-White (2001); Cheng et al. (2013): dominance vs. prestige. ✅ Washington as "the American Cincinnatus"; *Henry V*, Act 4, Scene 3. 🟡 The Patton sandbag account is a single veteran's recollection.

## Key Corrections

- **Presidential greatness ratings.** The historian ratings used in Watts et al. (2013) are Dean Simonton's; personality estimates come from expert raters, not the presidents themselves, so they are indirect measures. 🟡
- **Narcissistic pastors and larger congregations; agreeable founders raising money more easily.** Both studies are reported by Campbell but not verified here. ⚠️
- **The moderate-narcissism "sweet spot."** Supported in some meta-analytic work, but replication is mixed. 🟡
- **Entitled employees earn more.** Suggestive, not firmly established. 🟡
- **The retailer CEO story.** The outline is public record (a large exit payout followed by an automaker post), but "wrecked the culture" is Campbell's interpretation. 🔴
- **Megachurch versus parish Mass.** A personal impression, not data. 🔴
- **Patton and the sandbags.** One veteran's memory; Patton's men also widely admired him, so the contrast with Bradley is simplified. 🟡

## Meditations

- What dazzles at the start is not what lasts to the end.
- One selfish choice can teach a whole community to devour its common good.
- The rarest leader is the one who can lay power down.

## Socratic Question

"If narcissistic leaders often accomplish more but behave worse, should a society judge leaders by results or by character, and can the two truly be separated over the long run?"
