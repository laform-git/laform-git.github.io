# On Narcissism — L1 — The Faces of Narcissism

**Source:** W. Keith Campbell, *On Narcissism*, Lecture 1: "The Faces of Narcissism" (eight-lecture course, about 9.5 hours) · [Peterson Academy](https://petersonacademy.com/courses/on-narcissism/lecture-1)

*About the course:* Campbell is a research psychologist (social and personality psychology, University of Georgia). He traces narcissism's history, how it is measured and how it works; its clinical form (narcissistic personality disorder, NPD); its effects on relationships, work and social media; and how narcissistic traits can change. He studies ordinary people, not patients, and does not diagnose. Eight lectures, one note each.

## Pocket Version

Narcissism is a normal trait everyone has to some degree. It is also a childhood phase, a clinical disorder and a cultural trend. At its core are an inflated self-image, little emotional warmth toward others, and strategies (flattery, status, attacking critics) to keep the illusion alive. It has a grandiose face that goes on the offensive and a vulnerable face that is resentful and defensive. It can be measured because people readily admit it. It differs from healthy self-esteem, which values oneself without needing to stand above others.

## The Insight

Narcissism is not one thing. It is a normal personality trait everyone has to some degree, a stage of development, a clinical disorder, and a cultural variable. It has three core features: an inflated view of oneself, relatively little emotional warmth toward others, and strategies for keeping that inflated self-image alive. It shows two main faces, grandiose (offensive, seeking attention) and vulnerable (defensive, resentful), plus a clinical one.

## Why It Matters

It corrects the popular idea that "narcissist" is just an insult for a vain person. It shows that the ego's habit of puffing itself up is common to everyone, so narcissism becomes a lens for understanding the ego in general. And it separates narcissism from its neighbors (self-esteem, entitlement, psychopathy), which are often confused with it.

## Key Points

### 1. Why study it: the ego at its most visible

Campbell came to narcissism through research on self-enhancement: the tendency to see oneself as better than one is. Most people show the "better-than-average effect." Ask a room how attractive they are and the average comes out about 7 out of 10. In graduate school (Wisconsin, around 1990–91, with his advisor Constantine Sedikides) he wondered whether some people did this far more than others. He was also drawn to Buddhist and Vedic ideas of non-self, but couldn't study selflessness: "I can study ego." Narcissism lets you watch the ego working in its most visible form, which helps explain the ego in everyone.

> 🟢 The better-than-average effect is one of the most replicated findings in social psychology.

### 2. The myth of Narcissus

The best-known telling is Ovid's *Metamorphoses* (Book 3). Narcissus, son of a river god and a nymph, is so beautiful that people fall in love with him at birth. The seer Tiresias prophesies he will live long "if he never knows himself." At about 16 he is adored by men and women; none is good enough for him. The nymph Echo, cursed to repeat only the last words of others, falls for him. He rejects her, and she wastes away in a cave until only her voice remains. A rejected admirer prays for justice. Narcissus sees his reflection in a still pool, falls in love with it, can never touch it, and wastes away, becoming the narcissus flower.

Campbell's reading: it is a story of self-love that is never returned. His self-love destroys him, and destroys everyone around him on the way.

> ✅ Ovid, *Metamorphoses* 3.339–510. ⚠️ Small corrections: the goddess who answers the prayer is Nemesis (Campbell guesses Zeus), and the narcissus is a daffodil-family flower, not a day lily.

### 3. A short history of the idea

- **1850s:** the first clinical description Campbell has found, "the mania of Narcissus." It is a calm mania in which the patient is infatuated with his own beauty, wit, dress, talents, titles and birth. The concern with *titles* is the root of what we now call *entitlement*.
- **Late 1800s:** Havelock Ellis treats it as a sexual condition: erotic love of oneself.
- **1914:** Freud, *On Narcissism*. He describes narcissism as a normal childhood phase (a naked toddler shouting "look at me" is normal; a 25-year-old doing it is not), as tied to self-preservation, and as identifying with an idealized self-image (the "ego ideal") rather than one's real strengths and weaknesses. This was later called the "false self."
- **1930s:** Henry Murray at Harvard (*Explorations in Personality*) builds the first self-report measure ("narcism").
- **1979–80:** the Narcissistic Personality Inventory (NPI), from Berkeley, treats narcissism as a normal trait on which everyone falls somewhere.
- **1970s:** narcissism as a personality disorder, through Heinz Kohut (*The Analysis of the Self*) and Otto Kernberg (*Borderline Conditions and Pathological Narcissism*).
- **1970s onward:** narcissism as a cultural variable. Tom Wolfe's essay on the baby boomers' "Me Decade"; Christopher Lasch's *The Culture of Narcissism*, which influenced President Carter's 1979 "malaise" speech; Twenge and Campbell's *The Narcissism Epidemic* (2009).

> ✅ Freud 1914, Murray 1938, Raskin & Hall NPI 1979, Kohut 1971, Kernberg 1975, Lasch 1979, Carter's "Crisis of Confidence" speech (July 1979) shaped partly by Lasch, Twenge & Campbell 2009. ⚠️ The 1850s author is Joseph Guislain, a *Belgian* psychiatrist (Campbell says French). Wolfe's essay was titled "The 'Me' Decade and the Third Great Awakening" (1976).

### 4. The three core features

1. **A positive, inflated self-view:** special, unique, better and more attractive than others; entitlement and grandiosity.
2. **Relatively little warmth:** narcissists *can* take another person's point of view; they know what you're thinking. But the emotional caring is weak: "I can see you're in pain. I just don't care that much." They like company, especially admiring or high-status company, but not sacrificing for others.
3. **Strategies to keep the image up:** "In my mind, I'm a 10." In reality, a 6.5. That gap is an illusion that has to be maintained, and since other people matter less, they can be used to maintain it. Surround yourself with flatterers. Gain power, since people treat the powerful like 10s ("Washington, DC, is Hollywood for ugly people"). Obsess over appearance. Perform greatness on social media. Fantasize. Or attack whoever says you're a 6.5.

The DSM's definition fits exactly: "a pervasive pattern of grandiosity, need for admiration, and lack of empathy."

> ✅ DSM-5 wording accurate. 🟢 Narcissism tied to intact cognitive but reduced affective empathy is supported (e.g., Baskin-Sommers et al., 2014).

### 5. Grandiose narcissism: the offensive face

This is what most people mean by "narcissist": the charming ex, the politician, the boss, the celebrity. Bold, assertive, confident, charming, outgoing. Likable at first, with entitlement and hostility that show up when something blocks them. It moves through life *on offense*, looking for chances to look good ("Want to be on TV?" "Of course"), not on defense.

Campbell's example is the film character Doctor Strange, a surgeon whose whole sense of meaning collapses when his hands are injured, because his identity rested entirely on status and performance. An ordinary person would think, "At least my kids still like me." Words grandiose people use to describe themselves: glorious, acclaimed, high-status, prestigious, powerful, prominent. It is "agency" without vulnerability. Grandiose types can do more damage because they are likable: "It's like they have a megaphone all the time."

> 🟢 Grandiose narcissism correlates with extraversion and low agreeableness and predicts positive first impressions that fade (Paulhus, 1998).

### 6. Vulnerable narcissism: the defensive face

It is harder to see; clinicians notice it more. A client who seems depressed but, under the surface, believes he should be important and blames others for not recognizing it. Neurotic, anxious, self-focused, defensive, always asking how each situation affects *me*.

Campbell's example: a young man on a couch eating a cheeseburger who solved the final *Jeopardy!* question and shouted at the TV, "That prize should be mine," though he had never applied. In therapy: "I'm depressed because people don't see what a genius I am." "What have you done?" "I haven't manifested my genius yet." Words they use about themselves: underappreciated, insecure, ashamed, envious, resentful, self-absorbed. It does less damage to others but causes more suffering inside.

Many writers found the same split on their own over 80–90 years and gave it other names: overt/covert, thick-skinned/thin-skinned, oblivious/hypervigilant, exhibitionistic/closet, arrogant/shy. When people reach the same finding independently, that is what confirms it.

Note: Campbell uses "narcissist" as shorthand. These are dimensions, not types.

> 🟢 The grandiose/vulnerable distinction is well established (Wink, 1991; Miller et al., 2011).

### 7. Can you measure it? Yes, because people admit it

Asking people works because narcissism is *ego-syntonic*: people value it, so they report it. A psychopathy researcher's subjects would say, "I look out for number one, and you're an idiot not to." But self-report works only when little is at stake. In hiring or legal settings, scores can be gamed, so add reports from a spouse, boss or children, and (in clinics) structured interviews, which line up well with self-report.

- **SINS (Single Item Narcissism Scale):** "I am a narcissist (egotistical, self-focused, vain)." Works reasonably well.
- **NPI (grandiose):** "I like to be the center of attention." "I think I am a special person." "I like to look at myself in the mirror." "If I ruled the world it would be a much better place." Most young adults agree with about a third; the problem is agreeing with all of them at once: Napoleon on a horse.
- **HSNS (vulnerable):** "My feelings are easily hurt by ridicule." "I am secretly 'put out' when other people come to me with their troubles." "I dislike being with a group unless I know I am appreciated by at least one of those present."
- Also the Five-Factor Narcissism Inventory and the German NARC (admiration vs. rivalry).

> ✅ SINS (Konrath et al., 2014), NPI (Raskin & Hall), HSNS (Hendin & Cheek, 1997), NARC (Back et al., 2013): all real, validated instruments.

### 8. Who is narcissistic?

Men more than women. The difference is modest for grandiose narcissism (a correlation of about 0.2), small for vulnerable, and larger for NPD diagnoses (perhaps two-thirds men), though some of that may be clinician bias (men labeled narcissistic, women borderline or histrionic). It is highest in youth, especially young men, when promoting yourself helps launch a life, and it declines with age. "It's hard to be narcissistic when you're old."

Where it comes from: about 40–50% heritable. There is no single "narcissism gene," just many genes with small effects. Parenting within the normal range explains maybe 10–20%: cold, harsh, conflict-filled parenting goes with vulnerable narcissism; permissive parenting that puts the child on a pedestal goes with grandiose. Parents learn this with their second child: "Oh my god, it's just genetics." The rest is environment that siblings don't share and that can't be predicted, which is why one sibling may be narcissistic and the others not.

> 🟢 Sex difference: Grijalva et al. (2015) meta-analysis. 🟢 Age decline and heritability (~40–60%) are well supported. 🟡 Parenting percentages are rough estimates.

### 9. Variants: collective and communal

**Collective narcissism** applies grandiosity to one's group: "My group deserves special treatment." "I will never be satisfied until my group gets the recognition it deserves." It predicts conflict between groups and interests political scientists: "a form of toxic patriotism."

**Communal narcissism** (Jochen Gebauer) inflates oneself in areas of *helping others*: "I am the most helpful person I know." "I will bring peace and justice to the world." "I will be the best parent on this planet." It is competitive niceness, like sabotaging someone's better gift. It is less toxic than the self-promoting kind.

> ✅ Golec de Zavala et al. (2009) collective narcissism; Gebauer et al. (2012) communal narcissism.

### 10. The neighbors: self-esteem, entitlement, the Dark Triad

**Self-esteem** is simply liking yourself (Rosenberg: "I feel that I'm a person of worth, at least on an equal plane with others"). It is more modest and more social. Narcissism is not just very high self-esteem, nor is it secretly low self-esteem; research doesn't find the hidden self-hatred.

**Entitlement** is one part of narcissism: "If I were on the Titanic, I would deserve to be on the first lifeboat." Entitled people lack the outgoing side, so they're less fun than plain narcissists.

**The Dark Triad** shares antagonism: Machiavellianism (manipulation: "It's wise to keep track of information you can use against people later"; the power behind the throne), grandiose narcissism, and psychopathy (impulsive and callous: "Payback needs to be quick and nasty").

Other triads: a *vulnerable* dark triad (vulnerable narcissism, borderline traits, neurotic "Factor 2" psychopathy); an *energized* triad (grandiose narcissism, boldness, hypomania); and Scott Barry Kaufman's *Light Triad*: humanism (valuing others), faith in humanity, and Kantianism (honesty over charm).

> ✅ Dark Triad: Paulhus & Williams (2002); Light Triad: Kaufman et al. (2019); Psychological Entitlement Scale: Campbell et al. (2004). 🟢 Hidden low self-esteem ("mask model") is not supported by most data.

## Key Corrections

- **Ovid details.** The goddess who answers the spurned lover's prayer is Nemesis, not Zeus, and the narcissus is a daffodil-family flower, not a day lily. ✅
- **The 1850s clinical description.** Its author, Joseph Guislain, was Belgian, not French. ✅
- **Tom Wolfe's essay.** Its title is "The 'Me' Decade and the Third Great Awakening" (1976). ✅
- **NPD is about two-thirds men.** Clinical samples vary widely; part of the gap may be diagnostic bias, as Campbell himself notes. 🟡
- **Parenting explains 10–20%.** A rough estimate; studies on parenting and narcissism mostly rely on adults' memories of childhood and disagree on size. 🟡
- **No hidden low self-esteem.** True for grandiose narcissism in most data, but clinicians in the Kernberg and Kohut traditions still hold that a fragile core exists in some patients, and vulnerable narcissism does go with low self-esteem. 🟡

## Meditations

- In my own mind I am a 10; the gap between that and the truth is where the work begins.
- One can see another's pain clearly and still not care. Seeing is not loving.
- Narcissus died of self-love, but Echo died too.

## Socratic Question

"If narcissism and healthy self-esteem both involve thinking well of oneself, is the real difference in the self-image itself, or in how one treats others in order to protect it?"
