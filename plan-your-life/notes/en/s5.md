# How to Plan Your Life — S5 — Faith in Tragedy

**Source:** Jordan Peterson, *How to Plan Your Life*, Session 5: "Faith in Tragedy" (six-hour course) · [Peterson Academy](https://petersonacademy.com/courses/how-to-plan-your-life)

## Pocket Version

Every life will meet undeserved suffering and real evil, including the evil inside oneself, which is why the Passion and the Book of Job sit at the root of the culture. Admitting you would probably have joined the crowd makes you serious and formidable instead of naive. Fear of your own worst path is not enough, though: you also need Job's double faith, in your own worth and in the goodness of life. Meaning, love and real relationships make the struggle worth it, and a true adventure crowds out false ones like drink.

## The Insight

Every life will meet undeserved suffering and real evil, including the evil in yourself. The Passion of Christ and the Book of Job sit at the root of our culture because everyone has to face both. Facing your own capacity for evil makes you serious and strong instead of naive.

But fear is not enough. You also have to give yourself the benefit of the doubt and build a vision worth the struggle. Meaning is what makes the suffering worth it, and you find it in brave trust, in the people and loves that held you up at your worst, and in a real adventure big enough to crowd out the false ones.

## Why It Matters

It confronts both the cynic and the person who hates himself. It insists that you are capable of great evil *and* that you have worth: Job's double faith, in himself and in God. It explains why addiction is cured more by a better spirit than by willpower. And it shows identity as something worked out and lived with others, not simply declared.

## Key Points

### 1. The ultimate tragedy at the base of our culture

Jung called the Passion an archetypal tragedy, like Job. A tragedy isn't just something terrible happening; when it happens to a criminal, that's justice. Tragedy is the terrible thing happening unjustly, and the ultimate tragedy is the worst thing happening to the one who least deserves it. That story sits at the foundation because everyone will suffer undeservedly at some point, or watch their children suffer, which is worse.

Job: God says Job is upright. Satan says Job is only lucky: strip him of everything and he'll curse God. God allows the test, and everything terrible happens to Job.

> ✅ Job 1:8–12 accurate. 🔴 The archetypal reading is Jung's and Peterson's.

### 2. The descent into hell: evil is worse than death

Christian tradition holds that after the crucifixion Christ descended into hell. Psychologically, every person must confront not only tragedy but evil.

Soldiers with PTSD are often traumatized less by what was done to them than by what they did. People can bear a great deal of undeserved suffering; betrayal by someone evil, especially by themselves, is far harder. A four-year-old abused by an uncle isn't only suffering. The problem of evil in the human heart has been laid at that child's feet, and it shatters them. Perhaps by 30 they can spend a year working out the nature of evil and put it in perspective.

> ✅ Christ's descent into hell: Apostles' Creed; 1 Peter 3:19; CCC 631–637. 🟢 "Moral injury," trauma from what one has done, is a recognized and well-studied idea.

### 3. *Ordinary Men*: you probably would have done it

Peterson had PTSD clients read accounts of great evil. Christopher Browning's *Ordinary Men* follows Reserve Police Battalion 101: middle-aged German policemen who grew up before Nazi propaganda and were sent to Poland. Their commander offered to let anyone opt out of the killing without penalty. A few did, without consequences. Those who stepped out felt they were leaving their comrades to do the dirty work, so loyalty, a virtue, pulled men in. Step by step they went from rounding up men to shooting women and children in the forests. Many turned to drink; some came to enjoy it.

People always imagine they'd be Oskar Schindler. Peterson: "I don't think so." Rescuers were rare. His lesson, learned from Jung: if you fear such evil returning, the first step to not taking part is realizing you probably would. That calls for deep changes in your character. Ask yourself "in which handbasket would you ride to which particular hell?" This is what Jung called integrating the shadow, owning the dark side of yourself.

> ✅ Browning (1992), Battalion 101 and Major Trapp's opt-out offer are accurate. 🟢 Browning estimates only about 10–20% refused or avoided the killing. 🔴 Peterson's comparison to Covid-era compliance is his political opinion.

### 4. From dangerous to formidable

If you're more capable of evil than you think, you're also more capable, period. Many people can't stand up for themselves. Bringing the vengeful, resentful part of you under control ("getting Satan behind you") puts anger's force at your fingertips, so a hand raised to stop you won't succeed. "You're not going to be dangerous till you know you can be. And if you know you can be… then you're formidable." Respect yourself the way you'd respect dynamite.

> 🔴 Jungian interpretation. Catholic parallel: anger rightly ordered serves justice (Aquinas, *Summa* II-II q.158).

### 5. One man who stops lying

Solzhenitsyn: one man who stops lying can bring down a totalitarian state (Peterson's paraphrase). If you live in a small tyranny, maybe the job you hate, how much are you lying to keep it going? If you were straighter, things around you might be less crooked. That responsibility is what makes us free persons with real worth. Solzhenitsyn's own conclusion: they'll probably kill you, perhaps because you started telling the truth too late. Better to risk death than live in hell.

> ⚠️ Not a direct quote. Solzhenitsyn's essay "Live Not by Lies" (1974) argues that refusing to take part in lies is the key to defeating the regime, and his Nobel lecture says "one word of truth outweighs the whole world." Peterson's line is a paraphrase.

### 6. Cain and Abel: full versus half-hearted offering

Placed at the start of human history, the story shows two patterns. Abel offers sacrifices pleasing to God: full commitment. You know when you're giving everything and when you're 60% in, late, coasting, maybe cynical about the system "so that you can be useless with a good conscience." What you're proud of, looking back, is where you truly gave yourself.

Cain offers second-rate sacrifices. The text doesn't say why, because your reasons are your own. At root it's pride: like a child sure he can fool his father, you act as if you can bend reality your way without it snapping back.

In therapy, untangling a life that went bad usually leads back to a decision the person *knew* was wrong at the time and then forgot. Five years down the wrong path is five years building "the wrong Tower of Babel."

> ✅ Genesis 4:3–7 accurate. 🔴 Interpretation.

### 7. Fear isn't enough: you need a picture of heaven

Spend 15 minutes imagining how grim your life could get if you kept up your foolish habits, until you think, "I don't think I'm going there." Later, when tempted, remember it.

But fear alone is a weak weapon. Use "ask, seek, knock" again and give yourself the benefit of the doubt, like the presumption of innocence in law. Assume it would be fine for things to work out for you. That's hard: many people carry a deep sense of guilt and feel they deserve punishment. Flawed as you are, you might still be worthy.

> 🟢 Picturing the goal *and* the obstacles together (Gabriele Oettingen's "mental contrasting") works better than either one alone.

### 8. Job's two faiths

Job holds on to two things.

**Faith in his own worth.** His friends insist he suffers because he's wicked. Job admits he has done wrong like anyone, but his suffering doesn't cancel his worth. At your lowest, the temptation is to conclude you shouldn't exist, sometimes literally, through suicide. His wife says, "Curse God and die" (Job 2:9). We know Job is good because God says so at the start. You'll be punished for your wrongs, and you'll also suffer for no reason at all, sometimes because of your innocence. Lose faith in yourself and you add to the suffering.

**Faith in God and in life itself.** He refuses to conclude that life is meaningless and evil. In practice: struck with cancer, you can turn bitter and drive everyone away ("maybe you've got 10 months left, buddy. How are you going to spend them?"), or do the incredibly hard thing and look for what you can still be grateful for.

> ✅ Job 2:9; 13:15; 27:5–6 support this reading. 🟢 Hope and gratitude are linked to better outcomes in serious illness.

### 9. Treat yourself like someone you love

Many people aren't selfish but the opposite; at least half value others above themselves. Agreeable mothers fight fiercely for their children but not for themselves. Picture someone you truly care about (a sibling, child, parent or friend) and see yourself through that lens. If they have value, so do you. Stand up for yourself, not out of vanity, but with the worth you'd grant another person.

Clients bullied at work by skilled manipulators, who blew up one bad moment into "proof" they were unstable in order to steal their clients, had to learn to argue for their own sanity and innocence. If pushed, how would you defend yourself? What is good about you?

> 🔴 Clinical advice. 🟢 Self-compassion research supports "treat yourself as you'd treat a friend." Catholic link: "love your neighbor *as yourself*" (Mt 22:39) assumes a proper love of self (Aquinas, *Summa* II-II q.25 a.4).

### 10. Brave trust: the strategy that wins

Meet others as possible allies, not with suspicion, even knowing people are "full of snakes," yourself included. That's courage, not naivety. Naivety says you're wonderful; cynicism says everyone's a snake; courage says we both are, but let's walk uphill together. Done well, it makes a good response more likely. In computer tournaments of repeated games, the winning strategy was: cooperate first, punish cheating in proportion, then cooperate again.

The puppy that growls gets ignored and kicked, and learns that growling was right. The one that runs up wagging meets the odd cruel person, but mostly gets petted. First impressions set the tone. This is a religious practice: loving your neighbor, even your enemy. It's naive only if you don't see the danger. Apply it to yourself too.

> ✅ Robert Axelrod's tournaments (1980s): "Tit-for-Tat" won, and more forgiving versions do well when mistakes happen. 🟡 "No strategy beats it" overstates it; it depends on the setting.

### 11. Meaning is what makes the struggle worth it

You'll struggle, and you'll die, perhaps not gently. What could make that worth it? Look back at something hard you accomplished that felt worth the effort: "meaning is what justifies the struggle."

Love is the clue. You're glad your vulnerable children exist; you grieve the dead because their lives had worth despite their troubles. Turn that toward yourself: how would your life have to go so you could carry its weight without becoming bitter?

Find what's real by asking what held you up in your worst times. Peterson interviewed a man who nearly committed a school shooting as a teenager: a violent, addicted mother, two violent stepfathers, living alone in a field at 15, turned away by a mental-health clinic. What saved him was his love of books, and one friend who stuck by him and threw him a surprise birthday party that "shifted him back into the human camp." The pattern: interests, duty, responsibility to others, and above all relationships. During Peterson's own long illness, family and friends got him through. If you hit hard times with broken relationships, there may be nothing to hold you up.

> 🟢 Social support is among the strongest predictors of resilience and survival (Julianne Holt-Lunstad's meta-analyses).

### 12. False adventure and the better spirit

If you're drawn to drugs, affairs or rule-breaking, it's partly because flirting with disaster feels like adventure. If your life isn't demanding enough to satisfy the part of you that wants trouble, your vision has too little risk in it.

Abraham lived in comfort to age 75, "an overgrown infant," until the voice told him to leave and go out into the world. Then came famine, war, and Egypt's ruler taking his wife. Yet the story concludes it was better. Bigger dragon, bigger treasure.

Wild partying is false adventure. It's better than lying depressed on the couch, but it's a substitute. Proof: religious conversion reliably cures alcoholism. Physical dependence ends within a week or two of stopping (sometimes needing a hospital); relapse comes from craving. Alcohol is literally called "spirits," a false, Dionysian spirit. To escape its grip, you need a better spirit.

The perfect movie is a romantic adventure; maybe that's what your life could be. Raise the stakes until it's worth the ultimate sacrifice, which you'll make anyway. Since you'll lose everything regardless, "taking a risk on what's worthwhile, that's not a risk. Not taking a risk on what's worthwhile, that's a risk."

> ✅ Abraham was 75 (Gen 12:4); the episode with his wife is Gen 12:10–20. 🟢 Jung's 1961 letter to Bill Wilson, founder of Alcoholics Anonymous, used the phrase *spiritus contra spiritum* (spirit against spirits) and helped shape AA. 🟢 A Cochrane review (2020) found AA as effective as, or more than, other treatments for staying sober. 🟢 Acute withdrawal usually resolves within about a week.

### 13. Identity is worked out with others, not declared

Peterson criticizes the claim that I define my identity however I like and everyone must go along, especially when that identity is reduced to momentary sexual desire. That's the two-year-old's game: "I want what I want right now, or else." By three you have to play with others, so identity is negotiated all the time. Married? "It's about me" won't last.

Your real identities (husband, father, brother, uncle) are social, lived out, and they scale up: you, spouse, family, neighborhood, town, state, nation, "one nation under God." Mental health (which is never only mental) is harmony across these levels. The more responsibility you take on across them, the stronger your identity and the more meaning in your life, meaning tied to sacrifice you choose. In marriage, it's not about you or your wife but about the marriage; both serve that unity.

Children size each other up: "Can you take turns?" Those who never learn by four become outcasts, sometimes career criminals, who often grow up only around 30.

> 🔴 The critique of sexual identity is a contested cultural position. 🟢 Crime peaks in adolescence and declines through the twenties; this is well established. Catholic link: "the two become one flesh" (Gen 2:24; Eph 5:31), marriage as a third reality both spouses serve.

## Key Corrections

- **"Religious conversion reliably cures alcoholism."** Overstated. Spiritual change and AA participation help many people, and a 2020 Cochrane review supports AA, but conversion is not a reliable cure and relapse after it is common. 🟡
- **Solzhenitsyn's "one man who stops lying."** A paraphrase, not a quotation; the source ideas are "Live Not by Lies" (1974) and the Nobel lecture. ⚠️
- **Covid-era compliance compared with Battalion 101.** Political opinion; the analogy between public-health rules and taking part in mass murder is strongly disputed. 🔴
- **Axelrod's tournaments.** Tit-for-Tat won the original contests, but no strategy wins in every setting; forgiving variants do better when errors occur. 🟡
- **"At least half of people value others above themselves."** No source given; treat it as a clinical impression. 🔴
- **Identity critique.** His view that identity is socially negotiated rather than self-declared is a contested cultural position; supporters of self-identification argue it protects people from coercion. 🟡

## Meditations

- I would probably have stayed in the battalion. That is where self-knowledge starts.
- Flawed as I am, I may still be worthy.
- Not taking a risk on what's worthwhile: that's the risk.
