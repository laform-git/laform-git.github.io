# How to Plan Your Life — S2 — The Walled Garden

**Source:** Jordan Peterson, *How to Plan Your Life*, Session 2: "The Walled Garden" (six-hour course) · [Peterson Academy](https://petersonacademy.com/courses/how-to-plan-your-life)

## Pocket Version

It does not matter which fear you face first, because every fear leads to the same dragon, and facing one on purpose makes you braver in general, not just less afraid. Telling the truth about the past shrinks it into something usable. Since the unknown is vast, people survive by building walls (rules, vows, schedules, limited choices) around a space small enough to master. Within those walls, the aim with others is invitation into a shared game, never force.

## The Insight

It doesn't matter where you face chaos, only that you do. Every entry point leads to the same dragon, and facing one small fear on purpose makes you braver everywhere. Because what you don't know is far bigger than what you know, you survive by building walls: boundaries around a space small enough to master. Paradise is a walled garden, order with some living chaos inside. Inside those walls, the goal with other people is neither compromise nor force but invitation: a shared game both people want to play.

## Why It Matters

It recasts discipline, rules, vows and schedules as walls that make life possible, not as tyranny. It challenges both the man who rushes to fix things and the person who avoids conflict to keep the peace. And it sets a high bar for marriage and business: if you need force or manipulation to get agreement, you won't reach where you're going.

## Key Points

### 1. Every entry point leads to the same dragon

The knights of the Holy Grail sit at a round table, as equals, and each enters the forest "at the point that looks darkest to him." The Grail holds the water that revives: something of infinite worth whose location no one knows.

The lesson: it doesn't matter which fear you face first. That should ease the shame of how small your first step is. Wherever you meet the dragon of chaos, it is the same dragon.

> 🔴 Mythological reading.

### 2. Telling the truth shrinks the story

Therapy works partly by getting people to tell the truth, which takes trust and is itself a step into the unknown. The first telling of a painful story sprawls and drags in every terrible thing. Each retelling gets shorter and more focused, until the whole mess becomes a conclusion: "a marker that you can now use to step forward." Getting your story straight is your vision of past, present and future.

> 🟢 Putting painful experience into words and facing it are core mechanisms of trauma therapy.

### 3. Let people talk before you fix

Peterson says women are, on average, more sensitive to threat and often sense something is wrong before they know what. A man's instinct is "tell me the problem so I can fix it," but "there's no sense fixing the wrong problem." Like a good doctor, let the person talk until they reach the point. It is usually smaller than the whole list of complaints, and fixable.

Do the same with yourself. Thought is "a secular form of prayer." Ask "what is bothering me?" and truly want the answer. The first fantasies may be angry; keep circling. You know you've hit the core when you don't like the answer ("Really? That?"), and admitting it is the humility you need to move on.

> 🟢 Average sex differences in negative emotion are well replicated. 🔴 The application is his.

### 4. Facing fear makes you braver, not just less afraid

The person with agoraphobia isn't really afraid of the elevator. Underneath is a fantasy: my heart races and I die in front of strangers. That combines physical death and public humiliation, the two deepest fears. One client, at the open elevator doors, said: "That's a tomb." A serious obstacle stands in for your vulnerability itself.

The downside: small things can stop you. The upside: facing small things on purpose confronts the whole fear. The surprising clinical finding is that people "don't become less afraid. They become braver." Less afraid would mean the thing was less threatening; braver means there is more to you than you knew, and you take that everywhere.

A side effect: women treated for anxiety often argued more with their husbands afterward, because they were braver and less dependent. Some husbands had quietly discouraged their progress ("No, dear, you don't have to do that"), care that was really sabotage.

> 🟢 Exposure therapy, and its spread into general confidence, are well supported. 🔴 The marriage anecdote is clinical observation.

### 5. Don't steal people's destinies

A rule from elder care: never do for someone what they can do for themselves, because "you're stealing from them." As a therapist: your problems are yours; I help you solve them so you get the gift of having solved them. To a mother wondering why she should let her kids struggle: "so you don't steal their destinies from them." Too much care can smother.

> 🟢 Care that supports independence is linked to better outcomes. 🔴 The framing is his.

### 6. A new vision rewrites your past

If you learn how not to be bullied, your story changes. It stops being "innocent victim of psychopathic narcissists" (maybe 50% true) and starts including what you brought to it. The past is "less fixed than you think." That means taking on more responsibility, which is heavy, but it clears the way forward. This is the logic of confession and repentance in the Judeo-Christian tradition: bring your faults to mind so you can stop. As a teenager, Peterson thought the minister overstated human sinfulness; later he saw the point.

When a spouse criticizes you, maybe they're a tyrant; maybe they have a point. Ask exactly what the problem is, whose it is, and how. "Any corrective truth is offensive"; it requires a part of you to die. But it is easier to sort yourself out voluntarily than to live out your foolishness. No progress without "a bit of death and resurrection."

> 🔴 Interpretation. It connects naturally to the Catholic examination of conscience.

### 7. Rules make things possible; discipline is integration

Why may you interrupt a child's fun? Because a child who annoys everyone meets disapproval, neglect and rejection everywhere: no friends, no adults willing to teach. Two parents together make "one half-sane person"; if both are annoyed, the world probably will be too.

About 5% of boys are highly aggressive at age two. Left unsocialized, they head toward unpopularity and possibly crime. Most can be socialized by four, not by crushing their aggression but by channeling it into fair competition, like a fierce athlete who plays by the rules. Rules are "enabling principles for more sophisticated games."

Same for you: if your schedule feels like a burden, you planned it badly. Plan the best day you'd want: when you get up, how, what you eat for breakfast. Don't assume you already know who you are. Ask yourself, the way you'd ask your wife what her ideal day would be, and listen.

> 🟢 Richard Tremblay's research: physical aggression peaks around age two, and most children are socialized by school age.

### 8. Saying what you want takes faith and courage

If you have a habit of betraying yourself, you won't let yourself know what you want. Couples get stuck when neither can please the other because neither will say what would please them; saying it gives the other the power to refuse it. If you've been betrayed, that trust is hard. "This is why you need some faith and some courage. And those two things are very, very similar." Revealing your needs is the same as facing a fear: showing more of your vulnerability, step by step.

> 🔴 Interpretation.

### 9. Sort out the past to lower stress now

Your brain keeps a tally of how much of your past went wrong. The more went wrong, the more dangerous it assumes the world is, and the more reactive you become. Sorting out the past lowers that reactivity.

With a memory that haunts you, think about it on purpose, say for 10 minutes before sleep. That tells your mind you're big enough to face it. The surrounding fantasies unfold, and now that you're older, you can see what you might have done differently. Resentment, bitterness and anxiety are doorways in. This isn't exactly forgiveness (which needs the other person to change); it is putting down the load by learning to handle such situations in the future.

> 🟢 Deliberately revisiting a memory (imaginal exposure) is evidence-based for PTSD. 🟡 The "brain tally" is a simplification.

### 10. Count the cost of not acting

Afraid of a harsh boss who reminds you of your father, or a fifth-grade bully? Ask what happens if you do nothing: he grows harsher, you shrink, for 30 years. "How afraid are you of not confronting him?" Same with a marriage problem: "You want to live with this problem every week for the next five decades?" Peterson dislikes conflict, but he dislikes "the same stupid fight forever" more.

How to do it: rehearse. Go in telling the truth, not hostile: "I'm afraid to come to work. Maybe it's me; tell me what I'm doing wrong. If not, is it something between us?" Schedule it, and prepare for the worst; you probably can't have a hard conversation with your boss unless you're prepared to lose the job. Often it's a misunderstanding. Sometimes the harsh boss becomes your best ally because he tells you the truth. Sometimes you have to leave. "You don't think you can get another job you hate? You probably can."

> 🔴 Clinical advice.

### 11. The walled garden

What you don't know far outweighs what you know. You can't really explain your car, your computer or a plane. So how do you act at all? You frame the world: you build walls. Children push to find where the walls are, because they don't want to be dropped in the ocean and told to swim anywhere.

Carcassonne, the medieval walled city in France, has walls within walls and houses with walls inside. That is paradise: Eden, the well-watered walled garden, order with something alive and changing inside.

Limited choice helps. Don't offer a three-year-old 100 outfits; offer two or three. Fewer shampoos make it easier to pick a good one, and an overstuffed toy box can stop play.

A professor's bold ideas rest on invisible walls: a free society, an economy that pays for it, the university, its staff, an office. Be grateful for what protects you. Hobbes saw life without structure as "nasty, brutish, and short"; Rousseau saw structure corrupting our natural goodness. Peterson: corrupt power does harm, but structure itself is necessary. Your mind is a walled city of assumptions.

> ✅ Carcassonne and the Hobbes quote are accurate. 🟡 Research on "too much choice" is mixed.

### 12. Vows are walls; betrayal is hell

Your spouse's faithfulness is a wall you rely on. It keeps out the chaos of endless romantic entanglement, which is part of why we marry. "Find someone foolish enough to take you and offer them the same favor."

Break that wall and everything floods in. Betrayal wipes out not only the present and future but the past: "Did we ever have anything?" Dante put traitors at the bottom of hell. Betraying someone also shows you to yourself as unreliable; sometimes a marriage ends because the betrayer can't forgive himself.

> ✅ Dante places traitors in the lowest circle of hell (*Inferno*, Cantos 32–34).

### 13. Wall off a small enough space: the 15-minute book

Wall off a space small enough that its chaos looks like opportunity, not threat. Want to write a book? Find and protect 15 minutes a day. A paragraph a day is about a page a week, a book in a year. Can't do that? Lower the bar: 200 words on anything, not good, not even grammatical. By day 200 you'll know what you're writing about: "you write to discover what it is that you have to say."

Planning a step small enough that you'll actually take it is the same as learning to love yourself. "A fear is infinitely fractionable": it can always be cut smaller.

His three-year-old son, at a T. rex skeleton, stood about 200 feet away, at the edge, watching. Stand at the edge and pay attention; your nervous system calms; then you step closer. That edge is play. Heroes often run the first time they see the dragon, as Pinocchio did from the whale Monstro.

> 🟢 Gradual exposure and getting used to a fear (habituation) are well supported.

### 14. Marriage: a worthy partner, not compromise or force

Peterson reads the biblical Eve as a "beneficial adversary": a partner who pushes you, like a slightly better basketball opponent. Most people don't want to marry a doormat.

Compromise is what you settle for when you aren't creative enough to find the best solution. Aim instead for a shared game better than either of you could play alone. Ask: if coming home went perfectly, what would it look like, in detail? Food, 10 minutes to unwind, a hug, 15 to 20 minutes of talk, not a list of problems at the door. Try it for a week, imperfectly. Therapists call this "collaborative empiricism": try something together, then look at what happened.

Small steps matter even for the isolated. Teaching a lonely client to shake hands, say his name and introduce himself to the corner-store cashier can save a life; one person who smiles at you is more than zero. Couples need about 90 minutes a week on the practical side of life, or "you'll end up taking the time in divorce court." Even small repeated things, like how the dishes get put away, deserve a solution you both like.

Moses (Numbers 20) is told to speak to the rock to bring out water. Instead he strikes it twice with his staff, and he is barred from the Promised Land. Using force when you could have used invitation keeps you from your destination. If you need force with children, you let things go too far. With a spouse or business partner, force or manipulation means you won't get where you're going. Aim for the deal the other person is thrilled with.

> 🟡 The name Eve (*Chavah*) relates to "living" (Gen 3:20). Genesis 2:18 calls the woman *ezer kenegdo*, "a helper corresponding to him"; "beneficial adversary" is Peterson's reading of *kenegdo*, not the meaning of the name. ✅ Numbers 20:7–12 confirmed. ✅ "Collaborative empiricism" is a standard term in cognitive behavioral therapy.

## Key Corrections

- **Eve as "beneficial adversary."** The Hebrew *ezer kenegdo* (Gen 2:18) means a helper "corresponding to" or "fit for" him; "adversary" stretches *kenegdo* and is Peterson's reading, not standard translation. 🔴
- **Too much choice.** The famous jam study has not replicated reliably; meta-analyses find choice overload only under specific conditions. Limiting a toddler's options is sound practice, but the general claim is weaker than presented. 🟡
- **The brain's "tally" of past failures.** A simplification. The real mechanisms (fear conditioning, threat sensitization, prior expectations) are more specific and not a single running count. 🟡
- **Husbands sabotaging anxious wives' progress.** Clinical observation only; family accommodation of anxiety is documented, but not as a pattern specific to husbands. 🔴
- **Rousseau.** Summarized fairly at the level of a slogan; Rousseau also argued for a social contract and the general will, so he did not reject all structure. 🟡
