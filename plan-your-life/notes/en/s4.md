# How to Plan Your Life — S4 — Fear as a Catalyst

**Source:** Jordan Peterson, *How to Plan Your Life*, Session 4: "Fear as a Catalyst" (six-hour course) · [Peterson Academy](https://petersonacademy.com/courses/how-to-plan-your-life)

## Pocket Version

How much you want something shows in what you will sacrifice for it, because humans live in a bargain with the future. Reward is the best motivator, but fear helps when it is placed behind you, pushing, rather than in front of you, stopping you. So picture in vivid detail both the heaven you could build and the particular hell your own weaknesses would lead to. Then think the way one prays: admit you do not know, ask honestly, and accept whatever has to be given up.

## The Insight

The course now turns from vision to plan, from theory to practice. You know how much you want something by what you will give up for it; we live in a bargain with the future. Reward is the best motivator, but fear is powerful too. Put fear **behind** you, pushing you on, not **in front** of you, stopping you.

To do that, picture two futures in vivid detail: the heaven you could build, and the particular hell your own weaknesses would lead you to. Then ask honestly for guidance, ready to give up whatever has to go.

## Why It Matters

It challenges the self-help habit of only thinking positive: the worst version of yourself is a motivator you shouldn't waste. It treats the Gospel's "ask, seek, knock" as a real method of thinking and self-examination. And it rests on measured results: a 90-minute writing exercise that raised grades and cut dropout rates, most of all for students least likely to succeed.

## Key Points

### 1. From vision to plan: small things get meaning from the whole

The course works at different levels. Big ideas and vision sit at the top. They scale down to where they "touch the ground" as small, doable changes. Small changes are a nuisance, but they matter because they are tied to the whole. Seeing how the part connects to the whole is what gives small actions meaning. Broad ideas cover more situations; specific plans are what you can act on. Session 3 was vision and theory; this one is plan and practice.

> 🔴 Framework.

### 2. You measure wanting by sacrifice

Peterson praises the behaviorists (B. F. Skinner and those after him) for their precision. He calls Jeffrey Gray's *The Neuropsychology of Anxiety* (1982), which drew on behavior, brain anatomy and drug research, the most profound psychology book of the late 20th century. Gray was a student of Hans Eysenck.

The rat study: hungry rats are run toward food with a spring scale tied to the tail, measuring how hard and how long they pull. A well-fed rat won't pull. "I feel like I need this" means almost nothing. Ask instead: what will you give up, invest, sacrifice, work for? How much you want something is not how much you complain when you don't get it, but how much work you'll do to make it likely.

We're unusual among animals: we give up things now for a future we imagine. That is what work is. The Old Testament's emphasis on sacrifice expresses this: "You live in a contractual relationship with the future," even with the structure of reality itself, and you're supposed to hold up your end.

> ✅ Gray (1982) and his link to Eysenck are accurate. 🟢 Effort spent as a measure of motivation is standard behavioral science. 🔴 The theological reading is his.

### 3. Reward plus fear: the rat and the cat

Rats raised without ever seeing a cat still panic at the smell of one. A hungry rat runs fast toward food; waft cat smell behind it and it really moves. An athlete runs to win; release a grizzly behind him and he finds more speed.

Reward is the best kind of motivation. Running toward a goal brings hope, curiosity and enthusiasm, driven by the brain's dopamine system, the same system cocaine and most addictive drugs hijack to make you feel you're doing something worthwhile. But fear behind you plus a goal ahead makes you formidable.

> 🟢 Rats' inborn fear of cat smell and dopamine's role in reward are well established.

### 4. A ladder to heaven and a ladder to hell

Dante's heaven and hell are both built in levels, like the layers of identity from setting the table up to being a good person. There's a heavenly ladder (who you could be, at every level) and a hellish one (where all your foolish choices would lead). To be fully motivated, you need both pictures. Don't throw away negative emotion; it's a powerful engine.

> ✅ The *Divine Comedy*'s structure (nine circles of hell, nine heavenly spheres) is accurate. 🔴 The application is his.

### 5. Fear behind you, not in front of you

"It's better to have your fear behind you, pushing you forward, than your fear in front of you, stopping you."

Take a hated job or marriage. After 10 years, you fear facing it, because you'd have to go back through the levels, see why you wasted a decade, and discover that you betrayed yourself. That decade won't come back. Sometimes digging into a great mistake gives you the wisdom the decade should have given you ("a confession and a redemption and an atonement"), but it means facing everything you avoided. Why bother? Because the alternative is 20 more years. "You're picking your hell": one you walk through on purpose and repair, or one you stay in.

His clinical exercise: fine, don't change jobs. Now, how angry, resentful, bitter, arrogant and dishonest are you because of it? Ten years in, are you better or worse? Stronger or weaker? Are your family relationships damaged? Now look 15 years further ahead: older, smaller, less healthy, less able to fix it.

The bus: you've waited 30 minutes for a bus that should take five, so you wait two more hours. Cut your losses. Don't think about it abstractly: "bloody well think about it. Envision it." At 50, with 20 years of unresolved mess, what are the chances you start now? An artist friend told him some people's mess is bigger than the energy they have left to fix it. Barring a miracle, they're stuck.

> 🟢 The "sunk cost" trap (staying because of what you've already spent) is well documented. 🔴 Clinical advice.

### 6. Profit, price and fair exchange

About 30 years ago, Peterson founded a company with Daniel Higgins and Robert Pihl (his McGill supervisor) to produce psychological programs. Their rules:

- **Low cost but not free.** Someone always pays; it should be the user.
- **Self-sustaining.** If it can't stand on its own, why do it?
- **Listen to the market.** If nobody wants it, you designed or marketed it badly, or solved a problem nobody has.

Profit isn't a dirty word; without it you only work to keep working. Reinvest it, rather than spending it like the mobster's 10-year plan of "hookers and cocaine." Price is what the market will bear; the Soviet central pricing committee faced thousands of pricing decisions a day and couldn't keep up. Most people would rather trade than take charity. A fair, voluntary exchange leaves both sides even and makes societies productive and generous. With little bureaucracy it scales: about three people and a part-time assistant served around 150,000 users.

> 🔴 Business philosophy. 🟢 Prices as information is standard economics (Friedrich Hayek).

### 7. Self-Authoring and what it showed

The Self-Authoring Suite has three parts: write about your past, assess your present strengths and faults, then write a vision for your future. That matches the past–present–future map. Two lines of research back it: clinical (writing about past trauma improves physical and mental health) and management (setting goals improves performance).

Asking employees to write about their whole life, and how the job fits into it, works better than asking them to plan how to be good employees. A manager wants to know why working there serves *your* aims; then you'll work hard without supervision. If the job doesn't fit your vision: "Sayonara," better for both sides. Motivation forced from outside requires power, "standing over you with a stick." Children's pretend play shows the alternative: "Do you want to play house?" The other child has to want to play. Do the same with yourself.

The studies compared 90 minutes of writing about one's future with writing about the past two weeks:

- **McGill University:** grades rose (Peterson says about 35%) and dropout fell sharply.
- **Mohawk College** (done before enrollment, with no feedback): dropout fell about 50%.
- **Rotterdam School of Management:** similar results, with the biggest gains among students predicted to do worst. Non-white men eventually outperformed white women. Peterson's guess: men, being less agreeable on average, perform best when driven by their own purposes rather than the system's.

> ✅ James Pennebaker's research on writing about painful experience is real and replicated, with modest effects. 🟢 Morisano et al. (2010, McGill) and Schippers et al. (2015, Rotterdam) are published studies. 🟡 Effect sizes vary between studies, Peterson's figures are on the high side, and independent replication is limited.

### 8. Test your programs: unintended consequences

Most programs help the strong more than the weak (the Matthew principle). Good intentions don't guarantee good results, and side effects can outweigh the benefit. So it's an ethical rule: build in measurement, and try it small first.

His examples: the self-esteem movement, which he says increased anxiety and confused narcissism with self-esteem; and "whole-word" reading instruction, which ignored phonics and the alphabet (throwing away something as basic as "discovering the wheel"). He says California's reading scores fell from near the top to near the bottom.

> 🟢 That the self-esteem movement failed to improve outcomes is broadly accepted (Baumeister et al., 2003). 🟢 Phonics works better; this is well established. 🟡 The California ranking is approximate (1994 national scores placed California near the bottom).

### 9. Why schools won't use it

No university adopted the program, even where the studies were run. That told him they didn't care that half their students dropped out. Hillsdale College, under Larry Arnn, reports very low dropout. You'd think 15 years of schooling would include one afternoon writing seriously about your vision for your life. Peterson traces public schooling to the 19th-century Prussian model, designed to produce obedience and imported by industrialists to train farm children as factory workers.

> 🟡 The Prussian roots of compulsory schooling (via Horace Mann) are historical. 🔴 That it was designed to make "will-less soldiers" for "fascist industrialists" is Peterson's polemic. ⚠️ Hillsdale is in Michigan, not the Northeast, and the "1% dropout" figure is unverified.

### 10. Ask, seek, knock: thinking as a form of prayer

"Ask, and it will be given you; seek, and you will find; knock, and it will be opened to you" (Mt 7:7). What does real thinking require?

1. **Admit** you have a problem or don't know. That's humility, opening up.
2. **Wait.** An answer appears. From where? If you could produce it at will, why did you have the problem? Jung said thoughts appear like furniture in a room you walk into: you notice them, but don't know how they got there.
3. **Judge** the answer. You can gauge its truth by how much you wish it weren't so.

Peterson: "thought is literally secularized prayer." Approach the mystery "on your knees."

Dreams are early drafts of thoughts. Not, as Freud said, a disguise for what you've buried, but a first attempt at a solution, reshaping your view before you have words for it. Daydreams and novels are visions with a life of their own; novelists don't know what their characters will do. Jung called the deep source of these first solutions the "collective unconscious." His hypothesis was that what people call God is the concrete form of that independent source, an idea Peterson calls brilliant.

Try it. Close your eyes and ask: what one thing could be better in my life in a year, realistically? Let the image come. Ask as if "I'll accept any answer, no matter what I have to give up." If you never ask, the buried problem-solving can turn into a violent fantasy. Parts of you may have to die, "not just like fragments… whole parts of you that have to burn away."

> ✅ Mt 7:7 verified. 🔴 "Thought as prayer" and Jung's God hypothesis are interpretation. Catholic note: the Church teaches that prayer is addressed to a personal God and cannot be reduced to the unconscious (CCC 2558–2565).

### 11. Picture your particular hell

List the foolish things you know you do and could stop. Now imagine you stop resisting them and let them take you wherever they're heading. What does that look like in five years?

Each temperament has its own temptation. Narcissism (craving unearned attention and status) is predicted by being outgoing plus disagreeable, each about 50% inherited; not destiny, but a real tilt. In one study, sons of families with several generations of alcoholism had their heart rate rise roughly 30% after four drinks: alcohol hit them as a stimulant. "There's going to be a genetic proclivity to some domain of sin for you": temper, drugs, despair, appetite. Every temperament's strengths come with matching temptations; the charming person who is hard to exploit can easily exploit others.

Make it real: "maybe you're on crank in downtown Portland, bent over. That's you." In exposure therapy, the more vivid the imagined scene (measured by sweating), the faster the progress.

Don't think "I'd never do that." From his study of totalitarian regimes: those most sure they'd resist are often the most likely to go along. "It's probably you, buddy." Bitterness, betrayal, the wish for revenge, even against God, live in you. Those motives block and derail you, but harnessed behind you, they make you hard to stop. His own example: years ago he spoke out publicly because he feared losing control of his tongue more than he feared the university administrators. "You've got to know what to be afraid of," above all your own capacity for self-destruction.

> 🟢 Big Five personality traits are about 40–60% inherited; Peterson and Pihl's research on alcohol as a stimulant in sons of alcoholics is published. 🟢 Vivid imagery predicts better exposure-therapy outcomes. Catholic link: concupiscence and each person's "predominant fault," the classic examination of conscience.

## Key Corrections

- **Self-Authoring results.** Peterson co-created and sells the program, a conflict of interest he does not mention. The published studies are real, but his figures (about 35% higher grades, 50% less dropout) sit at the top end, and large independent replications are lacking. 🟡
- **Prussian schooling.** The Prussian model did influence Horace Mann and U.S. schooling. That it was designed to make obedient soldiers for "fascist industrialists" is polemic and anachronistic, since fascism postdates it by a century. 🔴
- **Hillsdale College.** It is in Michigan, and the near-zero dropout figure is unverified. ⚠️
- **California reading scores.** The 1994 national tests placed California near the bottom, but "top to bottom" overstates the earlier ranking, and whole-language teaching was one cause among several. 🟡
- **"Thought is secularized prayer."** A psychological analogy. In Catholic teaching, prayer is addressed to a personal God and is not reducible to problem-solving or the unconscious (CCC 2558–2565). 🔴
- **Gray as the most profound psychology book of its era.** Peterson's personal judgment, not a field consensus. 🔴
