# How to Plan Your Life — S3 — Desire and Discipline

**Source:** Jordan Peterson, *How to Plan Your Life*, Session 3: "Desire and Discipline" (six-hour course) · [Peterson Academy](https://petersonacademy.com/courses/how-to-plan-your-life)

## Pocket Version

Instincts are not blind drives but small goal-directed personalities that change what you see. Maturity means integrating them into a higher self that satisfies them together and with others, not suppressing them. Conscience calls you out when you fall short, and interest draws you toward what will transform you; neither is fully under your control. Good feeling comes from moving toward a goal, so pick one you can step toward and break failures down to the smallest broken piece.

## The Insight

Your instincts (hunger, thirst, anger, desire) are not blind drives. Each one acts like a small personality with its own goal, and it changes what you see. Maturity is not crushing them, as in Freud's picture of conscience at war with instinct. It is bringing them together into a higher self that satisfies them over time, together, and alongside other people.

Two guides you don't control pull you toward that whole self: **conscience**, which calls you out when you fall short, and **interest**, which calls you toward what will change you. Good feeling comes from moving toward a goal, not from reaching it. So you need a goal, held lightly, that you can take steps toward.

## Why It Matters

It overturns the idea that discipline means holding yourself back. It agrees with the classical view that the passions are to be ordered, not destroyed. It treats conscience as something with real authority over you, not mere social programming. And it gives a concrete method for fixing almost anything, from a child's table-setting to your own character: break the failure down until you find the smallest piece that is broken.

## Key Points

### 1. More walls: social rank, the body, the senses

Besides physical walls, we hold back chaos with social structures (agreed ways to compete and cooperate without falling apart) and with our bodies. You see only a sliver of what's around you, and never what's behind you.

Aldous Huxley (*The Doors of Perception*) described the overwhelming wonder around us that we normally filter out. Having a body limits perception to what we can handle: "it actually excludes more" than it includes. The senses sample the unknown; motivations and emotions are the built-in categories that sort it.

> ✅ Huxley's book and argument (borrowing Henri Bergson's idea of the brain as a "reducing valve") are described accurately. 🟢 Selective attention is basic science of perception.

### 2. Instincts act like small personalities

When you say "I'm hungry," no one asks what you mean. Hunger is universal, and shared instincts are what make us understandable to each other.

Hunger doesn't just push you. It takes over how you see: "your goal is now to find something to eat," and the whole world rearranges around that. Getting food may require real problem-solving, like sharing. So hunger is "a personality or a story." The Promised Land flows with "milk and honey," fat and sugar, because to hungry ancient people that was paradise.

There are two broad groups: self-maintenance (hot, cold, hungry, thirsty, tired) and social or reproductive (finding a mate, getting along). With a crying baby, the checklist (hot, cold, wet, hungry, tired) solves the problem 95% of the time. Kids get cranky about 15 minutes before the adults do; that's when to leave the party.

> 🟢 Strong motivational states shape attention and perception. 🔴 "Small personalities" is Peterson's framing.

### 3. The two-year-old and the 30-year-old

A two-year-old is a bundle of these states, cycling through pure rage, tears, hunger and joy. That intensity is delightful at two. A 30-year-old having a tantrum is terrifying; you want to call the police.

Instincts raise problems but don't solve them: being hungry is not eating. They also clash: too hungry to sleep, too tired to eat, hating someone you're attracted to. Maturity is integrating them so all are satisfied, over time, with each other, and alongside other people. Toilet training is the first example.

> 🟢 UK school reports have documented rising numbers of children starting school (around ages 4–5) not toilet-trained; Peterson's "six-year-olds" overstates the age. 🟡 Causes are debated; he blames broken families.

### 4. Integration, not suppression: where Freud went wrong

Freud described instinct (the "id," mostly sexual) meeting the "superego," the inner voice of social authority that says no. Peterson calls this "interestingly wrong." It leads back to the problem of force: control breeds rebellion, or resentful rule-followers who don't understand the rules. And you can do that to yourself. The better way is to offer a better game.

His son was a strong-tempered toddler (roughly 12 months to two years). He'd be sent to sit on the stairs "until you can act like a civilized human being that other people would like to have around." Fists and teeth clenched. "Are you ready?" "Not yet." He was learning to fold his rage into a social self. He grew into a competitive athlete; the aggression didn't disappear, it was raised to a higher level. A teenage athlete who plays fiercely, gives others credit and doesn't hog the glory is admired and attractive. All his lower drives are satisfied at once, in a way that helps others too.

In adult relationships, a man who can't manage his anger is dangerous. Peterson says women test men, consciously or not, to see whether irritation turns a man into a tyrant, who is "probably not the best person to have around children."

> 🔴 Interpretation. The integration model echoes the classical view (Aristotle, Aquinas) that virtue orders the passions rather than eliminating them.

### 5. Your ideal self shows up in daydreams

Imagine your life set up the way you'd want, with some wisdom: a partner who likes you, a family, getting along with parents and siblings, children who respect you, the friends you'd want, career, education. Answered honestly, those images are "the collective voice of your integrated instincts," describing who you could be if everything that needs satisfying were satisfied at once.

> 🔴 Framing; the later practical sessions build on it.

### 6. Conscience: the better self that calls you out

It's three in the morning, you've done something stupid, and your conscience visits. If it were simply "you," you could switch it off, but it doesn't listen. Peterson: it is the person you could be, calling you to account for falling short.

Jung described three layers:

- **Persona:** what you show others. It's needed for small talk; the bank teller's "How are you?" checks that you can play simple social games.
- **Ego:** who you think you are. It's only partial; your brain holds more possible patterns of connection than there are particles in the visible universe.
- **Self:** the whole person you could become, which speaks through conscience.

Conscience can malfunction: too harsh from your upbringing, or warped by your own lies. That's a reason not to lie. You program yourself falsely, and one day, when you badly need to know what's true, you won't be able to tell.

> ✅ Jung's persona, ego and self are described correctly. ⚠️ The particle comparison only works for possible *patterns* of connection (as Peterson phrases it); the brain's actual synapses (about 10¹⁴–10¹⁵) are far fewer than the universe's particles (about 10⁸⁰).

### 7. Elijah and the still small voice

Pagan religion found the divine in nature's power: the canyon, the ocean, the storm. Peterson says Elijah is the first biblical figure to find God's voice in conscience: not in the wind, the earthquake or the fire, but in "a still small voice" (1 Kings 19:11–12).

Calling conscience "just society's voice" doesn't solve the mystery. Where did society's voice come from? You still end up with an independent spirit that holds you accountable.

> ✅ 1 Kings 19:11–12 cited correctly. 🔴 "First to identify God with conscience" is Peterson's reading. Catholic connection: the Catechism calls conscience "man's most secret core and his sanctuary," where he is alone with God (CCC 1776, quoting *Gaudium et Spes* 16).

### 8. The burning bush: interest as a calling

Moses, on the run after killing an Egyptian who was beating a Hebrew slave, settles down as a shepherd. It's hard, admirable work, guarding lambs from lions and wolves, which is why the shepherd becomes an image of the savior.

Something off to the side catches his eye: a burning bush. Not a blazing oak across his path, but something he could have walked past. A bush is a small tree, a symbol of life; fire means transformation. A bush on fire is life so intense it's in flames.

That's what interest is. It comes to you; you can't manufacture it. You can smother it, but you can't create it. One of Peterson's clients had a kind, good friend but no romantic spark, and couldn't make it happen. People have a "calling."

As Moses comes closer, it deepens, as any real study spreads and reaches toward foundations, until he realizes he's on holy ground and takes off his sandals: humility. Pursuing an interest honestly changes you and burns away what isn't worthy, the way you clean yourself up when you're drawn to someone. Then God speaks and reveals himself as the ground of being ("I AM"), and Moses becomes a leader sent to confront Pharaoh. Follow what deeply compels you, and you reach the depths, and that makes you a leader.

> ✅ Exodus 2:11–15 and 3:1–14 summarized accurately. 🔴 The symbolic reading is his.

### 9. Conscience plus interest: the direction of growth

Interest pulls you toward something; conscience corrects you when you stray. Both are partly independent of you, outside you or deep within you.

Against the nihilist claim that growth has no direction: a baby's whole strategy is cry and be fed. Growing up means caring for yourself in more situations, in ways that make you welcome to others, and there is no upper limit. If you were all you could be, you'd be welcome and useful wherever you went. Embarrassment at a party is the ideal judging you: "There's no ideal without judgment."

Don't lie, or this guidance gets corrupted and you follow false idols. The same painful experience repeating itself is the sign that something false is bending your vision.

> 🔴 Interpretation.

### 10. What you want decides what you see

You don't see neutral facts and then decide what they're worth. Your motivation shapes perception. When you're hungry, the kitchen shows up as a place to make food: tools, paths, obstacles. Everything else (the decor) disappears.

Practical use: sit on your bed and ask, "What in this room annoys me?" Then clear the paths you walk, remove the obstacles, and put the things you need within reach.

The closet: every garment needs a decision. Does it fit? What is it for? What image are you aiming at? You have to let go of clothes that fit only yesterday's you. "There's actually no way of organizing your closet properly without arranging your aspirations properly." About the cluttered homes of the elderly: "Every single one of those cluttered items is a fight that no one had." Start with one foot of closet, or six garments on the bed: who bought these, and what were they aiming for?

> 🟢 Motivation shaping perception is well supported. ✅ Rule 6 of *12 Rules for Life* is "Set your house in perfect order before you criticize the world."

### 11. Mental health is never just private

"Get your act together" is incomplete. There's you, your spouse, family, community, country. "There's no mental health that's just inside your head." Proof: we punish even the worst criminals with solitary confinement. "There's no being sane in a toxic relationship."

Treat others as you want to be treated, because in practice there's no difference. Serve 1,000 people, and 900 will want to return the favor: one of you, 900 of them. Teen popularity, properly understood, is being genuinely useful to your peers.

Self-consciousness is one part of the trait psychologists call neuroticism: "the more you think about your narrow self, the more miserable you are." Telling yourself to stop thinking about yourself doesn't work. Anxious at a party? Focus on making someone else feel welcome. Ask questions and listen. Listen 10% better than average and "you'll be so popular, you won't be able to stand it."

> ✅ Self-consciousness is one of the six facets of neuroticism in the standard NEO-PI-R personality test. 🟢 The harm of solitary confinement, and the benefit of focusing on others in social anxiety, are supported.

### 12. Emotion: tools, obstacles and hope

At every moment you're inside a small frame: a starting point, a goal (a peanut butter sandwich), tools (bread, a knife; these bring good feelings), and obstacles (bad feelings). Your child showing you a drawing becomes an obstacle when you're hungry.

There are two kinds of good feeling:

- **Satisfaction:** you got it, and the frame closes. King of the high-school castle, and the next day you're looking for work.
- **Hope or enthusiasm:** comes from moving toward a goal you value. The emotion that runs your life comes "in relationship to a goal, not as a consequence of its accomplishment."

So have a goal, and make it one you can step toward. If it's too lofty, the world looks like nothing but obstacles; lower it. The best goal draws you in, shows progress, and changes you so the next goal is better. A third category, what you don't understand at all ("a bottomless pit"), comes in the next session.

> 🟢 Research on goal progress and positive feeling (e.g., Carver and Scheier) supports this.

### 13. Pick something: standing still has a cost

"I don't know what to do," for 15 years. Meanwhile you're aging: "there's no zero-cost stasis." You will aim at the wrong thing, but aim and practice, and your aim improves. Many paths go uphill and meet as you correct course.

Relativism is right that many ways of life work for different people. The one that doesn't work is no game at all. You can't actually do nothing; you'll fall under "the most narrow and bitter visions." Start with the basics: relationship, friends, family, education, work, being useful, interests.

Teenagers fitting in with peers is healthy; it is how they grow up and away from their parents. Don't sell your soul to the crowd, but someone who cares nothing about what others think is a psychopath. If most people think something is wrong with you, they're usually right.

> 🔴 Advice.

### 14. Your identity is layers of goals: break them down to fix them

Goals nest inside each other, and that nesting is your identity. "Make dinner" means nothing to a three-year-old, so break it down. At 15 months, his son learned: go to the knife drawer, open it, find a knife, put it on the table. A pat on the head: he has started setting the table.

Then the layers build: cut vegetables, set the table, do the dishes → cook a meal (macaroni and cheese at five) → care for a baby → good parent → good partner → good person. "Be good" has to be broken down until you find the level where something is missing. Training a new customer-service hire: maybe the problem is simply that they whisper on the phone. Find it.

The piano: you trip on a note. Slow down, find the exact mistake, fix it, practice until it's automatic, then put it back into the phrase. Any problem of adjusting to life works the same way.

The team captain is skilled not just at scoring, but at scoring in a way that makes everyone better and makes the game worth playing again. That's the moral ideal too, and meaning is the instinct telling you you're living that way.

> 🟢 Deliberate practice and breaking skills into layered steps are well supported.

## Key Corrections

- **Freud as pure suppression.** Freud also described *sublimation*, channeling drives into higher aims, which is close to Peterson's integration model. The contrast is sharper than Freud's actual view. 🟡
- **Elijah as the first to find God in conscience.** Earlier figures hear God speak personally (Samuel in 1 Sam 3, the call of Abraham). "First" is Peterson's interpretation, not a scholarly consensus. 🔴
- **Six-year-olds not toilet trained.** Reports describe children starting school around ages 4–5; the age is overstated, and the causes are debated. 🟡
- **Brain connections and particles.** True only for possible *patterns* of connection; actual synapses number about 10¹⁴–10¹⁵, far fewer than the roughly 10⁸⁰ particles in the universe. ✅
- **"Most people think something is wrong with you, they're usually right."** A heuristic. History has many cases of the majority being wrong about a person or minority. 🔴

## Meditations

- The voice at 3 a.m. is the man I could be, calling me to account.
- Hope lives on the road, not at the destination.
