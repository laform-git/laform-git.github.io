# How to Plan Your Life — S1 — Between Order and Chaos

**Source:** Jordan Peterson, *How to Plan Your Life*, Session 1: "Between Order and Chaos" (six-hour course) · [Peterson Academy](https://petersonacademy.com/courses/how-to-plan-your-life)

*About the course:* Six sessions, one note each. Peterson covers order and chaos, boundaries, setting a vision, and how instinct and motivation shape who we become. He calls it "Vision and Plan: Theory and Practice." A vision is the broad picture; a plan is that vision narrowed to a step you can take. The course moves from the general to the specific.

## Pocket Version

Everyone lives by some vision; refusing to choose one just hands control to impulse or to someone else. Life is lived on the border between order (what works as intended) and chaos (the unknown), and meaning is the signal that you are at that growing edge. Change starts with steps so small they feel embarrassing, and it compounds. The repeated, ordinary parts of the day are most of a life, so get those right.

## The Insight

There is no "no vision" option. Something will run your life: a good vision, a bad one, or someone else's. Life is split between order (what you understand) and chaos (what you don't). You feel meaning on the border between the two, because that is where you grow. Most of life is small moments that repeat, and change can start embarrassingly small and still speed up.

## Why It Matters

Drifting is not neutral. Without a chosen aim, short-term impulses take over. Peterson says there is little difference between being governed and being possessed, so choose to be possessed by the right thing. It also moves attention away from vacations and big events toward the ordinary daily routines, which is where most of a life actually happens.

## Key Points

### 1. There is no neutral ground

Peterson opens with: "There's no no-vision option." You live out a good vision or a bad one, your own or someone else's. Even a childish, pleasure-seeking vision shapes the world; it just puts your passing impulses in charge. If the wrong thing possesses you, you are in trouble, and you become trouble for everyone around you.

An idea only matters once you can act on it. That is why the course goes from vision to plan, and why anyone who leads, a family or a business, has to give both the big picture and the next concrete step.

> 🔴 His framing. 🟢 That people are always pursuing some aim fits motivation research.

### 2. Order and chaos: the map of experience

He uses the Daoist yin-yang: two serpents, head to tail. White is order; black is chaos (traditionally called "feminine," which he says is not the same as "female"). Wherever you stand, some things you understand and some you don't.

His definition: **you are in order when your actions produce the results you intended.** A smooth family dinner is order. An old, unresolved grievance thrown on the table is chaos breaking in. Chaos is both threat and promise: new knowledge, and a new version of you, live there.

The biology: animals have a mapped home territory and fear the unmapped ground where predators hide. Your nervous system feels safe in known territory and reacts to the unknown first with fear, then with curiosity. Humans add mental territory, fields of knowledge with frontiers where you can "almost understand."

> 🟢 Fear followed by exploration in response to novelty is well supported. 🔴 The Daoist metaphysics is interpretation.

### 3. How you face the unknown is the key decision

You can freeze in terror before the unknown, or welcome it, because the person who comes out the other side may be better than the one you are now.

The tyrant sides 100% with what he already knows. The cost: he never changes, and he forces his certainty on others because he doesn't trust himself to face the unknown and grow. You can be a tyrant to yourself, and that usually comes first. "Tyrants tend to live in hell."

Children naturally trust that the unknown, approached well, is good. Trauma often breaks that trust and produces cynics. Peterson cites Jesus: unless you *become* like little children you cannot enter the Kingdom (Mt 18:3). Becoming, not staying: an adult who has been hurt can regain that openness. He calls this an act of faith: deciding that the unknown, faced properly, is good.

> ✅ Scripture citation verified. 🔴 The application is his.

### 4. Meaning is a signal, not a luxury

You become interested when you are at the edge of what you know. That pull of interest is the instinct that drives learning; being absorbed in a book or a conversation is the sign that you are learning well.

Against the claim that life is meaningless, he answers from experience: meaning is the deepest signal that you are where you should be, becoming the next better version of yourself. That is why people say they can't live without meaning. Standing still is not living.

Music shows it: enough pattern to follow, enough surprise not to bore. A well-lived life has that musical quality, the right thing at the right time, falling into harmony.

Safety alone brings no growth. The best place is one foot in order, one in chaos. That is play: you pick an opponent who challenges you, not one you always beat, because a sure win teaches you nothing.

> 🔴 Peterson's thesis, stated as experience, not a proven mechanism. 🟢 The balance between challenge and skill in deep engagement ("flow") is supported.

### 5. The past has order and chaos too

Painful memories that were never resolved keep coming back on their own, and they unsettle you in proportion to how deep the hole was. The reason: if you fell into a hole and don't know why, part of you assumes the danger is still ahead and keeps replaying it, pushing you to build the skill you lacked. In therapy, with friends, or alone, you work out why it happened. Some of it is the randomness of the world; some may be your own misjudgment.

His example is bullying. If you were bullied as a child, you may dread a new workplace, which may mean the weakness that made you a target hasn't been addressed. He notes that who gets bullied, and who bullies, tends to stay stable over time, and insists this is not blaming the victim. His rule of thumb: once is chance, maybe twice; "if you have this same sort of relationship with an intimate partner three times, it's you."

In a conflict, anger says it's 95% the other person. Maybe it's 90%. "Even if it's 10% you," that 10% is the part you control, and fixing it may stop the other 90% from showing up.

> 🟢 Being bullied tends to persist over time. 🔴 The "three times" rule is a rule of thumb.

### 6. Shrink the task

Don't change so fast that it knocks you over. If a needed change feels overwhelming, you are taking on too much; go at the pace that works for you. If you know you should do something and aren't doing it, shrink it until you will take the step, ideally until you'd take it gladly. The garage that has been a mess for 10 years: clean one corner this week.

In his clinical work, first steps in a weak area were often "so trivial that it was embarrassing or shameful." That takes humility, and then "change accelerates." He quotes Jung: "The fool is the precursor to the savior." To improve, you have to let yourself be a beginner and do it badly; badly beats not at all.

The marriage arithmetic: three intimate moments a week for 50 years is about 7,500. If the first 200 go badly, that is nothing against 7,500. He prescribed regular dates to married couples; the first ones often went badly, and that was fine.

> 🟢 Breaking a task into small graded steps is standard behavioral practice. ⚠️ The Jung quote is unverified.

### 7. What repeats is your life

People overvalue the exceptions, vacations and special occasions, and are often disappointed. Part of that is pride: wanting to identify only with the exceptional.

Coming home from work takes about 20 minutes, five days a week. That is about 2 hours a week, roughly 12 workdays a year, about 5% of your life. Get 20 repeated things like that right, and your whole life is right.

Parents average about 20 minutes a day one-on-one with a child. If that time is a nightly bedtime fight, the whole relationship turns into conflict. The test of mastery is play: Peterson spent decades looking for the opposite of tyrannical power and concluded it is play. If bedtime becomes play, you've got it.

If you dread something every day, ask two questions: is my attitude wrong, or am I doing the wrong thing? "I have to keep this job for my family" turns your family into your chains: "a lovely gift to lay at the feet of your family," with a fair bit of the pleasure of martyrdom mixed in.

> 🔴 The arithmetic is an illustration, not a measurement.

### 8. Case study: the CV that never got opened

Clients stuck in jobs they hated wouldn't bring in their CV. Why? It hadn't been updated in 10 years. It had gaps they'd want to hide or lie about. A degree was left 95% finished, maybe to avoid responsibility, maybe to spite a father who forced engineering on an artist. Fix it, or you'll lack confidence, won't apply, and will end up blaming your wife for the job.

Rebuilding the CV means rebuilding your past. It might take two years, and it is worth it for work you like.

- **Know the odds.** About 95% of applications fail for reasons that apply to everyone, so don't take it personally.
- **Set a pace you can stand.** How many rejections a week can you take? Maybe one. One beats zero. Then three, then 10 a week for a year.
- **Prepare.** Write out 40 likely interview questions with honest answers.
- **Don't lie.** If a lie gets you the job, the lie got the job, and it isn't really yours.
- **Start small.** List what frightens you, rank it, start with the least frightening, and break it down further if needed.

Keep your deal with yourself. If the assignment was "open the CV and look at it," don't start rewriting; that's pushing too hard. Give yourself credit, as you would a child. And notice who around you belittles your small steps.

His disorganized clients' laptops were "a reflection of their psyche"; they couldn't even find the CV. Someone can stay 30 years in a job they hate because they won't face a messy hard drive.

> 🔴 Clinical anecdote. 🟢 The point about most job applications failing is sound.

### 9. Success compounds: the Matthew principle

"To those who have everything, more will be given" (Mt 25:29). Progress isn't a straight line; it snowballs. The second album is easier than the first. So a slow start doesn't doom you. Take the job that fits your talents, do it superbly, and someone will notice.

In his lab, he gave student applicants a real task. Some finished in two days; others came back a week later with reasonable-sounding problems. The ones who finished had just as many problems; they just didn't let them stop the work. A request from someone with resources is an opportunity, not a burden; confuse the two and you're doomed. And bringing in people who are a problem infects the whole lab and demoralizes the good workers.

> ✅ Sociologist Robert Merton named the "Matthew effect" (1968). 🟢 Advantage building on advantage is well documented.

### 10. Make good work visible; know your rewards

If you're doing well and no one notices, ask whether they know. Self-promotion is just communication. To ask for a raise, bring a list of what you did that made their life easier or more profitable, and what more you would do. Update your CV first so you know what you're worth on the market.

Peterson attributes part of the gender pay gap to women promoting themselves less (on average more agreeable and more prone to negative emotion). In the Alberta civil service, he saw conscientious older women do the work while narcissists took the credit.

Everyone needs rewards, and "none" is not a good plan. Find out what rewards you, and work it out with your spouse, children and employees. If she says "if you loved me, you'd know," ask exactly what she would like to hear. Let the other person do it awkwardly the first 20 times; by the 50th they'll mean it.

> 🟡 The pay-gap claim is contested; personality is one factor among several.

### 11. Specific praise beats conflict

B. F. Skinner found reward the most effective way to shape behavior; he even trained pigeons to guide missiles by pecking at images. The catch: wait until the behavior happens, then reward it.

A five-year-old who tidies up by accident: "That was the sort of thing an eight-year-old would do. Good work." The child will start doing that whole kind of thing. Same with employees: name exactly what they did well, and the good ones will work to earn it again. Specific praise is pleasant to give and to receive, and it builds harmony without fights.

> ✅ Skinner's Project Pigeon is historical fact. 🟢 Specific praise and positive reinforcement work.

## Key Corrections

- **Jung, "The fool is the precursor to the savior."** Not located in Jung's published works; the idea fits his writing on the fool or trickster figure, but the exact wording is unverified. ⚠️
- **The yin-yang as "two serpents."** The taijitu is usually described as two interlocking fish or simply light and dark halves; serpents is Peterson's own image. 🔴
- **Matthew 25:29.** Quoted loosely. The text reads "to every one who has will more be given"; Peterson's "everything" is a paraphrase. ✅
- **Gender pay gap.** Most of the raw gap is explained by occupation, hours and career breaks; lower self-promotion linked to agreeableness is one proposed factor, not an established main cause. 🟡
- **"Three times, it's you."** A clinical rule of thumb, not a research finding. Repeated bad relationships can also come from a narrow social pool or unchanged circumstances. 🔴
- **20 minutes a day one-on-one with a child.** Time-use figures vary widely by country, age and how "one-on-one" is counted; treat it as illustrative. 🟡
