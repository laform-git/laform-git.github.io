
# Bikman — Basics of Biology, Ch.6: Metabolic Mechanisms
*The cell's full energy-extraction pipeline — glycolysis, beta-oxidation, the citrate cycle, the electron transport system and ATP synthase — with insulin as the master switch deciding whether acetyl-CoA is burned, stored as fat, or turned into ketones.*

*Source: Dr. Benjamin Bikman, Peterson Academy course "Basics of Biology," Lecture 6 (transcript) — the final lecture of the course, followed by a Q&A on statins and cancer metabolism. Same lecturer as the "Why We Get Sick" and "Essentials of Endocrinology" series; this lecture is the energy-chemistry foundation underneath both.*

**In plain words:** your cells burn fuel (sugar, fat, ketones) much like a campfire burns wood, but instead of mostly heat they capture the energy in a small molecule called ATP, the cell's spending money. This happens in three stages: breaking fuel down, a recycling loop called the citrate cycle, and a final energy chain that uses oxygen. Whether a cell burns fuel or stores it as fat is largely decided by one hormone: insulin.

**Words in this note:**
- **ATP**: adenosine triphosphate, the cell's energy currency
- **CoQ10**: coenzyme Q10, a helper in the mitochondria's energy chain
- **ApoB**: apolipoprotein B; counts all the cholesterol particles that can clog arteries
- **NADH**: a molecule that carries energy from food to the mitochondria
- **ADP**: adenosine diphosphate, "spent" ATP
- **LDL**: low-density lipoprotein, the "bad" cholesterol carrier
- **DHEA**: a hormone made by the adrenal glands that the body turns into sex hormones
- **TRT**: testosterone replacement therapy

## Why This Matters
Every hormone, receptor and transporter in the other Bikman series comes down, in the end, to a question about energy: is the cell breaking fuel down to make ATP, or building something and storing it? This lecture lays out the machinery behind that choice and names the one signal that largely decides it: **insulin**.

## The Big Idea — Metabolism = Breaking Down + Building Up, Linked by ATP
**Metabolism** is the total of every chemical reaction in every cell: hundreds of thousands of reactions across trillions of cells. It is the *balance* of two processes, not just "breaking things down," as the buzzword suggests. ✅
- **Catabolism:** breaking things down. It releases energy.
- **Anabolism:** building things up (nucleic acids, carbohydrates, proteins, fats). It uses the energy that catabolism releases.
- **ATP links the two.** ATP (adenosine triphosphate) is the cell's "currency." It is built on a ribose sugar and a double-ring base (like the nucleic acids), with **three phosphates** on its tail. Breaking those high-energy bonds lets an enzyme do work it could not otherwise do. The cell "buys" everything with ATP: building molecules, pumping things against a gradient, nerve signals, muscle contraction. ✅
- **The fire comparison:** a campfire takes in carbon fuel plus oxygen and gives off carbon dioxide (CO₂) and heat. A cell takes in carbon fuel (carbohydrate or fat) plus oxygen and gives off CO₂, a little heat and, the key difference, **a lot of ATP**. ✅
- **Where the energy comes from:** all energy ultimately comes from the sun. Plants capture CO₂ from the air and lock it into carbon bonds, and animals eat those bonds. Bikman's thought experiment: the fuel in your body was once CO₂ in the air. ✅

The lecture covers three steps in order: **(1) breaking down nutrients → (2) the citrate cycle → (3) the electron transport system + ATP synthase.**

## Punchlines & Quotable Facts (Lecture 6)
- **"Animals do not make lactic acid. It does not exist."** We make **lactate**; he says lactic acid applies only to bacteria. The "lactic acid burn" his old track coach blamed is the wrong molecule *and* the wrong cause. 🟡 Evidence check: at the body's normal acidity level (pH), lactic acid exists almost entirely as lactate, in humans and bacteria alike. So the useful point is the chemistry, not a split between humans and bacteria.
- **Insulin "reigns supreme."** Its main effect is to make a cell store energy, "always, always." To understand how a cell uses energy, you have to understand insulin.
- **Acetyl-CoA is the meeting point of all fuel use** that involves the mitochondria. Glucose, fat, ketones and lactate all flow into it, and it can be turned into anything else. 🟢 With one key exception: humans cannot turn acetyl-CoA into new glucose.
- **One turn of the citrate cycle is worth about 10 ATP** (mostly through NADH and FADH₂ later on). So an 18-carbon fatty acid, split into 9 acetyl-CoAs, gives about **90 ATP from the citrate cycle alone**. 🟢 Counting the NADH and FADH₂ made while the fat is being split (beta-oxidation), the whole 18-carbon fatty acid gives about 120 ATP.
- **Insulin as the "fat shot":** people with type 1 diabetes (whose bodies make no insulin) describe insulin this way. Untreated, they eat huge amounts and waste away. Once on insulin, they eat less and gain fat.
- **"You're not burning fat in the fat cell."** Lipolysis only *moves* fat out of the fat cell. The burning (beta-oxidation) happens in mitochondria elsewhere.
- **Carbohydrate in the diet is not an essential nutrient.** The body can make all the glucose it needs. 🟢 Evidence check: broadly correct, because the liver and kidneys can make glucose. The US recommended intake of 130 g/day was set from how much glucose the brain uses each day (National Academies 2007).
- **Oxygen is the final electron catcher and ends up as water.** This "metabolic water" is how, he says, camels get much of their water from the fat in their humps. 🟡 Not confirmed and probably overstated: burning hump fat does make water, but breathing in the extra oxygen needed to burn it also loses water, so the hump is mainly an energy store.

## The Three-Step Pipeline at a Glance

| Step | Where | Goes in | Comes out | Main control point |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **1a. Glycolysis** (splitting glucose) | Cytosol (the cell's fluid) | Glucose (6 carbons) | 2 pyruvate (3 carbons each), NADH, a little ATP | Reactions 1, 3, 9 (one-way enzymes): insulin turns on, glucagon turns off |
| **1b. Lipolysis → beta-oxidation** (releasing fat, then cutting it up) | Fat cell (lipolysis); mitochondria of any cell (beta-oxidation) | Stored fat (triglyceride) → free fatty acids | 2-carbon acetyl-CoA pieces | Insulin guards the fat droplet; CAT1/CAT2 control entry into mitochondria |
| **2. Citrate cycle** | Inside the mitochondrion (the matrix) | Acetyl-CoA (2 carbons) | 1 ATP, 3 NADH, 1 FADH₂, 2 CO₂ per turn | Citrate synthase slowed by its own product (citrate) |
| **3. Electron transport + ATP synthase** | Inner membrane of the mitochondrion | NADH, FADH₂, oxygen | ~2.5 ATP per NADH, ~1.5 per FADH₂; water | Supply of electron carriers; oxygen as the final catcher |

Bikman stresses that **NADH and FADH₂ are not ATP.** They are loaded electron carriers, like charged batteries. Whenever a reaction makes one, read it as "future ATP, and a lot of it." ✅

## Glycolysis — Nine Reactions, a Buy-In and a Payoff
Glycolysis means "splitting sugar." The cell first spends a little ATP (the buy-in) and then earns more back (the payoff).

| Reaction | What happens | Energy | Direction |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **1** | Glucokinase/hexokinase adds a phosphate to glucose, locking it in the cell and committing it to glycolysis | Costs ATP (buy-in) | One-way |
| **2** | Small change in shape | None | Two-way |
| **3** | Second buy-in: another phosphate added | Costs ATP | One-way |
| **4** | The 6-carbon molecule is **split into two 3-carbon molecules**; everything after this happens twice. End of the setup phase | None | Two-way |
| **5** | NADH made (payoff phase begins) | Gives NADH | Two-way |
| **6** | ATP made | Gives ATP | Two-way |
| **7–8** | Small rearrangements | None | Two-way |
| **9** | ATP made; final product is **pyruvate** | Gives ATP | One-way |

🟢 Evidence check on the numbering: textbooks count **ten** reactions in glycolysis. The one-way steps are 1, 3 and 10, NADH is made at step 6, and ATP at steps 7 and 10. Bikman's nine-step numbering merges two steps; the logic is the same.

- ✅ The two ATP spent at reactions 1 and 3 are paid back by reaction 6 alone (two molecules, one ATP each): "you already got your money back." 🟢 Adding reaction 9 (also done twice), the arithmetic gives a **net of 2 ATP** per glucose from glycolysis itself. Bikman does not state the net figure in this lecture, so treat that number as my tally rather than a quote.
- ✅ At pyruvate, glycolysis is a "choose your own adventure." Either **anaerobic / non-oxidative / outside the mitochondria** (pyruvate → lactate, staying in the cytosol), or **aerobic / oxidative** (pyruvate enters the mitochondria). Aerobic means with oxygen.

**Pyruvate → lactate (the no-oxygen route):** uses up one NADH (the "reducing power"). ✅ When it is the only option:
- **Red blood cells:** they have no mitochondria and no nucleus, so they only ever make lactate. They live about 115 days, roughly 4 months (Mock 2014).
- **Hard-working muscle:** demand outruns the oxygen supply.
- **Cancer cells:** rely almost entirely on glycolysis without the mitochondria. 🟡 Bikman says cancer "abhors" mitochondria; this is his emphasis, and he presents it as an active research area. Evidence check: most cancer cells still have working mitochondria. The "Warburg effect" is heavy glycolysis even when oxygen is present, thought to supply building materials for growth (Vander Heiden 2009).

**Pyruvate → mitochondria:** needs a large, multi-part enzyme called **pyruvate dehydrogenase**, which makes another NADH and turns pyruvate into **acetyl-CoA**. It shuts down when the mitochondria are "loaded" with too much acetyl-CoA, ATP or NADH, which leaves lactate as the only way out. ✅

## Lactate Is Not the Villain — The Cori Cycle
- 🟡 The burning feeling in hard-working muscle happens at the same time as lactate is made, but lactate does not cause it. The same high-demand state that makes lactate also breaks down huge amounts of ATP, and **each ATP split releases a free hydrogen**. This makes the area more acidic and irritates nerve endings. Evidence check: the ATP-hydrogen explanation is one side of a published debate (Robergs 2004). In humans, the feelings of muscle fatigue and ache came from hydrogen (protons), lactate and ATP acting together, not from any one alone (Pollak 2014).
- ✅ **The Cori cycle** (discovered by the husband-and-wife Cori team): muscle or red blood cells release lactate into the blood → the liver takes it up → **gluconeogenesis** (making new glucose) rebuilds it into glucose.
- 🟢 After roughly **24 hours without food**, all blood glucose has to be made from scratch by the liver, and **lactate is the main raw material** for it: "beautiful metabolic recycling." Evidence check: making new glucose supplied 64% of the glucose released in the first 22 hours of fasting and 96% by about 40 hours (Rothman 1991). The kidneys also help, and glycerol and amino acids are raw materials alongside lactate.
- 🟡 Bikman's claim that lactate "doesn't contribute to any problem whatsoever" is stated strongly and without exceptions in this lecture. Carry it as his position, not a settled rule. Lactate is now seen mainly as a fuel and signal passed between tissues (Brooks 2018), though a high blood lactate is still a marker of serious illness such as shock.

## Making New Glucose and the No-Wasted-Effort Rule
- The three one-way enzymes of glycolysis (reactions 1, 3, 9) are the **control points**. Gluconeogenesis uses **detour enzymes** that go around them (three steps need detours). ✅
- **Opposite switches:** insulin turns the three glycolysis enzymes **on** and the detour enzymes **off**. Glucagon, insulin's opposite hormone, does exactly the reverse. Bikman says insulin and glucagon are never high together, so the cell never runs both directions at once. 🟡 Evidence check: they can be high together. Glucagon is wrongly raised in poorly controlled diabetes even when insulin is high (Unger 2012).
- A cycle that builds and breaks the same thing at the same time is a **futile cycle**: "the cell abhors the lack of efficiency." ✅
- The cell's own energy state also has a say: plenty of ATP slows the glycolysis enzymes. ✅
- 🟢 In practice: after a starchy meal, insulin is up and glucose is burned. With fasting or cutting carbohydrates, glucagon is up, the body shifts toward burning fat, and the liver makes the small amount of glucose that cells without mitochondria (like red blood cells) still need.

## Releasing Fat → Cutting It Up → Acetyl-CoA
- **The fat cell:** one giant droplet of **triglyceride** (stored fat: a glycerol backbone plus fatty acids) pushes the nucleus and other parts to the edge. A **single layer of phospholipids** (tails in, heads out) coats the droplet and guards it. ✅
- **Lipolysis** means enzymes called lipases pulling free fatty acids off the triglyceride. It only happens once hormones tell the phospholipid guards to "move out of the way." **High insulin keeps the guards in place; low insulin lets lipolysis go ahead.** ✅ 🟢 More exactly, the "guards" are proteins such as perilipin-1 on the phospholipid surface. Adrenaline-type signals change them so the lipases can work, and insulin opposes that signal (Lass 2011).
- Bikman says the fat cell has **no direct nerve link to muscle**: a working muscle changes the body's hormones, and those hormones carry the message back to the fat cell. ✅ 🟢 Evidence check: fat tissue does have its own nerve supply from the "fight or flight" (sympathetic) nervous system, and it is a main driver of fat release (Bartness 2014).
- **Three possible fates of a free fatty acid** once it reaches a cell: (1) become **phospholipids** for membranes; (2) be **rebuilt (re-esterified) into new triglyceride**, which is what drives **fatty liver disease**: insulin-resistant fat cells leak fat, and high insulin plus high free fatty acids tell the liver to store it; (3) **beta-oxidation**, the actual burning. ✅ In people with fatty liver, about 59% of the liver fat came from fatty acids released by fat tissue, 26% from new fat made in the liver, and 15% from the diet (Donnelly 2005).
- **CAT1 and CAT2** (carnitine acyl transferase; the current names are CPT1 and CPT2, carnitine palmitoyltransferase) sit on the outer and inner membranes of the mitochondrion and move fatty acids in. The carrier, carnitine, is what cycles back out. This is a controlled checkpoint. ✅
- **Beta-oxidation** cuts the carbon chain after the second ("beta") carbon, two carbons at a time. Each piece becomes **acetyl-CoA**, the same molecule glucose becomes after pyruvate dehydrogenase. ✅

## The Citrate Cycle (Krebs Cycle)
- Named after **Sir Hans Krebs** (Nobel Prize). "Citrate cycle" is more exact, because **citrate is the first molecule made**. It is called a cycle because its parts are used again and again, though they are not arranged in a physical circle. ✅
- **Every** fuel burned in the mitochondria passes through it: glucose, fat, **ketones**, even **lactate**, almost always after being turned into acetyl-CoA. ✅
- **For each acetyl-CoA, one full turn gives:** **1 ATP, 3 NADH, 1 FADH₂ and 2 CO₂.** ✅ The two CO₂ match the two carbons that came in with acetyl-CoA (strictly, the carbons released in a given turn come from another molecule in the cycle, oxaloacetate, and the new ones leave in later turns). This is where most of the CO₂ we breathe out comes from.
- **The CO₂ loop:** we breathe out CO₂ → plants take it up → plants release oxygen → we breathe it in for the electron transport system. ✅

## The Fork in the Road — Making Fat vs. Making Ketones (Insulin Decides)
The first reaction of the cycle (acetyl-CoA → citrate, done by citrate synthase) slows down when citrate builds up. Acetyl-CoA then needs somewhere else to go:

|  | **Insulin HIGH** | **Insulin LOW** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Acetyl-CoA goes to** | **Lipogenesis**: making fat from scratch (de novo lipogenesis), two carbons at a time | **Ketogenesis**: making ketones, a fuel for nearly every cell with mitochondria. Made only in the liver, which cannot use them itself |
| **How** | Citrate leaves the mitochondria into the cytosol and is turned back into acetyl-CoA. A molecule made along the way (malonyl-CoA) **blocks CAT1 (CPT1)** (McGarry 2001), so fat cannot get in to be burned | Nothing tells fat release to stop; fat flows out and is burned or turned into ketones |
| **Overall direction** | Build and store | Break down and burn |

✅ A cell does not "know" by itself what to do. It relies on hormone signals about the state of the whole body, and here insulin is the main one.

**The no-insulin experiment: type 1 diabetes**
- ✅ With no insulin, there is no brake on fat release and no way to make new fat. A person can overeat enormously and still not gain fat. People with untreated type 1 diabetes eat huge amounts, waste away, burn energy fast, and lose ketones in their breath and urine. They also lose large amounts of glucose, and its calories, in the urine.
- ✅ When they start insulin, they eat less and begin to gain fat; hence "fat shots." It also explains the eating disorder **diabulimia**, skipping insulin to stay thin. That causes runaway blood sugar and **ketoacidosis** (a dangerous build-up of acid in the blood), which is **clearly not the same as nutritional ketosis** from fasting or a ketogenic diet. Cutting back on insulin was linked to about three times higher death rates in women with type 1 diabetes (Goebel-Fabbri 2008).

## The Electron Transport System — Making Most of the ATP
- ✅ **Oxygen pulls electrons harder than almost anything else in the cell** (it is extremely electronegative). That pull is what powers the whole system.
- Bikman prefers **"system" to "chain"** because **Complex I and Complex II are not linked**. Complex I takes electrons from **NADH**; Complex II takes electrons from **FADH₂** (its own "direct deposit"). Both hand electrons to **ubiquinone (CoQ10)**, which carries them to **Complex III**, then (through cytochrome c) to **Complex IV**. ✅
- **Proton pumping:** as electrons pass, the complexes pump protons (hydrogen ions) into the space between the inner and outer membranes. Complex I = 4, Complex II = 0, Complex III = 4, Complex IV = 2 (the lecture swaps III and IV; the totals are the same). ✅

| Electron source | Enters at | Protons pumped | ATP made |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **NADH** | Complex I | 4 + 4 + 2 = **10** | **~2.5 ATP** |
| **FADH₂** | Complex II (skips I) | 0 + 4 + 2 = **6** | **~1.5 ATP** |

- ✅ At Complex IV, **oxygen** finally receives the electrons. It also picks up protons so it stays neutral instead of becoming a damaging **superoxide** (a reactive oxygen species), and it forms **water**.
- ✅ **ATP synthase** (Bikman refuses to call it "Complex V") is the only way for the protons to flow back in. It works like a water wheel: **every 4 protons that pass through make 1 ATP** by attaching a free phosphate to ADP (ATP with one phosphate missing). That is why 10 protons ≈ 2.5 ATP and 6 ≈ 1.5. 🟢 Evidence check: the measured yields of about 2.5 and 1.5 hold (Hinkle 2005), but the bookkeeping differs. About 2.7 protons pass through the synthase per ATP in mammals, plus about 1 more to bring in the phosphate and send the ATP out (Watt 2010). Some protons also leak back without making ATP, which releases heat.
- ✅ The ATP total for one turn of the citrate cycle: 3 × 2.5 + 1 × 1.5 + 1 = **10 ATP**.
- **CoQ10 comes from cholesterol.** 🔴 (Bikman's claim) He says cholesterol is turned into ubiquinone, the electron carrier, so making less cholesterol means making less ubiquinone, and the electron transport system suffers. Evidence check: CoQ10 is not made from cholesterol. The two share the early part of the same production line (the mevalonate pathway), which is why statins, which block that pathway, also lower CoQ10 levels (Littarru 2007).

## Plants in Reverse — Chloroplast vs. Mitochondrion
- Mitochondria are long and stringy ("mitos" means thread); the bean shapes in textbooks are slices through a string. Chloroplasts look like **stacks of contact lenses**. Plant cells have some mitochondria too, plus chloroplasts. ✅
- The chloroplast runs the mirror image: it takes in **CO₂ plus energy from sunlight** and links carbons into chains, releasing oxygen. The two systems keep each other in balance. ✅
- 🟡 Bikman adds that rising CO₂ is making plants "blossom" because CO₂ is plant fuel. This is a side comment, not developed. 🟢 Evidence check: satellite data show substantial greening of the planet since the 1980s, about 70% of it put down to CO₂ acting as a fertilizer (Zhu 2016).

## Q&A Addendum — Statins and How Cancer Uses Glucose
**Statins:** these drugs block **HMG-CoA reductase**, the enzyme that makes cholesterol. Bikman says every cell can make cholesterol, so cholesterol in food does not set blood cholesterol. 🟡 Evidence check: the effect is modest but real; most studies that changed people's diets link intakes above current averages with higher LDL cholesterol (Carson 2020). According to Bikman, the side effects include muscle pain and breakdown (likely damage to mitochondria from low ubiquinone), possible effects on the heart, and lower sex-hormone production. So he says people on statins should also take CoQ10. 🟡 These are Bikman's stated positions; the lecture puts no numbers on any of them. Evidence check: in blinded trials of about 155,000 people, statins caused a small excess of mostly mild muscle pain in the first year (about 11 extra cases per 1,000 person-years), and over 90% of muscle symptoms reported on statins were not due to the drug (CTT 2022). Reviews of CoQ10 for statin muscle symptoms disagree (no benefit in Banach 2015; some benefit in Qu 2018). Statins lowered testosterone by about 0.66 nmol/L in men (Schooling 2013).
- 🟡 **His own paper:** whether statins helped was not predicted by LDL level alone. In people with high LDL, statins seemed to help only if **triglycerides (blood fats) were also high**. He says people on low-carb, ketogenic or carnivore diets may have high LDL with low triglycerides and get no benefit. The paper is a review of other studies by Diamond, Bikman and Mason (Diamond 2022). Evidence check: large pooled analyses of trials show that each 1 mmol/L drop in LDL cuts major heart and blood-vessel events by just over a fifth (CTT 2010). No trial has tested statins specifically in people on low-carbohydrate diets.
- 🟡 He also says high LDL is a lasting feature of long-lived people. Worth keeping as a claim to check, not a conclusion. Evidence check: a systematic review found high LDL linked to lower death rates in people over 60 (Ravnskov 2016), but such studies can be misled by reverse causation (illness itself lowers cholesterol). In a large Copenhagen study, higher LDL raised heart-attack risk in people aged 70 to 100 (Mortensen 2020).

**Cancer and glucose:** it does not matter whether the glucose comes from the liver or from food. ✅ Cancer cells use glucose at **about 200 times the normal rate**. This is the basis of PET scans, which use glucose tagged with a tracer to find tumors. 🟡 The 200-times figure is not confirmed; tumor glucose uptake on PET varies widely by type of tumor. Bikman describes **Thomas Seyfried's "glucose ketone index"**: aim for blood glucose and ketones near 1:1. He reports shrinkage of some brain cancers, such as glioblastoma, and chemotherapy drugs working better. 🟡 Presented by the lecturer as new evidence, not as medical advice. Evidence check: the glucose ketone index comes from Seyfried's group. A 2026 systematic review of ketogenic diets in glioma (a type of brain tumor) found that the two randomized trials showed no difference in survival and concluded that benefit is unproven (Persiani 2026).

## Key Corrections to Carry Forward
- 🔴 "Metabolism" means only burning calories: it is the balance of breaking down **and** building up, linked by ATP.
- 🔴 "Lactic acid builds up in muscle and causes the burn": animals make **lactate**, and the burn follows ATP breakdown and the acid from released hydrogen, not lactate.
- 🔴 "Lipolysis is fat burning": lipolysis only releases fatty acids; **beta-oxidation in the mitochondria** is the burning.
- 🔴 "Extra calories make fat no matter what the hormones do": with no insulin, storing fat is impossible however much is eaten; **insulin decides between making fat and making ketones**.
- 🔴 "Ketoacidosis and ketosis are the same": ketoacidosis (in uncontrolled type 1 diabetes) is not nutritional ketosis.
- 🔴 "The electron transport chain is one straight chain": Complexes I and II feed in separately, from NADH and FADH₂.

## Practical Takeaway
Whether your cells are building or burning depends on one signal you can influence: insulin. High insulin: store, make fat, keep fat out of the mitochondria. Low insulin: release fat, burn it, make ketones.

## Questions to Learn From (Socratic) — with Answer Options
**1. Acetyl-CoA can become either new fat or ketones. What decides which path it takes?**
A. The cell senses its own fat stores and decides by itself
B. \* The body's insulin level: high insulin drives fat-making (citrate leaves the mitochondria and is rebuilt into fat, and CAT1/CAT2 are blocked), while low insulin drives ketone-making
C. Whether the cell contains more NADH than FADH₂
*Why B: Bikman stresses that a cell does not know by itself what to do and relies on hormones. Insulin is the main signal at this fork.*

**2. CoQ10 (ubiquinone) is made through the same pathway as cholesterol and carries electrons to Complex III. What is a sensible question to raise with a doctor if a statin is ever suggested?**
A. Whether CoQ10 is irrelevant because the electron transport system works without it
B. Whether LDL alone is enough reason, since triglycerides play no role in statin benefit
C. \* Whether triglycerides are also high, given Bikman's paper that benefit followed high LDL plus high triglycerides, and how making less ubiquinone might affect mitochondria and muscle
*Why C: this combines the lecture's CoQ10 mechanism with the Q&A claim. Both are Bikman's positions (🟡) and are framed as questions for a doctor, not conclusions.*

**3. NADH pumps 10 protons and FADH₂ pumps 6, and 4 protons are needed per ATP. Roughly how much ATP does each give?**
A. \* About 2.5 ATP per NADH and about 1.5 ATP per FADH₂
B. About 10 ATP per NADH and 6 per FADH₂
C. Both give exactly 1 ATP
*Why A: ATP synthase makes one ATP per four protons, so 10 ÷ 4 = 2.5 and 6 ÷ 4 = 1.5. Complex II skips Complex I's four protons.*

**4. Lactate is not lactic acid. Which statement best explains the "burn" in a hard-working muscle?**
A. Lactic acid builds up and has to be cleared
B. \* The high-demand state both makes lactate and splits huge amounts of ATP, releasing hydrogen that makes the area acidic and irritates nerves; lactate is there at the same time but does not cause it
C. Lactate is a poison that damages nerve endings
*Why B: Bikman presents lactate as a fuel the liver recycles through the Cori cycle. The pain comes from the hydrogen released when ATP is split.*
