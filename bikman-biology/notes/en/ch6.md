
# Bikman — Basics of Biology, Ch.6: Metabolic Mechanisms
*The cell's full energy-extraction pipeline — glycolysis, beta-oxidation, the citrate cycle, the electron transport system and ATP synthase — with insulin as the master switch deciding whether acetyl-CoA is burned, stored as fat, or turned into ketones.*

*Source: Dr. Benjamin Bikman, Peterson Academy course "Basics of Biology," Lecture 6 (transcript) — the final lecture of the course, followed by a Q&A on statins and cancer metabolism. Same lecturer as the "Why We Get Sick" and "Essentials of Endocrinology" series; this lecture is the energy-chemistry foundation underneath both.*

## Why This Matters
Every hormone, receptor and transporter covered in the other Bikman series ultimately cashes out as a question about energy: is the cell breaking fuel down to make ATP, or building something up and storing it? This lecture lays out the machinery behind that choice and names the one signal that largely decides it — **insulin**.

## Scope-Setting Claim — Metabolism = Catabolism + Anabolism, Brokered by ATP
**Metabolism** is the sum of every reaction in every cell — hundreds of thousands of reactions across trillions of cells. It is the *balance* of two processes, not just "breaking things down" as the buzzword implies. ✅
- **Catabolism:** breaking things down — releases energy.
- **Anabolism:** building things up (nucleic acids, carbohydrates, proteins, lipids) — consumes energy supplied by catabolism.
- **ATP is the mediator between them** — adenosine triphosphate, the cell's "currency." It is built on a ribose sugar and a double-ring base (like the nucleic acids), with **three phosphates** on its tail; breaking those high-energy bonds lets an enzyme do work it could not otherwise do. The cell "buys" everything with it: synthesis, active transport against a gradient, nerve signals, muscle contraction. ✅
- **The fire analogy:** a campfire takes in carbon fuel plus oxygen and releases CO₂ and heat. A cell takes in carbon fuel (carbohydrate or lipid) plus oxygen and releases CO₂, a little heat, and — the key difference — **a lot of ATP**. ✅
- **Energy lineage:** all energy ultimately comes from the sun; plants bind atmospheric CO₂ into carbon bonds, and animals eat those bonds. Bikman's thought experiment: the fuel in your body was once CO₂ in the air. ✅

The lecture runs three steps in order: **(1) nutrient catabolism → (2) the citrate cycle → (3) the electron transport system + ATP synthase.**

## Punchlines & Quotable Facts (Lecture 6)
- **"Animals do not make lactic acid. It does not exist."** — we make **lactate**; lactic acid applies only to bacteria. The "lactic acid burn" blamed by the old track coach is the wrong molecule *and* the wrong cause. 🟡 Evidence check: at body pH, lactic acid exists almost entirely as lactate in humans and bacteria alike, so the useful point is the chemistry, not a human-versus-bacteria split.
- **Insulin "reigns supreme"** — its main effect is to make a cell store energy, "always, always." Understanding how a cell uses energy requires understanding insulin.
- **Acetyl-CoA is the nexus of all nutrient metabolism** involving the mitochondria — glucose, fat, ketones and lactate all funnel into it, and it can be turned into anything else. 🟢 With one key exception: humans cannot turn acetyl-CoA into net new glucose.
- **One turn of the citrate cycle is worth about 10 ATP** (via NADH and FADH₂ downstream) — so an 18-carbon fatty acid, split into 9 acetyl-CoAs, yields about **90 ATP from the citrate cycle alone**. 🟢 Counting beta-oxidation's own NADH and FADH₂, the whole 18-carbon fatty acid yields about 120 ATP.
- **Insulin as the "fat shot"** — type 1 diabetics describe insulin this way: untreated, they eat voraciously and waste away; once on insulin, intake drops and fat is gained.
- **"You're not burning fat in the fat cell"** — lipolysis only *moves* fat out of the fat cell; the burning (beta-oxidation) happens in mitochondria elsewhere.
- **Dietary carbohydrate is a non-essential nutrient** — the body can make all the glucose it needs. 🟢 Evidence check: broadly correct, because the liver and kidneys can make glucose; the US recommended intake of 130 g/day was set from the brain's daily glucose use (National Academies 2007).
- **Oxygen is the final electron acceptor and ends up as water** — metabolic water, which is how camels get much of their water from the fat in their humps. 🟡 Unverified and likely overstated: burning hump fat does make water, but breathing in the extra oxygen needed to burn it also costs water, so the hump is mainly an energy store.

## The Three-Step Pipeline at a Glance

| Step | Where | Input | Output | Main control point |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **1a. Glycolysis** | Cytosol | Glucose (6C) | 2 pyruvate (3C), NADH, a little ATP | Reactions 1, 3, 9 (one-way enzymes) — insulin on, glucagon off |
| **1b. Lipolysis → beta-oxidation** | Fat cell (lipolysis); mitochondria of any cell (beta-oxidation) | Triglyceride → free fatty acids | 2-carbon acetyl-CoA units | Insulin guards the lipid droplet; CAT1/CAT2 gate entry to mitochondria |
| **2. Citrate cycle** | Mitochondrial matrix | Acetyl-CoA (2C) | 1 ATP, 3 NADH, 1 FADH₂, 2 CO₂ per turn | Citrate synthase inhibited by its own product (citrate) |
| **3. Electron transport + ATP synthase** | Inner mitochondrial membrane | NADH, FADH₂, O₂ | ~2.5 ATP per NADH, ~1.5 per FADH₂; water | Supply of electron carriers; oxygen as final acceptor |

Bikman stresses that **NADH and FADH₂ are not ATP** — they are loaded electron carriers. Whenever a reaction produces one, read it as "future ATP, and a lot of it." ✅

## Glycolysis — Nine Reactions, a Buy-In and a Payoff

| Reaction | What happens | Energy | Direction |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **1** | Glucokinase/hexokinase phosphorylates glucose — locks it in the cell and commits it to glycolysis | Costs ATP (buy-in) | Irreversible |
| **2** | Minor structural change | None | Reversible |
| **3** | Second buy-in phosphorylation | Costs ATP | Irreversible |
| **4** | The 6-carbon molecule is **split into two 3-carbon molecules** — everything after happens in duplicate. End of the preparatory phase | None | Reversible |
| **5** | NADH produced (payoff phase begins) | Gives NADH | Reversible |
| **6** | ATP produced | Gives ATP | Reversible |
| **7–8** | Minor rearrangements | None | Reversible |
| **9** | ATP produced; final product is **pyruvate** | Gives ATP | Irreversible |

🟢 Evidence check on the numbering: textbooks count **ten** glycolytic reactions. The one-way steps are 1, 3 and 10, NADH is made at step 6, and ATP at steps 7 and 10; Bikman's nine-step numbering merges two steps, and the logic is unchanged.

- ✅ The two ATP spent at reactions 1 and 3 are paid back by reaction 6 alone (two molecules, one ATP each) — "you already got your money back." 🟢 Adding reaction 9 (also in duplicate), the arithmetic implies a **net of 2 ATP** per glucose from glycolysis itself; Bikman does not state the net figure in this lecture, so treat that number as my tally rather than a quote.
- ✅ Glycolysis is a "choose your own adventure" at pyruvate — **anaerobic / non-oxidative / non-mitochondrial** (pyruvate → lactate, stays in the cytosol) versus **aerobic / oxidative** (pyruvate enters the mitochondria). Aerobic = with oxygen.

**Pyruvate → lactate (anaerobic end):** uses up one NADH (the "reducing power"). ✅ When it is the only option:
- **Red blood cells** — no mitochondria, no nucleus; they only ever make lactate (and live about 115 days, roughly 4 months; Mock 2014).
- **Vigorously working muscle** — demand outruns oxygen supply.
- **Cancer cells** — rely almost exclusively on anaerobic glycolysis. 🟡 Bikman says cancer "abhors" mitochondria; this is his emphasis, and he frames it as an active research area. Evidence check: most cancer cells still have working mitochondria; the "Warburg effect" is heavy glycolysis even when oxygen is present, thought to supply building blocks for growth (Vander Heiden 2009).

**Pyruvate → mitochondria:** requires the multi-part enzyme **pyruvate dehydrogenase**, which makes another NADH and converts pyruvate to **acetyl-CoA**. It shuts down when the mitochondria are "loaded" — too much acetyl-CoA, ATP or NADH — leaving lactate as the only exit. ✅

## Lactate Is Not the Villain — The Cori Cycle
- 🟡 The burning sensation in hard-working muscle is a coincidence of state, not an effect of lactate: the same high-demand condition that produces lactate also breaks down huge amounts of ATP, and **each ATP split releases a free hydrogen**, acidifying the area and irritating nerve endings. Evidence check: the ATP-proton explanation is one side of a published debate (Robergs 2004); in humans, the sensations of muscle fatigue and ache came from protons, lactate and ATP acting together, not from any one alone (Pollak 2014).
- ✅ **The Cori cycle** (identified by the husband-and-wife Cori team): muscle or red blood cells release lactate into blood → the liver takes it up → **gluconeogenesis** rebuilds it into glucose.
- 🟢 After roughly **24 hours without food**, all blood glucose must be made from scratch by the liver, and **lactate is the primary carbon source** for it — "beautiful metabolic recycling." Evidence check: gluconeogenesis supplied 64% of glucose output in the first 22 hours of fasting and 96% by about 40 hours (Rothman 1991); the kidneys also contribute, and glycerol and amino acids are sources alongside lactate.
- 🟡 Bikman's claim that lactate "doesn't contribute to any problem whatsoever" is stated strongly and without qualification in this lecture; carry it as his position, not a settled blanket rule. Lactate is now seen mainly as a fuel and signal shuttled between tissues (Brooks 2018), though a high blood lactate remains a marker of serious illness such as shock.

## Gluconeogenesis and the Futile-Cycle Rule
- The three one-way glycolytic enzymes (reactions 1, 3, 9) are the **regulation points**; gluconeogenesis uses **detour enzymes** that bypass them (three steps need detours). ✅
- **Reciprocal regulation:** insulin turns the three glycolytic enzymes **on** and the detour enzymes **off**; glucagon does exactly the reverse. Insulin and glucagon are never high together, so the cell never runs both directions at once. 🟡 Evidence check: they can be high together; glucagon is inappropriately raised in poorly controlled diabetes even when insulin is high (Unger 2012).
- A cycle that builds and breaks the same thing simultaneously is a **futile cycle** — "the cell abhors the lack of efficiency." ✅
- The cell's own energy state also regulates: plenty of ATP inhibits the glycolytic enzymes. ✅
- 🟢 Practical logic: after a starchy meal, insulin is up and glucose is burned; with fasting or carbohydrate restriction, glucagon is up, the body shifts toward fat burning, and the liver makes the small amount of glucose that cells without mitochondria (like red blood cells) still require.

## Lipolysis → Beta-Oxidation → Acetyl-CoA
- **The fat cell:** one giant lipid droplet of **triglyceride** (glycerol backbone plus fatty acids) pushes the nucleus and organelles to the edge. A **phospholipid monolayer** (tails inward, heads outward) coats the droplet and guards it. ✅
- **Lipolysis** = lipase enzymes pulling free fatty acids off the triglyceride. It happens only once hormones tell the phospholipid guards to "move out of the way." **High insulin keeps the guards in place; low insulin lets lipolysis proceed.** ✅ 🟢 More precisely, the "guards" are proteins such as perilipin-1 on the phospholipid surface; adrenaline-type signals change them so the lipases can work, and insulin opposes that signal (Lass 2011).
- The fat cell has **no direct nerve link to muscle** — a working muscle changes the body's hormones, and those hormones carry the signal back to the fat cell. ✅ 🟢 Evidence check: fat tissue does have its own sympathetic nerve supply, a main driver of lipolysis (Bartness 2014).
- **Three fates of a free fatty acid** once it reaches a cell: (1) become **phospholipids** for membranes; (2) **re-esterification** back into new triglyceride — this is what drives **fatty liver disease** when insulin-resistant fat cells leak fat and high insulin plus high free fatty acids tell the liver to store it; (3) **beta-oxidation** — the actual burning. ✅ In people with fatty liver, about 59% of liver fat came from fatty acids released by fat tissue, 26% from new fat made in the liver and 15% from the diet (Donnelly 2005).
- **CAT1 and CAT2 (carnitine acyl transferase; current names CPT1 and CPT2, carnitine palmitoyltransferase)** on the outer and inner mitochondrial membranes move fatty acids in; it is the carrier, carnitine, that cycles back out. This is a regulated checkpoint. ✅
- **Beta-oxidation** cuts the carbon chain after the beta carbon, two carbons at a time, each piece becoming **acetyl-CoA** — the same molecule glucose becomes after pyruvate dehydrogenase. ✅

## The Citrate Cycle (Krebs Cycle)
- Named after **Sir Hans Krebs** (Nobel Prize); "citrate cycle" is more precise because **citrate is the first molecule made**. It is "cyclic" because its components are reused again and again, though not arranged in a physical circle. ✅
- **Every** oxidative (mitochondrial) fuel passes through it — glucose, fat, **ketones**, even **lactate** — almost always after conversion to acetyl-CoA. ✅
- **Per acetyl-CoA, one full turn yields:** **1 ATP, 3 NADH, 1 FADH₂ and 2 CO₂.** ✅ The two CO₂ equal the two carbons that came in with acetyl-CoA (strictly, the carbons released in a given turn come from oxaloacetate, and the incoming ones leave in later turns); this is where most exhaled CO₂ originates.
- **The CO₂ loop:** we exhale CO₂ → plants take it up → plants release O₂ → we inhale it for the electron transport system. ✅

## The Fork in the Road — Lipogenesis vs. Ketogenesis (Insulin Decides)
The first reaction (acetyl-CoA → citrate, via citrate synthase) is inhibited when citrate builds up. Acetyl-CoA then needs another fate:

|  | **Insulin HIGH** | **Insulin LOW** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Acetyl-CoA goes to** | **Lipogenesis** (de novo lipogenesis — building fat from scratch, two carbons at a time) | **Ketogenesis** (ketones, fuel for nearly every cell with mitochondria; made only in the liver, which cannot use them itself) |
| **Mechanism** | Citrate leaves the mitochondria into the cytosol and is turned back into acetyl-CoA; an intermediate of lipogenesis (malonyl-CoA) **blocks CAT1 (CPT1)** (McGarry 2001), so fat cannot enter to be burned | No signal to stop lipolysis; fat flows out and is burned or turned to ketones |
| **Net direction** | Build and store | Break down and burn |

✅ A cell does not inherently "know" what to do — it relies on hormonal signals about the body's overall state, and here insulin is the dominant one.

**The no-insulin experiment — type 1 diabetes:**
- ✅ Without insulin there is no brake on lipolysis and no ability to make new fat: a person can overeat enormously and still not gain fat. Untreated type 1 diabetics eat voraciously, waste away, run a high metabolic rate, and lose ketones in breath and urine. They also lose large amounts of glucose, and its calories, in the urine.
- ✅ On starting insulin, intake falls and fat gain begins — hence "fat shots," and the eating disorder **diabulimia** (skipping insulin to stay thin), which produces runaway glucose and **ketoacidosis — explicitly not the same as nutritional ketosis** from fasting or a ketogenic diet. Insulin restriction was linked to about three times higher mortality in women with type 1 diabetes (Goebel-Fabbri 2008).

## Electron Transport System and Oxidative Phosphorylation
- ✅ **Oxygen is extremely electronegative** — it pulls electrons harder than almost anything in the cell. That pull is what powers the whole system.
- Bikman prefers **"system" over "chain"** because **Complex I and Complex II are not linked**: Complex I takes electrons from **NADH**, Complex II takes electrons from **FADH₂** (its own "direct deposit"). Both hand electrons to **ubiquinone (CoQ10)**, which carries them to **Complex III**, then (via cytochrome c) **Complex IV**. ✅
- **Proton pumping** into the intermembrane space (between inner and outer membranes): Complex I = 4, Complex II = 0, Complex III = 4, Complex IV = 2 (the lecture swaps III and IV; the totals are unchanged). ✅

| Electron source | Enters at | Protons pumped | ATP equivalent |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **NADH** | Complex I | 4 + 4 + 2 = **10** | **~2.5 ATP** |
| **FADH₂** | Complex II (skips I) | 0 + 4 + 2 = **6** | **~1.5 ATP** |

- ✅ At Complex IV, **oxygen** finally receives the electrons — and picks up protons so it stays neutral rather than becoming a damaging **superoxide / reactive oxygen species** — forming **water**.
- ✅ **ATP synthase** (Bikman refuses to call it "Complex V") is the only way protons flow back into the matrix; **every 4 protons through it make 1 ATP** by attaching a free phosphate to ADP. That is why 10 protons ≈ 2.5 ATP and 6 ≈ 1.5. 🟢 Evidence check: the measured yields of about 2.5 and 1.5 hold (Hinkle 2005), but the accounting differs: about 2.7 protons pass through the synthase per ATP in mammals, plus about 1 more to import phosphate and export the ATP (Watt 2010). Some protons also leak back without making ATP, which releases heat.
- ✅ The ATP tally per citrate-cycle turn: 3 × 2.5 + 1 × 1.5 + 1 = **10 ATP**.
- **CoQ10 comes from cholesterol.** 🔴 (Bikman's claim) Cholesterol is converted into ubiquinone, the electron carrier. Reduced cholesterol synthesis means reduced ubiquinone, so the electron transport system suffers. Evidence check: CoQ10 is not made from cholesterol. The two share the early mevalonate pathway, which is why statins, which block that pathway, also lower CoQ10 levels (Littarru 2007).

## Plants in Reverse — Chloroplast vs. Mitochondrion
- Mitochondria are long and stringy ("mitos" = thread; textbook bean shapes are slices through a string). Chloroplasts look like **stacked contact lenses**. Plant cells have a little mitochondria too, but also chloroplasts. ✅
- The chloroplast runs the mirror image: it takes in **CO₂ plus solar energy** and binds carbons into chains, releasing oxygen. The two systems keep each other in balance. ✅
- 🟡 Bikman adds that rising CO₂ is "blossoming" plant growth because CO₂ is plant fuel; this is a side comment, not developed. 🟢 Evidence check: satellite data show substantial global greening since the 1980s, about 70% of it attributed to CO₂ fertilization (Zhu 2016).

## Q&A Addendum — Statins and Cancer Glucose Use
**Statins:** inhibit **HMG-CoA reductase**, the enzyme that makes cholesterol; every cell can make cholesterol, so dietary cholesterol does not set blood cholesterol. 🟡 Evidence check: the effect is modest but real; most intervention studies link intakes above current averages with higher LDL cholesterol (Carson 2020). Per Bikman, consequences include muscle pain/breakdown (likely mitochondrial damage via low ubiquinone), possible effects on the heart, and reduced sex-hormone production; hence he says people on statins should also take CoQ10. 🟡 These are Bikman's stated positions; the lecture does not quantify any of them. Evidence check: in blinded trials of about 155,000 people, statins caused a small excess of mostly mild muscle pain in the first year (about 11 extra cases per 1,000 person-years), and over 90% of muscle symptoms reported on statins were not due to the drug (CTT 2022). Meta-analyses of CoQ10 for statin muscle symptoms disagree (no benefit in Banach 2015; some benefit in Qu 2018). Statins lowered testosterone by about 0.66 nmol/L in men (Schooling 2013).
- 🟡 **His own paper:** statin efficacy was not predicted by LDL level alone — in people with high LDL, statins appeared to help only if **triglycerides were also high**. He says people on low-carb, ketogenic or carnivore diets may have high LDL with low triglycerides and gain no benefit. The paper is a literature review by Diamond, Bikman and Mason (Diamond 2022). Evidence check: large trial meta-analyses show that each 1 mmol/L LDL reduction cuts major vascular events by just over a fifth (CTT 2010); no trial has tested statins specifically in people on low-carbohydrate diets.
- 🟡 He also asserts high LDL is a persistent feature of long-lived people. Worth carrying as a claim to verify, not a conclusion. Evidence check: a systematic review found high LDL associated with lower mortality in people over 60 (Ravnskov 2016), but such cohorts are prone to reverse causation (illness lowers cholesterol); in a large Copenhagen cohort, higher LDL raised heart-attack risk in people aged 70 to 100 (Mortensen 2020).

**Cancer and glucose:** the source of the glucose (liver-made vs. dietary) does not matter. ✅ Cancer cells use glucose at **about 200× the normal rate** (the basis of PET scans, which use radiolabeled glucose to find tumors) 🟡. The 200× figure is unverified; tumor glucose uptake on PET varies widely by tumor type. Bikman describes **Thomas Seyfried's "glucose ketone index"** — aim for glucose and ketones near 1:1 — with reported shrinkage in some brain cancers like glioblastoma and improved efficacy of chemotherapeutics. 🟡 Presented as emerging evidence from the lecturer, not as clinical guidance. Evidence check: the glucose ketone index comes from Seyfried's group; a 2026 systematic review of ketogenic diets in glioma found that the two randomized trials showed no survival difference and concluded that benefit is unproven (Persiani 2026).

## Key Corrections to Carry Forward
- 🔴 "Metabolism" means only burning calories: it is the balance of catabolism **and** anabolism, linked by ATP.
- 🔴 "Lactic acid builds up in muscle and causes the burn": animals make **lactate**, and the burn tracks ATP breakdown and acidity from released hydrogen, not lactate.
- 🔴 "Lipolysis is fat burning": lipolysis only releases fatty acids; **beta-oxidation in mitochondria** is the burning.
- 🔴 "Excess calories make fat regardless of hormones": with no insulin, fat storage is impossible however much is eaten; **insulin determines lipogenesis vs. ketogenesis**.
- 🔴 "Ketoacidosis and ketosis are the same": ketoacidosis (uncontrolled type 1) is not nutritional ketosis.
- 🔴 "The electron transport chain is a linear chain": Complexes I and II feed in separately via NADH and FADH₂.

## Practical Takeaway
Whether your cells are building or burning depends on one signal you can influence: insulin. High insulin: store, make fat, block fat entry to mitochondria. Low insulin: release fat, burn it, make ketones.

C. Whether the cell contains more NADH than FADH₂
*Why B: Bikman stresses that a cell does not inherently know what to do and relies on hormones; insulin is the dominant signal governing this fork.*

**2. Given that CoQ10 (ubiquinone) is made through the same pathway as cholesterol and carries electrons to Complex III — what is a sensible question to raise with a clinician if a statin is ever proposed?**
A. Whether CoQ10 is irrelevant because the electron transport system works without it
B. Whether LDL alone is sufficient grounds, since triglycerides play no role in statin benefit
C. \* Whether triglycerides are also elevated, given Bikman's paper that benefit tracked high LDL plus high triglycerides, and how reduced ubiquinone production might affect mitochondrial and muscle function
*Why C: this combines the lecture's CoQ10 mechanism with the Q&A claim; both are Bikman's positions (🟡) and are framed as questions for a clinician, not conclusions.*

**3. Given that NADH pumps 10 protons and FADH₂ pumps 6, with 4 protons needed per ATP — roughly how much ATP does each yield?**
A. \* About 2.5 ATP per NADH and about 1.5 ATP per FADH₂
B. About 10 ATP per NADH and 6 per FADH₂
C. Both yield exactly 1 ATP
*Why A: ATP synthase makes one ATP per four protons, so 10 ÷ 4 = 2.5 and 6 ÷ 4 = 1.5; Complex II skips Complex I's four protons.*

**4. Given that lactate is not lactic acid — which statement best explains the "burn" in a hard-working muscle?**
A. Lactic acid accumulates and must be cleared
B. \* The high-demand state both produces lactate and splits huge amounts of ATP, releasing hydrogen that acidifies the area and irritates nerves — lactate is coincident, not causal
C. Lactate is a toxin that damages nerve endings
*Why B: Bikman presents lactate as a fuel recycled by the liver via the Cori cycle; the pain comes from the ATP-splitting hydrogen.*
