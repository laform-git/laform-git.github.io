
# Bikman — Basics of Biology, Ch.3: Biomolecules as Building Blocks
*The four organic molecules every cell is built from (carbohydrates, lipids, proteins, nucleic acids), how a protein folds into a working machine, and how enzymes make the cell's hundreds of thousands of reactions possible, with glucokinase as the worked example.*

*Source: Dr. Benjamin Bikman, Peterson Academy course "Basics of Biology," Lecture 3 (transcript). Third chapter of a six-lecture foundations course; follows Ch.1 (The Cell) and Ch.2 (Crossing the Cell Wall). Separate course from the "Why We Get Sick" and "Essentials of Endocrinology" series by the same lecturer, but it supplies the molecular vocabulary both rely on.*

**In plain words:** the body is built from four kinds of molecules: carbohydrates (sugars and starches), lipids (fats), proteins, and nucleic acids (DNA and RNA). This lecture shows what each one is made of and what it does. It also introduces enzymes, the proteins that make chemical reactions in the body happen fast enough to keep us alive.

**Words in this note:**
- **mRNA**: messenger RNA, the copy of a gene the cell reads to build a protein
- **GLP-1**: a gut hormone that lowers appetite and blood sugar; also a class of drugs (such as semaglutide)
- **DNA**: the genetic code inside each cell
- **RNA**: a working copy of genetic code
- **ATP**: adenosine triphosphate, the cell's energy currency
- **GLUT5**: the gut's door for fructose
- **FDA**: U.S. Food and Drug Administration
- **ATPase**: an enzyme that uses ATP for energy
- **TRT**: testosterone replacement therapy
- **DHEA**: a hormone made by the adrenal glands that the body turns into sex hormones
- **ApoB**: apolipoprotein B; counts all the cholesterol particles that can clog arteries

## Why This Matters
Every later topic in this course (how genes make proteins, how cells divide, how cells get energy) and in the other Bikman series is built from these four families of molecules and from enzymes. Once you can sort any molecule into carbohydrate, lipid, protein or nucleic acid, and you know that enzymes (whose names end in -ase) are what actually do the work, words like triglyceride, peptide hormone, glycolysis or mRNA stop being jargon and start making sense. It also explains some everyday facts: why insulin and most GLP-1 drugs (the popular weight-loss injections) have to be injected, why sugar and starch must be "snipped" into pieces before the gut can absorb them, and why cholesterol is more than a number on a blood test.

## What Makes a Molecule "Biological" — Carbon
- **Biomolecules** are the carbon-based (organic) molecules found in living things. They are not alive themselves (they do not meet the life checklist from Ch.1), but every cell needs them. ✅
- What they share is **carbon**. Carbon forms strong bonds with many elements, including other carbon atoms, oxygen and hydrogen. It can build both **chains and rings**, which is why it can make such a wide variety of shapes. ✅
- Bikman mentions that a later lecture (on cell energy) will show how carbon gets into biomolecules using energy from the sun. Here he only makes the point that carbon is essential. 🟢

## The Four Biomolecules — Side by Side

| Feature | Carbohydrates | Lipids (fats) | Proteins | Nucleic acids |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Elements** | Carbon, hydrogen and oxygen in fairly even amounts | Lots of carbon, even more hydrogen, very little oxygen | Carbon chains plus a nitrogen group (amine) on each amino acid | Phosphate + a sugar with five carbons + a nitrogen-containing base |
| **How they behave in water** | Polar (carry a slight charge), water-loving, dissolve well; cannot slip through membranes, so need transporters | Non-polar (no charge), avoid water; pack a lot of energy | Not described this way in the lecture | Not described this way in the lecture |
| **Building block** | Monosaccharide, a single sugar (glucose, fructose, galactose) | Depends on the class (glycerol + fatty acids, cholesterol ring, and so on) | 20 amino acids joined by peptide bonds | Nucleotides (DNA and RNA) |
| **Main jobs** | Energy; "ID flags" on the cell surface | Stored energy, membranes, signals, steroid hormones | Enzymes, receptors, channels, transporters, structure (the cell's skeleton) | Store genetic information (DNA) and put it to use (RNA). ATP, the cell's energy molecule, is a single nucleotide (a building block of nucleic acids, not a nucleic acid itself) |

✅ The "polymer" idea ties the table together. A **polymer** is a chain of linked units. Starch is a chain of glucose, a protein is a chain of amino acids, and DNA and RNA are chains of nucleotides.

## Carbohydrates
- **The name explained:** carbo (carbon) + hydrate (hydrogen and oxygen, the elements of water). Carbohydrates contain only those three elements. ✅
- **Three sizes:** a monosaccharide is a single sugar: glucose, fructose (fruit sugar) or galactose. Only this size is small enough to absorb. Glucose and galactose get in through the sodium-linked door and the back-side door from Ch.2; fructose uses its own door, GLUT5 (Koepsell 2020). A disaccharide is two sugars joined: lactose (milk sugar) = glucose + galactose; sucrose (table sugar) = glucose + fructose. A polysaccharide is a long chain of glucose: the starch in bread, crackers, cereal and potatoes. ✅
- Larger carbohydrates must be **snipped apart** by digestive enzymes before the gut can absorb them. ✅
- **More than energy:** some carbohydrates stick up from the cell surface as ID flags, so immune cells recognize the cell as "self." Bikman links a failure of this recognition to autoimmune disease, where the body attacks itself (carried over from the membrane lecture). ✅

## Lipids (Fats)
- There are many classes and huge variety. Bikman recalls a colleague calling fat chemistry "the most complicated realm of chemistry." 🟢

| Class | Structure / key idea | Role |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Glycerolipids** (triglyceride) | A glycerol backbone with three fatty acids attached. No charge at all (unlike phospholipids, which have a water-loving end) | The only class used for energy; the fat you can pinch |
| **Phospholipids** | A phosphate head + two fatty acids | Build the membranes of the cell and its inner rooms |
| **Sphingolipids** | Named after the mysterious sphinx (their job was long unknown) | Send signals; tell a cell to live or die; involved in how cells respond to insulin (ceramides, one type, are linked to insulin resistance; Chaurasia 2021) |
| **Steroids** | Built from cholesterol; anything that becomes a steroid started as cholesterol | Sex hormones (estrogens, androgens such as testosterone), cortisol, aldosterone (a salt-balancing hormone), and, as he says, a cholesterol-like molecule the cell's energy chain needs (coenzyme Q10; in fact it is not a steroid and is not made from cholesterol, but it shares the first steps of the body's cholesterol-making pathway, Littarru 2007) |

- **Why fat has more calories:** fat has lots of carbon-carbon bonds, and that means lots of stored chemical energy. A calorie is simply a unit of energy. ✅ 🟢 More exactly, fat is packed with carbon-hydrogen bonds and has little oxygen, so it gives about 9 kcal per gram against about 4 for carbohydrate or protein.
- **The phospholipid guard:** inside a fat cell, the droplet of triglyceride is wrapped in phospholipids that "protect" it. A chain of events (covered in the cell energy lecture) has to move the phospholipids aside before the fat can be broken down. 🟡 Only the idea is given here; the details come later. 🟢 Evidence check: the surface of the droplet is a single layer of phospholipids coated with proteins such as perilipin-1. Hormone signals change these proteins so that the fat-cutting enzymes (lipases called ATGL and HSL) can reach the triglyceride (Lass 2011).

## Proteins
- **The cell's main machines.** When work gets done, a protein usually does it: enzymes, receptors (on the surface and inside the cell, even for steroid hormones that pass through the membrane), channels, transporters, and structural parts like the cell's skeleton (cytoskeleton). ✅
- **A chain of amino acids.** There are only **20 amino acids**, combined and folded in countless ways. Each has a nitrogen group (an amino group). Protein you eat is too big to absorb, so it is broken down into amino acids first. ✅
- **Some amino acids become other molecules:** thyroid hormone is built from tyrosine; melatonin (the sleep hormone) from tryptophan. ✅
- **Branched-chain amino acids (BCAAs)** are a group of three amino acids. According to Bikman, only leucine has been shown to directly affect muscle. 🟡 (a side comment, given as his claim) Evidence check: leucine is the main amino acid that switches on muscle building. But a review found no human study showing that BCAAs alone build muscle, because building muscle needs all the essential amino acids (Wolfe 2017).
- **Size words (as he gives them):** amino acid (one) → peptide (two) → polypeptide (up to about 20) → protein (more than 50). 🟡 These cut-offs are loose in the lecture and leave a gap between 20 and 50, so treat them as rough.

### Four Levels of Protein Structure

| Level | What it is | Does it work yet? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Primary** | A straight "string of pearls" of amino acids | No; all the parts, but not yet a usable protein |
| **Secondary** | Alpha helix (a candy-cane spiral) and beta sheet (a pleated sheet) | No, still in between |
| **Tertiary** | Helices and sheets wrapped around each other; insulin is his example | Yes, the minimum for a useful protein |
| **Quaternary** | Several tertiary proteins linked together; hemoglobin, the oxygen carrier in red blood cells | Not all proteins need to go this far |

- **Why protein hormones must be injected:** insulin swallowed as a pill, even in its perfect working shape, would be unwound and cut by digestive enzymes into amino acids, which the body then reuses for whatever it likes. The same goes for GLP-1 agonists, the widely used weight-loss drugs, which are peptides and are therefore injected. ✅ 🟢 Evidence check: there are now pill exceptions. Oral semaglutide (Rybelsus), made absorbable with a helper ingredient, was approved by the FDA for type 2 diabetes in 2019. A Wegovy pill for weight loss followed in December 2025. And orforglipron (Foundayo), a GLP-1 drug that is a small chemical rather than a peptide, was approved in April 2026 (FDA 2019; ABC News 2025; Healio 2026). Insulin itself is still injected.

## Nucleic Acids
- A broad family. It includes ATP (the cell's key energy molecule, covered later) and the **nucleotides** that store genetic information: **DNA and RNA**. ✅
- **Every nucleotide has three parts:** a phosphate, a ring-shaped sugar with five carbons (ribose or deoxyribose), and a nitrogen-containing base. The base is what makes each nucleotide different. ✅

| Feature | DNA | RNA |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Sugar** | Deoxyribose (just a hydrogen where RNA has an oxygen-hydrogen group) | Ribose (one extra oxygen, as a hydroxyl group) |
| **Bases** | A, G, C, T | A, G, C, and **U** (uracil) in place of T |
| **Strands** | Two strands running in opposite directions, twisted into a double helix, kept in the nucleus | One strand (messenger RNA) |
| **Job** | Stores the genetic code | Puts the code to use; three types: messenger (mRNA), transfer (tRNA), ribosomal (rRNA) |

- **Purines and pyrimidines:** purines (A, G) have two rings and are longer. Pyrimidines (C, T) have one ring and are shorter. A long one always pairs with a short one: **A with T, C with G** (A with U in RNA). This is called complementary base pairing. ✅
- **Anti-parallel:** the two strands run in opposite directions, and that direction decides which way the code is read (explained in the central dogma lecture). ✅
- Watson and Crick described the double helix in 1953, using X-ray data from Rosalind Franklin, and shared the 1962 Nobel Prize with Maurice Wilkins (Watson 1953; Franklin 1953; Nobel Prize 1962). ✅

## Enzymes — Doing the Work of the Cell
- **Why they are needed:** even a simple red blood cell runs hundreds of thousands of reactions, and the body has about 30 trillion cells of about 200 types. Without enzymes, most of these reactions would not happen fast enough to matter. ✅ 🟡 Evidence check: the figures are loose. The most complete map of human body chemistry lists about 13,500 different reactions for the whole body (Brunk 2018), and a red blood cell, with no nucleus or mitochondria, runs far fewer. The cell count is about 30 to 36 trillion (Hatton 2023).
- **Types of chemical bond:** a **covalent bond** shares electrons and is very strong. A **hydrogen bond** is only a weak electrical attraction (heat can break it, as when water boils). A cell cannot boil itself, so enzymes do the job instead. ✅
- **What an enzyme does:** it lowers the energy barrier that would otherwise stop a reaction, so the reaction happens now instead of someday. ✅

### Rules of Enzymes
- **Specific:** usually one enzyme for one reaction and one substrate (the molecule it works on).
- **Not changed and not used up:** after each reaction it is ready to go again.
- **Name ends in -ase:** sucrase cuts sucrose into glucose + fructose; ATPase builds or breaks ATP. If a word ends in -ase, it is an enzyme. The reverse is not always true: some enzymes keep older names without -ase (pepsin, trypsin, lysozyme).
- **Amount of substrate:** more substrate means a faster reaction, up to the point where the enzyme is working flat out (saturated).
- **Grip and competition:** the enzyme must grip its substrate strongly enough to pull it in (its affinity), and another molecule that also fits can compete for the same spot.

### Worked Example — Glucokinase, the First Step of Glycolysis
Glycolysis is the cell's first pathway for burning glucose.
1. Glucose enters the cell, and glucokinase (also called hexokinase in the lecture) grabs it.
2. The enzyme attaches a phosphate with a strong (covalent) bond, making **glucose 6-phosphate** (the phosphate sits on carbon number 6).
3. The phosphate **locks glucose inside** the cell (otherwise it could slip back out through its door) and commits it to the glucose-burning pathway.
4. The enzyme lets go of the product and is ready again.
5. **Slowed by its own product:** the enzyme has some grip on its own product, so a pile-up of glucose 6-phosphate can sit in the binding spot and slow the next reactions (product inhibition). 🔴 Evidence check: this is true of hexokinases I-III (in muscle, brain and most tissues), not of glucokinase, the form in the liver and the insulin-making beta cells, which is not slowed by its product (Matschinsky 2019).

- He calls this the **rate-limiting step**: the slowest step, which sets the pace of the whole glycolysis pathway. ✅ for the definition. 🟡 Evidence check: the step usually named as the main rate-limiting, committing step of glycolysis is phosphofructokinase-1 (step 3), not the hexokinase step (Yuan 2025).
- 🟡 In this lecture Bikman uses "hexokinase or glucokinase" as if they were one enzyme; treat that as his simplification.

## Punchlines & Quotable Facts (Lecture 3)
- **"Carbon is essential to the chemistry of life."** Chains and rings built on strong bonds with many elements.
- **One hydrogen separates DNA from RNA** in the sugar ring (deoxy = just a hydrogen).
- **A shopping-list rule:** any word ending in **-ase** is an enzyme.
- **Anything that becomes a steroid was first a cholesterol.**
- **Of the three branched-chain amino acids, only leucine** has shown a direct effect on muscle (his claim).
- **Protein hormones cannot be swallowed**, because digestion cuts the peptide bonds. Insulin and most GLP-1 drugs are injected (oral semaglutide and orforglipron are exceptions).
- **Enzymes are not changed and not used up.** The molecule they work on changes, not the enzyme.

## Key Corrections to Carry Forward
- 🔴 Thinking carbohydrates are "just energy": some act as ID flags on cells, and a failure to recognize "self" is the link to autoimmune disease.
- 🔴 Thinking "fat" means one thing: there are four distinct classes of lipids, and only glycerolipids (triglycerides) are used for energy. Phospholipids, sphingolipids and steroids do other work.
- 🔴 Thinking cholesterol is only a lab number: it is the starting molecule for every steroid, including sex hormones, cortisol and aldosterone.
- 🔴 Thinking a protein works as soon as its amino acids are linked: the straight chain is not usable; the tertiary (folded) shape is the minimum for it to work.
- 🔴 Thinking an enzyme gets used up: it is neither changed nor used up.

## Practical Takeaway
When you meet a new molecule, ask four questions. Which family is it in? Is it charged or uncharged (can it cross a membrane, or does it need a transporter)? Is it a chain that must be broken down before the gut can absorb it? Is there an -ase enzyme that acts on it? That framework covers most of what the remaining lectures and the hormone series will bring up.

## Questions to Learn From (Socratic) — with Answer Options
**1. A friend asks why someone with diabetes cannot just swallow an insulin pill. Using this lecture, what is the best explanation?**
A. Insulin is a fat, and fats cannot be absorbed in the gut
B. Insulin is too charged to cross any membrane, so it is blocked in the stomach
C. \* Insulin is a protein held together by peptide bonds; digestive enzymes would cut it into amino acids, even when it is swallowed in its working folded shape
*Why C: the lecture uses exactly this example. Digestion unwinds and cuts the chain, and the body would rebuild the amino acids into other proteins, not insulin. The same logic applies to GLP-1 drugs.*

**2. Glucokinase adds a phosphate to glucose and makes glucose 6-phosphate. Which statement about this step is correct?**
A. \* It locks glucose inside the cell and commits it to glycolysis, and the enzyme is left unchanged and ready for the next glucose
B. The enzyme is used up in the reaction, so the cell must make a new glucokinase each time
C. It helps glucose cross the membrane more easily because the phosphate makes it less charged
*Why A: the phosphate traps glucose (otherwise it could leave through its door) and the enzyme is neither changed nor used up. Adding the phosphate does not help glucose cross; it keeps it in.*

**3. Which pairing correctly describes a difference between DNA and RNA?**
A. DNA contains uracil and RNA contains thymine
B. \* RNA has a hydroxyl (oxygen-hydrogen) group on its sugar and uses uracil where DNA uses thymine
C. DNA is single-stranded and RNA is a double helix
*Why B: the one difference on the sugar (hydroxyl in RNA, just hydrogen in DNA) and the U-for-T swap are both stated. DNA is the two-stranded helix; RNA (mRNA) is a single strand.*

**4. Of the four lipid classes in the lecture, which one is actually used for energy, and what is its structure?**
A. Phospholipids: a phosphate head with two fatty acids
B. Steroids: four rings built on cholesterol
C. \* Glycerolipids (triglycerides): a glycerol backbone with three fatty acids
*Why C: Bikman says none of the other fat classes are used for energy. Phospholipids build membranes (and guard the fat droplet), and steroids become hormones.*
