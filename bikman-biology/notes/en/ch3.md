
# Bikman — Basics of Biology, Ch.3: Biomolecules as Building Blocks
*The four organic molecules every cell is built from (carbohydrates, lipids, proteins, nucleic acids), how a protein folds into a working machine, and how enzymes make the cell's hundreds of thousands of reactions possible, with glucokinase as the worked example.*

*Source: Dr. Benjamin Bikman, Peterson Academy course "Basics of Biology," Lecture 3 (transcript). Third chapter of a six-lecture foundations course; follows Ch.1 (The Cell) and Ch.2 (Crossing the Cell Wall). Separate course from the "Why We Get Sick" and "Essentials of Endocrinology" series by the same lecturer, but it supplies the molecular vocabulary both rely on.*

## Why This Matters
Every later topic in this course (central dogma, cell cycle, metabolism) and in the other Bikman series is built from these four molecule families and from enzymes. Once you can sort any molecule into carbohydrate, lipid, protein, or nucleic acid, and know that enzymes (always ending in -ase) are what actually get work done, terms like triglyceride, peptide hormone, glycolysis, or mRNA stop being jargon and become predictable. It also explains several practical facts you already live with: why insulin and GLP-1 drugs must be injected, why sugar and starch must be "snipped" before absorption, and why cholesterol is more than a lipid-panel number.

## What Makes a Molecule "Biological" — Carbon
- **Biomolecules** are organic molecules found in living things. They are not themselves alive (they do not meet the criteria for life from Ch.1), but every cell needs them. ✅
- The shared feature is **carbon**. Carbon forms strong bonds with many elements, including other carbon, oxygen, and hydrogen, and can build both **chains and rings**, which is why it supports such diverse structures. ✅
- Bikman previews that a later lecture (cellular energy) will show how carbon enters biomolecules via energy from the sun; here it is only the "essentiality of carbon" point. 🟢

## The Four Biomolecules — Side-by-Side

| Feature | Carbohydrates | Lipids | Proteins | Nucleic acids |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Elements** | Carbon, hydrogen, oxygen in a fairly balanced ratio | Lots of carbon, even more hydrogen, very little oxygen | Carbon chains plus an amine (nitrogen) group on each amino acid | Phosphate + five-carbon sugar + nitrogenous base |
| **Water behavior** | Polar, hydrophilic, dissolves well; cannot slip through membranes, so needs transporters | Non-polar, hydrophobic; energy dense | Not framed by polarity in this lecture | Not framed by polarity in this lecture |
| **Building unit** | Monosaccharide (glucose, fructose, galactose) | Varies by class (glycerol + fatty acids, cholesterol ring, etc.) | 20 amino acids joined by peptide bonds | Nucleotides (DNA and RNA) |
| **Main jobs** | Energy; cell-identity "flags" on the membrane surface | Stored energy, membranes, signaling, steroid hormones | Enzymes, receptors, channels, transporters, structure (cytoskeleton) | Store genetic information (DNA) and express it (RNA); ATP is an energetic nucleic acid |

✅ The "polymer" idea ties the table together: a **polymer** is a chain of bound units. Starch is a polymer of glucose, a protein is a polymer of amino acids, DNA/RNA are polymers of nucleotides.

## Carbohydrates
- **Name decoded:** carbo (carbon) + hydrate (hydrogen and oxygen, the elements of water). Only those three elements. ✅
- **Three sizes:** monosaccharide (glucose, fructose, galactose; the only form small enough to absorb, via the sodium symport and back-side transport from Ch.2), disaccharide (lactose = glucose + galactose; sucrose = glucose + fructose), polysaccharide (long glucose polymers: starch in bread, crackers, cereal, potatoes). ✅
- Larger carbohydrates must be **snipped apart** by digestive enzymes before absorption. ✅
- **Beyond energy:** some carbohydrates stick up from the cell surface as identity flags so immune cells recognize "self." Bikman links failure of this recognition to autoimmune disease (carried over from the membrane lecture). ✅

## Lipids
- Many classes, enormous variety; Bikman recalls a colleague calling lipid chemistry "the most complicated realm of chemistry." 🟢

| Class | Structure / key idea | Role |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Glycerolipids** (triglyceride) | Glycerol backbone + three fatty acids; amphipathic | The only class used for energy; the fat you can pinch |
| **Phospholipids** | Phosphate head + two fatty acids | Build cell and organelle membranes |
| **Sphingolipids** | Named for the enigmatic sphinx (function long unknown) | Signaling; tell a cell to live or die; involved in insulin response |
| **Steroids** | Built on cholesterol; anything that becomes a steroid started as cholesterol | Sex hormones (estrogens, androgens such as testosterone), cortisol, aldosterone, and a tweaked cholesterol-like molecule the electron transport system needs |

- **Why fat carries more calories:** lots of carbon-carbon bonds means lots of stored chemical energy; a calorie is simply a unit of energy. ✅
- **Phospholipid guard:** a fat cell's triglyceride droplet is surrounded by phospholipids that "protect" it; a series of events (to be covered in the cellular energy lecture) must move the phospholipids aside before the triglycerides can be broken down. 🟡 Mechanism deferred, only the concept is given here.

## Proteins
- **The cell's main machines.** When work gets done, a protein usually does it: enzymes, receptors (surface and intracellular, even for steroid hormones that cross the membrane), channels, transporters, and structural parts like the cytoskeleton. ✅
- **Polymer of amino acids.** Only **20 amino acids**, combined and folded in countless ways. Each has an amino (nitrogen) group. Eaten protein is too big to absorb and is broken down to amino acids. ✅
- **Some amino acids become other molecules:** thyroid hormone is built on tyrosine; melatonin on tryptophan. ✅
- **Branched-chain amino acids** are three amino acids; per Bikman only leucine has been shown to directly affect muscle. ✅ (his tangent, stated as his claim)
- **Size vocabulary (as given):** amino acid (single) → peptide (two) → polypeptide (up to about 20) → protein (more than 50). 🟡 These cut-offs are stated loosely in the lecture and leave a gap between 20 and 50, so treat them as rough.

### Four Levels of Protein Structure

| Level | What it is | Functional? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Primary** | Straight "string of pearls" of amino acids | No; all parts, not yet a protein you can use |
| **Secondary** | Alpha helix (candy-cane spiral) and beta (pleated) sheet | No, still intermediate |
| **Tertiary** | Helices and sheets wrapped around each other; insulin is his example | Yes, the minimum for a useful protein |
| **Quaternary** | Several tertiary proteins linked together; hemoglobin in the red blood cell | Not all proteins need to get this far |

- **Why peptide hormones must be injected:** swallowed insulin, even in perfect tertiary form, is unwound and cut by digestive enzymes into amino acids the body reuses as it likes. The same holds for GLP-1 agonists, the widely used weight-loss drugs, which are peptides and therefore injected. ✅

## Nucleic Acids
- A broad family that includes ATP (the cell's key energy molecule, covered later) and the **nucleotides** that store genetic information: **DNA and RNA**. ✅
- **Every nucleotide has three parts:** phosphate, five-carbon sugar ring (ribose or deoxyribose), and a nitrogenous base. The base is what makes each nucleotide unique. ✅

| Feature | DNA | RNA |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Sugar** | Deoxyribose (just a hydrogen where RNA has a hydroxyl) | Ribose (one extra oxygen, a hydroxyl) |
| **Bases** | A, G, C, T | A, G, C, and **U** (uracil) in place of T |
| **Strands** | Double-stranded, anti-parallel, double helix in the nucleus | Single-stranded (messenger RNA) |
| **Job** | Stores the genetic code | Expresses it; three types: messenger (mRNA), transfer (tRNA), ribosomal (rRNA) |

- **Purines vs. pyrimidines:** purines (A, G) have two rings and are longer; pyrimidines (C, T) have one ring and are shorter. A long always pairs with a short: **A with T, C with G** (A with U in RNA). This is complementary base pairing. ✅
- **Anti-parallel:** the two strands run in opposite directions, and orientation determines the direction the code is read (to be developed in the central dogma lecture). ✅
- Watson and Crick identified the double helix and won the Nobel Prize. ✅

## Enzymes — Doing the Work of the Cell
- **Why needed:** even a simple red blood cell runs hundreds of thousands of reactions, and the body has about 30 trillion cells of about 200 types. Without enzymes most of these would not happen on a useful timescale. ✅
- **Bond types:** a **covalent bond** shares electrons and is very strong; a **hydrogen bond** is only an electrostatic attraction and is weak (heat can break it, as when water boils). A cell cannot boil, so enzymes do the job. ✅
- **What an enzyme does:** lowers the energy that would otherwise make a reaction insurmountable, so it happens now instead of eventually. ✅

### Rules of Enzymes
- **Specific:** generally one enzyme per reaction and one substrate.
- **Unchanged and not consumed:** ready to run again after each reaction.
- **Name ends in -ase:** sucrase cuts sucrose into glucose + fructose; ATPase builds or breaks ATP. If it ends in -ase, it is an enzyme.
- **Substrate concentration:** more substrate means a faster reaction rate.
- **Affinity and competition:** binding must be strong enough to pull the substrate in, and another molecule with some affinity can compete for the same site.

### Worked Example — Glucokinase, the First Step of Glycolysis
1. Glucose enters the cell; glucokinase (also called hexokinase in the lecture) binds it.
2. It attaches a phosphate by a covalent bond, making **glucose 6-phosphate** (phosphate on carbon 6).
3. The phosphate **locks glucose inside** (otherwise it could slip back out the transporter) and commits it to the glucose-burning pathway.
4. The enzyme releases the product and is ready again.
5. **Product inhibition:** glucokinase has some affinity for its own product, so a lot of glucose 6-phosphate can occupy the binding site and slow further phosphorylation.

- This is the **rate-limiting step**: the slowest step sets the pace of the whole glycolysis pathway. ✅
- 🟡 Bikman uses "hexokinase or glucokinase" as if they were one enzyme in this lecture; treat that as his simplification.

## Punchlines & Quotable Facts (Lecture 3)
- **"Carbon is essential to the chemistry of life"** — chains and rings built on strong bonds with many elements.
- **One hydrogen separates DNA from RNA** in the sugar ring (deoxy = just a hydrogen).
- **A shopping-list rule:** any word ending in **-ase** is always an enzyme.
- **Anything that becomes a steroid was first a cholesterol.**
- **Of the three branched-chain amino acids, only leucine** has shown a direct muscle effect (his claim).
- **Peptide hormones cannot be swallowed** because digestion cuts the peptide bonds; insulin and GLP-1 drugs are injected.
- **Enzymes are not changed and not consumed** — the molecule they act on changes, not the enzyme.

## Key Corrections to Carry Forward
- 🔴 Assuming carbohydrates are "just energy": some act as cell-identity flags, and failure of self-recognition is the autoimmune link.
- 🔴 Assuming "fat" means one thing: lipids are four distinct classes, and only glycerolipids (triglycerides) are used for energy; phospholipids, sphingolipids, and steroids do other work.
- 🔴 Assuming cholesterol is only a lab number: it is the starting molecule for every steroid, including sex hormones, cortisol, and aldosterone.
- 🔴 Assuming a protein is functional once its amino acids are linked: the primary string is not usable; tertiary structure is the minimum for function.
- 🔴 Assuming an enzyme is used up: it is neither changed nor consumed.

## Practical Takeaway
When you meet a new molecule, ask four questions: which family is it, is it polar or non-polar (can it cross a membrane or does it need a transporter), is it a polymer that must be broken down before absorption, and is there an -ase that acts on it. That framework covers most of what the remaining lectures and the hormone series will throw at you.

## Questions to Learn From (Socratic) — with Answer Options
**1. A friend asks why a diabetic cannot simply swallow an insulin pill. Using this lecture, what is the best explanation?**
A. Insulin is a lipid, and lipids cannot be absorbed in the intestine
B. Insulin is too polar to cross any membrane, so it is blocked in the stomach
C. \* Insulin is a protein held together by peptide bonds; digestive enzymes would cut it into amino acids, even though it is taken in its working tertiary form
*Why C: the lecture uses exactly this example. Digestion unwinds and cuts the polymer, and the body would rebuild the amino acids into other proteins, not insulin. The same logic applies to GLP-1 agonists.*

**2. Glucokinase adds a phosphate to glucose and produces glucose 6-phosphate. Which statement about this step is correct?**
A. \* It locks glucose inside the cell and commits it to glycolysis, and the enzyme is left unchanged and ready for the next glucose
B. The enzyme is consumed in the reaction, so the cell must make a new glucokinase each time
C. It lets glucose cross the membrane more easily because phosphorylation makes it less polar
*Why A: the phosphate traps glucose (otherwise it could leave via the transporter) and the enzyme is neither changed nor consumed. Phosphorylation does not help glucose cross; it keeps it in.*

**3. Which pairing correctly describes a difference between DNA and RNA?**
A. DNA contains uracil and RNA contains thymine
B. \* RNA has a hydroxyl group on its sugar and uses uracil where DNA uses thymine
C. DNA is single-stranded and RNA is a double helix
*Why B: the single difference on the sugar (hydroxyl in RNA, just hydrogen in DNA) and the U-for-T swap are both stated. DNA is the double-stranded helix; RNA (mRNA) is a single strand.*

**4. Of the four lipid classes in the lecture, which is the one actually used as an energy source, and what is its structure?**
A. Phospholipids: a phosphate head with two fatty acids
B. Steroids: four rings built on cholesterol
C. \* Glycerolipids (triglycerides): a glycerol backbone with three fatty acids
*Why C: Bikman states that none of the other lipid classes are used for energy. Phospholipids build membranes (and guard the triglyceride droplet), and steroids become hormones.*
