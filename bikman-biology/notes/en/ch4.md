
# Bikman — Basics of Biology, Ch.4: The Central Dogma
*How genetic information flows from DNA to RNA to protein: replication, transcription (with splicing), translation and post-translational folding, plus Q&A on seed-oil peroxidation, introns and epigenetics.*

*Source: Dr. Benjamin Bikman, Peterson Academy course "Basics of Biology," Lecture 4 (transcript). Fourth of six chapters in a foundational cell-biology course; builds on the biomolecule (Ch.3) and enzyme material and sets up the cell cycle (Ch.5).*

**In plain words:** your genes are written in DNA, but DNA does not do the work itself. The cell first copies a gene into a working copy called RNA, then reads that RNA to build a protein, and the proteins do the actual work. This chain, DNA to RNA to protein, is called the central dogma. This lecture walks through each step: copying DNA before a cell divides, making the RNA copy, trimming it, and building and folding the protein.

**Words in this note:**
- **DNA**: the genetic code inside each cell
- **RNA**: a working copy of genetic code
- **mRNA**: messenger RNA, the copy of a gene the cell reads to build a protein
- **ApoB**: apolipoprotein B; counts all the cholesterol particles that can clog arteries
- **DHEA**: a hormone made by the adrenal glands that the body turns into sex hormones
- **TRT**: testosterone replacement therapy

## Why This Matters
This is the lecture where the earlier pieces (nucleic acids, enzymes, the cell's inner rooms, vesicles) snap into one story: **genes shape the body because DNA is turned into RNA, and RNA is turned into the proteins that actually do the work.** It is background knowledge rather than something you act on directly. The question-and-answer session at the end is where it touches diet and long-term health.

## The Central Dogma — The Big Picture
- **Definition:** the flow of genetic information from **DNA → RNA (a step called transcription) → protein (a step called translation)**. Put simply: how our genes shape our body. ✅
- **Where it came from:** proposed by **Francis Crick (UK) in 1958**, the same person who, with James Watson, worked out the structure of DNA (Crick 1958). ✅
- **Why protein is the end point:** we are made of cells, but what a cell *does* is set by the proteins it makes. Height, body size and shape, skin, hair and eye colour all come from which proteins are made. Bikman says the environment has only a "very modest" influence on this. 🟡 Evidence check: genes explain much of the difference in height between people, but the environment is not minor. Over the 20th century, average adult height rose by about 20 cm in South Korean women and 16.5 cm in Iranian men, mainly through better food and health (NCD-RisC 2016).
- **Genotype vs. phenotype:** genotype is the blueprint for a house; phenotype is the house itself. In biology: the genetic code is the blueprint, and the proteins are the building. ✅
- **Gene:** a stretch of DNA that holds the recipe for one particular product. A chromosome (one long strand of DNA) is *not* genes from end to end. Genes sit in certain places, with long stretches of non-coding "wasted space" between them. ✅
- **Where things happen:** DNA stays in the **nucleus**. RNA leaves into the **cytosol** (the fluid of the cell), where the protein is built. The finished protein either stays in the cell to work or is sent out to carry a signal elsewhere. ✅

## Setup Detail — DNA Has a Direction
- Nucleotides (the letters of DNA): **purines (A, G)** and **pyrimidines (C, T)**. The two strands run **in opposite directions** (anti-parallel). ✅
- Each strand has a **5' end** (pronounced "five prime"; the end with the phosphate) and a **3' end** ("three prime"; the sugar side). One strand reads 3'→5' going up, the other 5'→3'. ✅
- This matters because every copying enzyme in the lecture works in only one fixed direction. That explains why the two strands are copied differently (the leading and lagging strands, below).

## Step 1 — Copying DNA (Replication)
**Why:** before a cell divides (mitosis, next lecture), it needs two identical copies of its genetic information, so a liver cell stays a liver cell and the new cells match. Copying DNA is also what lets parents pass genes to children through eggs and sperm (gametes). ✅

**How it works:** by the **semi-conservative** model, the best-proven of several proposed ideas (Meselson 1958). Each new double helix has **one original strand and one newly made strand**. After many divisions, more and more cells carry none of the *original* physical strand, although every cell holds a copy that works the same. ✅

| Component | Job in copying | Bikman's picture |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Helicase** | Runs along the helix and pries the tightly coiled DNA open | Tugging a car door open |
| **Single-strand binding proteins** | Hold the two strands apart so they cannot snap back together | Books wedged in the gap |
| **Primase** | Marks the starting point: "copying begins here." It lays down a short piece of RNA (a primer), because DNA polymerase cannot start a new strand from nothing | — |
| **DNA polymerase** | Builds the new DNA strand; also checks its own work | — |
| **Ligase** | Seals separate pieces into one continuous strand | — |

- **Replication forks:** the two edges of the opened section, where the DNA is still wound up. ✅
- **The polymerase direction rule:** it reads the original (template) strand **3'→5'**, so it builds the new strand **5'→3'**. ✅
- **Leading strand:** the template strand whose 3' end faces the opening. Here the polymerase can run **smoothly without stopping** as the helicase keeps unzipping. ✅
- **Lagging strand:** the other strand. The polymerase can only work in its one direction, but the helix keeps opening the other way. So it attaches, runs until it bumps into the previous piece, then **skips ahead** and starts again. This makes short pieces called **Okazaki fragments**, which **ligase** then joins together. ✅
- 🟡 Bikman's spoken description of which end the polymerase "starts" from on each strand is hard to follow in the transcript (he was acting it out with his arms). The solid points are the rule (builds 5'→3'), the result (smooth on one strand, in pieces on the other) and the list of enzymes.

**Three ways the copy stays accurate** ✅
1. **Hydrogen bonding:** correct pairs (A-T, G-C) hold together more strongly and form a tighter coil. A wrong pair holds weakly, bends the helix out of shape, and gets noticed and fixed.
2. **The polymerase's working pocket:** it does not easily accept wrong pairs, and it knows where to start and stop.
3. **Proofreading:** the polymerase can look back, spot an error within a fraction of a second, back up, take that section apart and rebuild it.

## Step 2 — Transcription (DNA → mRNA)
**Three types of RNA, each made by its own RNA polymerase** (RNA pol I, II, III): ✅

| RNA type | Job |
| --- | --- |
| **mRNA** (messenger) | The copy of the gene that carries the message out of the nucleus; the star of this step |
| **tRNA** (transfer) | Recognises a specific three-letter code (codon) using its matching end (anticodon) and delivers the right amino acid |
| **rRNA** (ribosomal) | Makes up the ribosome, the machine that reads mRNA and gives tRNA a place to attach |

- **Why a messenger is needed:** DNA never leaves the nucleus, and proteins are built outside it. mRNA carries the instructions out without putting the DNA at risk. ✅
- **Simpler than copying DNA:** one enzyme, **RNA polymerase**, does the jobs of helicase, the binding proteins and the polymerase (Bikman says it effectively does all of them). It reads the template 3'→5' and builds RNA 5'→3'. ✅
- **Not a letter-perfect copy:** RNA uses **uracil (U) in place of thymine (T)**. Bikman skips the theories about why. ✅
- **Three phases:** **initiation** (find a specific start signal), **elongation** (build the growing RNA strand), **termination** (find a specific stop sequence). ✅

**Getting the message ready: introns, exons and splicing**
- The fresh mRNA is **unfinished** and **too big for the pores of the nucleus**. 🟡 Evidence check: size is not the real barrier. An mRNA leaves through the pores only after it has been processed and packed with export proteins, and export is tied to splicing, so unfinished copies are held back (Köhler 2007).
- **Exons** are the parts that carry the recipe; **introns** are the non-coding stretches in between. ✅
- The **spliceosome** recognises each intron, snips both ends, and joins the remaining pieces back together. (It is a cluster of small RNAs and proteins in which the RNA itself does the cutting, Fica 2013. The ending "-ome" means a complex or a whole set, as in proteome, all the proteins, or lipidome, all the fats.) ✅
- The result is a **finished (mature) mRNA**, roughly **a third to a quarter of the original length**, small enough to leave through the pore into the cytosol. 🟡 The fraction is given without a source and varies hugely from gene to gene. In many human genes the introns are far longer than the exons, so the finished mRNA is often much less than a quarter of the original copy (not confirmed as a general figure).

## Step 3 — Translation (mRNA → chain of amino acids)
- **The codon rule:** every **three letters (nucleotides)** on the mRNA form one **codon**, and each codon stands for one amino acid. The message is read three letters at a time, one group after another. ✅
- **Several codes for the same thing:** different codons can stand for the same amino acid. Example: **leucine has six codons**. Every codon stands for one amino acid (except the three stop codons), and most amino acids have several codons; methionine and tryptophan have only one. ✅
- **The ribosome:** a molecular machine with a **small and a large part (subunit)** that moves along the mRNA and gives tRNA a place to attach. ✅
- **What happens, in order:**
  1. The **small subunit** finds the **start codon**.
  2. The **large subunit** joins it.
  3. A **tRNA** whose **anticodon** matches the codon attaches, leaves its amino acid on the ribosome, and then goes.
  4. The ribosome moves along one codon at a time. Each matching tRNA delivers its amino acid and leaves, and the chain grows.
  5. At a **stop codon** (termination) the two subunits come apart, leaving a free chain of amino acids. ✅
- **Leader sequence:** the start of the chain carries a **leader sequence**, an address label that decides where the protein goes: the nucleus, the mitochondria, another inner room, or out of the cell. ✅ 🟢 More exactly: the labels for the ER (the route out of the cell) and for mitochondria usually sit at the start of the chain, but the labels that send a protein to the nucleus can be elsewhere in it.

## Step 4 — Finishing the Protein (Post-Translational Modification)
- The raw chain **does not work yet**. Other proteins fold it into its secondary and tertiary shapes, and sometimes it joins with others into a quaternary structure. ✅
- No more copying, transcription or translation happens at this stage. ✅
- **The route out:** proteins meant to leave the cell pass through inner rooms such as the **Golgi apparatus**, are packed into a **vesicle**, and are released by **exocytosis** to work somewhere else in the body. ✅

## Punchlines & Quotable Facts (Lecture 4)
- **"The central dogma helps us understand how our genes determine our body."** The one-sentence summary.
- **Genotype is the blueprint; phenotype is the house.**
- **Crick proposed the central dogma in 1958**, having already worked out the structure of DNA.
- **Copying is semi-conservative:** every new double helix keeps one original strand.
- **The lagging strand is built in pieces (Okazaki fragments) because the polymerase only works in one direction.**
- **After splicing, the finished mRNA can be a third to a quarter of the length of the original copy.**
- **Leucine has six codons** (so do serine and arginine).
- **The Dutch famine study:** within one generation, children of mothers who lived through a famine were more likely to become overweight and diabetic. Bikman puts this down to epigenetics, not to a change in the genes.

## Q&A Highlights (End of Lecture)
**Keeping cells healthy, and PUFAs (Bikman's answer)**
- His chosen answer: protect cell membranes by eating "real fats" rather than refined seed oils. By real fats he means animal fats plus fruit fats like olive, coconut and avocado, which can be pressed from the flesh of the fruit. He says seed oils were basically absent about 120 years ago and arrived in the 1950s. ✅ as his stated position. 🟡 Evidence check: soybean oil use in the US rose more than 1,000-fold between 1909 and 1999, a rise spread across the century rather than starting in the 1950s (Blasbalg 2011).
- **The mechanism he gives:** polyunsaturated fats (PUFAs) get built into membranes and **go rancid inside the body (peroxidise)** easily. This oxidative stress starts with a reactive form of oxygen (an oxygen radical) that passes its reactivity on to the fat. These damaged fats (lipid peroxides) move freely through fatty membranes. They can damage the mitochondria's energy chain (creating more reactive oxygen) and can cross the **membrane around the nucleus** and cause **breaks in DNA**. ✅ as his stated mechanism. 🟡 Evidence check: the chemistry is sound. Polyunsaturated fats are the fats most likely to go rancid, and their breakdown products can damage DNA (Yin 2011). But the health outcome is not shown. In pooled data from studies around the world, people with higher blood and tissue levels of linoleic acid, the main fat in seed oils, had a lower risk of heart and blood-vessel events (Marklund 2019). The Minnesota trial, meanwhile, found no survival benefit from replacing saturated fat with corn oil (Ramsden 2016).
- 🟡 This is a position argued in a Q&A, not a segment backed by data: the lecture cites no studies. His views on lab-grown or fake meat (fat sourced from cheap seed oils) are likewise opinion.

**Why do introns exist?**
- Bikman: **we do not yet know what they are for.** His own *guess*: the long non-coding stretches may work as a statistical **buffer**. A reactive fat is then more likely to hit a non-coding stretch, which can be cut out with no effect on the recipe. 🟡 clearly a guess. 🟢 Evidence check: introns do have known jobs. They allow alternative splicing, where the same gene is trimmed in different ways to make several proteins. This happens in about 95% of human genes with more than one exon (Pan 2008). Introns also carry control sequences.
- He presents science as answering *how* and *what*, not *why*, and states his personal view that biology shows intelligent design. This is not a scientific claim of the lecture; noted for completeness.

**Epigenetics**
- Epigenetics means changes that affect whether and how the genetic code is read, even when the DNA letters themselves are intact. Examples are **methylation** (small chemical tags added to DNA) and, he also mentions, carbonylation. They can arise at several stages (after transcription or after translation, according to Bikman) and are influenced by things such as the body's metabolic state. ✅ 🟢 Evidence check: the standard epigenetic marks are DNA methylation and chemical tags on histones (the spools DNA is wound around), such as acetylation and methylation, plus some control RNAs. Carbonylation is not usually counted as an epigenetic mark.
- **The Dutch famine study:** children exposed in the womb to the 1944-45 Dutch Hunger Winter at the end of WWII, born to healthy parents, were later more likely to be overweight and diabetic. Because this happened within one generation, it cannot be a change in the genes or evolution. Bikman reads it as an epigenetic signal saying "be thrifty, store everything," because food was scarce. 🟢 Evidence check: the exposed children did show more obesity when exposed early in pregnancy (Ravelli 1976) and worse blood sugar handling (Ravelli 1998). Six decades later they had different methylation of a gene called IGF2 (Heijmans 2008). That the epigenetic changes cause the metabolic effects is plausible but not proven.
- **Radiation exposure** is another example of an exposure that can change how genes are expressed (too much or too little of a given protein). ✅

## Key Corrections to Carry Forward
- 🔴 Thinking the central dogma is "genes make you" in one step: there are **three changes** (copying the DNA, transcription from DNA to mRNA, translation from mRNA to protein) plus a fourth stage, **folding after translation**, before anything works.
- 🔴 Thinking the RNA made from DNA goes straight to the ribosome: it is first **spliced** (introns removed) into a finished mRNA small enough to pass through the pores of the nucleus.
- 🔴 Thinking RNA is a letter-for-letter copy of DNA: RNA uses **uracil where DNA has thymine**.
- 🔴 Thinking there is one codon per amino acid: the code has **several codons for most amino acids** (leucine has six), even though each codon stands for only one amino acid.
- 🔴 Thinking that an inherited disease risk or metabolic tendency must come from a change in the DNA letters: the Dutch famine example shows that **epigenetic** changes can shift outcomes within one generation without changing the genes.

## Practical Takeaway
The useful content of this lecture is the framework itself. Any protein you care about (insulin, receptors, enzymes, transport proteins) is the end product of transcription, splicing, translation and folding, and each stage is a point where things can go wrong or be controlled. The one diet-related claim, to prefer traditional fats over refined seed oils to protect membranes and DNA from going rancid, is Bikman's stated opinion with a plausible mechanism, not something this lecture proves.

## Questions to Learn From (Socratic) — with Answer Options
**1. DNA polymerase can only build new DNA in the 5'→3' direction, while the helicase opens the double helix in one direction. What explains the lagging strand being copied in pieces?**
A. The lagging strand is a different molecule that needs its own RNA polymerase, which works slowly and in bursts
B. Ligase cuts the lagging strand into short pieces so it can check each one before the polymerase goes on
C. \* The polymerase can only work in one direction, so on the lagging strand it must keep jumping ahead to newly opened DNA and building short Okazaki fragments, which ligase later seals into one continuous strand
*Why C: the direction rule plus the two strands running opposite ways produces the difference between leading and lagging strands. The fragments are the result, and ligase is the joiner, not the cutter.*

**2. A newly made mRNA is too large for the pores of the nucleus and contains non-coding stretches. What must happen before it can go to the ribosome?**
A. \* The spliceosome removes the introns and joins the exons, producing a finished mRNA a third to a quarter of the original length that can slip out of the nucleus
B. The mRNA must be turned back into DNA so it can pass through the pore, then copied again in the cytosol
C. The ribosome enters the nucleus and reads the mRNA there, so it never needs to leave
*Why A: splicing removes the non-coding introns and joins the coding exons. DNA never leaves the nucleus, and proteins are built in the cytosol.*

**3. Leucine can be coded by six different codons. What does this tell you about the genetic code?**
A. Each codon can stand for several amino acids, so the ribosome has to guess
B. \* It has extra codes in one direction only: each codon stands for a single amino acid, but one amino acid can be coded by several codons
C. Only leucine has more than one codon, because it is the main amino acid for building muscle
*Why B: Bikman stresses that every amino acid has several possible codons (in fact methionine and tryptophan have only one), while the ribosome reads codons in groups of three, each matching one tRNA and its single amino acid.*

**4. Bikman argues about refined seed oils and DNA. Which chain of reasoning is he actually making, and what should you remember about how well it is supported?**
A. Seed-oil fats are too big to enter cells, so they block ribosomes from building proteins
B. Polyunsaturated fats cannot be built into membranes, so they float around freely and attach to DNA directly
C. \* Membranes rich in polyunsaturated fats go rancid easily, and the resulting mobile damaged fats can cross the fatty membrane around the nucleus and pass the damage on to DNA, causing breaks; this is his stated mechanism and opinion, with no studies cited in the lecture
*Why C: the argument depends on PUFAs being built into membranes and on the membrane around the nucleus being made of fat. The 🟡 flag is because it is presented as a reasoned position in a Q&A, not as evidence.*
