
# Bikman — Basics of Biology, Ch.4: The Central Dogma
*How genetic information flows from DNA to RNA to protein: replication, transcription (with splicing), translation and post-translational folding, plus Q&A on seed-oil peroxidation, introns and epigenetics.*

*Source: Dr. Benjamin Bikman, Peterson Academy course "Basics of Biology," Lecture 4 (transcript). Fourth of six chapters in a foundational cell-biology course; builds on the biomolecule (Ch.3) and enzyme material and sets up the cell cycle (Ch.5).*

## Why This Matters
This is the lecture where the earlier pieces (nucleic acids, enzymes, organelles, vesicles) snap into one story: **genes determine the body because DNA is turned into RNA, and RNA is turned into the proteins that actually do the work.** It is foundational vocabulary rather than something you act on directly, and the Q&A at the end is where it touches diet and long-term health.

## The Central Dogma — The Big Picture
- **Definition:** the flow of genetic information from **DNA → RNA (transcription) → protein (translation)**. In plain language: how our genes determine our body. ✅
- **Origin:** proposed by **Francis Crick (UK) in 1958**, the same person who, with James Watson, identified the structure of DNA (Crick 1958). ✅
- **Why protein is the endpoint:** cells make us up, but cell *function* is set by the proteins they express. Height, body size and shape, skin, hair and eye colour are all downstream of protein expression. Bikman says environment has only a "very modest" influence on this. 🟡 Evidence check: genes explain much of the height difference between individuals, but environment is not minor: over the 20th century average adult height rose by about 20 cm in South Korean women and 16.5 cm in Iranian men, mainly through better nutrition and health (NCD-RisC 2016).
- **Genotype vs. phenotype:** genotype is the blueprint for a house; phenotype is the house itself. Biologically: genetic code = blueprint, protein = the structure. ✅
- **Gene:** a DNA sequence that encodes a particular product. A chromosome is *not* gene from end to end; genes sit in selected regions with a lot of non-coding "wasted space" between. ✅
- **Where things happen:** DNA stays in the **nucleus**; RNA leaves into the **cytosol**, where translation occurs; the finished protein either stays to work or is released to signal elsewhere. ✅

## Setup Detail — DNA Directionality
- Nucleotides: **purines (A, G)** and **pyrimidines (C, T)**; the two strands run **anti-parallel**. ✅
- Each strand has a **5' end** (phosphate-bearing) and a **3' end** (sugar side). One strand reads 3'→5' going up, the other 5'→3'. ✅
- This matters because every copying enzyme in the lecture works in one fixed direction, which explains the leading/lagging strand asymmetry below.

## Step 1 — DNA Replication
**Purpose:** before a cell divides (mitosis, next lecture), it needs two identical copies of its genetic information, so a liver cell stays a liver cell and daughter cells stay identical. Replication is also what allows heredity through gametes. ✅

**Model:** the **semi-conservative** model, the most validated of several proposed mechanisms (Meselson 1958). Each new double helix contains **one original strand and one newly made strand**. Over repeated divisions, more and more cells carry none of the *original* physical strand, though every cell holds a functionally identical copy. ✅

| Component | Role in replication | Bikman's analogy |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Helicase** | Runs along the helix and pries the tightly coiled DNA open | Tugging a car door open |
| **Single-stranded binding proteins** | Anchor the strands apart so they cannot collapse back together | Books wedged in the gap |
| **Primase** | Marks the start point: "replication begins here" (primes the copying by laying down a short RNA primer, because DNA polymerase cannot start a new strand from scratch) | — |
| **DNA polymerase** | Builds the new DNA polymer; also proofreads | — |
| **Ligase** | Seals separate fragments into one continuous strand | — |

- **Replication forks:** the two ends of the opened region where the DNA is still wound. ✅
- **Polymerase direction rule:** it reads the template **3'→5'** and therefore builds the new strand **5'→3'**. ✅
- **Leading strand:** the template strand whose 3' end faces the opening, so the polymerase can run **continuously and uninterrupted** as the helicase keeps unzipping. ✅
- **Lagging strand:** the opposite strand. The polymerase can only work in its one direction, but the helix keeps opening the other way, so it repeatedly binds, runs until it hits the previous piece, then **skips ahead**. This makes short pieces called **Okazaki fragments**, which **ligase** then joins. ✅
- 🟡 Bikman's spoken description of which end the polymerase "starts" from on each strand is hard to follow in transcript form (he was acting it out with his arms). The robust takeaways are the rule (builds 5'→3'), the result (continuous vs. fragmented) and the enzyme list.

**Three accuracy mechanisms** ✅
1. **Hydrogen bonding:** correct pairs (A-T, G-C) bond more strongly and form a tighter coil; a mismatch bonds weakly, distorts the helix and gets recognised and fixed.
2. **Polymerase active site:** it does not readily accept mismatches and knows where to start and stop.
3. **Proofreading:** the polymerase can look back, recognise an error within a fraction of a second, back up, break down that section and rebuild it.

## Step 2 — Transcription (DNA → mRNA)
**Three types of RNA, each with its own RNA polymerase** (RNA pol I, II, III): ✅

| RNA type | Job |
| --- | --- |
| **mRNA** (messenger) | The copy of the gene that carries the message out of the nucleus; the star of transcription |
| **tRNA** (transfer) | Recognises a specific codon via its anticodon and delivers the matching amino acid |
| **rRNA** (ribosomal) | Forms the ribosome, the machine that reads mRNA and gives tRNA a place to bind |

- **Why an RNA message is needed:** DNA never leaves the nucleus, and protein synthesis happens outside it; mRNA carries the instruction out without putting the DNA at risk. ✅
- **Simpler than replication:** one enzyme, **RNA polymerase**, does the jobs of helicase, binding proteins and polymerase (Bikman says it effectively does all of them). It reads the template 3'→5' and builds RNA 5'→3'. ✅
- **Not a letter-perfect copy:** RNA uses **uracil (U) in place of thymine (T)**. Bikman skips the theories on why. ✅
- **Three phases:** **initiation** (recognise a specific start signal), **elongation** (build the growing RNA strand), **termination** (recognise a specific stop sequence). ✅

**Maturation: introns, exons and splicing**
- The fresh mRNA is **immature** and **too big for the nuclear pores**. 🟡 Evidence check: size is not the real gate. An mRNA leaves through the pores only once it has been processed and packed with export proteins, and export is coupled to splicing, so unprocessed transcripts are held back (Köhler 2007).
- **Exons** = coding information; **introns** = the non-coding in-between stretches. ✅
- The **spliceosome** (a complex of small nuclear RNAs and proteins, in which the RNA itself does the cutting, Fica 2013; the "-ome" suffix means a complex or totality, as in proteome, lipidome) recognises each intron, snips both ends and rejoins the remaining pieces. ✅
- Result: a **mature mRNA** that is roughly **a third to a quarter of the original length**, small enough to exit the pore into the cytosol. 🟡 The fraction is stated without a source and varies hugely between genes; in many human genes the introns are far longer than the exons, so the mature mRNA is often much less than a quarter of the original transcript (unverified as a general figure).

## Step 3 — Translation (mRNA → amino acid chain)
- **The codon rule:** every **three nucleotides** on the mRNA form one **codon**, and each codon specifies one amino acid. The sequence is read in sets of three, one after another. ✅
- **Redundancy:** several different codons can specify the same amino acid. Example: **leucine has six codons**. Every codon codes for one amino acid (except the three stop codons), and most amino acids have several codons; methionine and tryptophan have only one. ✅
- **The ribosome:** a molecular machine with a **small and a large subunit** that reads along the mRNA and provides the scaffold where tRNA binds. ✅
- **The sequence of events:**
  1. The **small subunit** finds the **start codon**.
  2. The **large subunit** joins.
  3. A **tRNA** whose **anticodon** matches the codon binds, leaving its amino acid on the ribosome and then departing.
  4. The ribosome moves one codon at a time; each matching tRNA delivers its amino acid and leaves, and the chain grows.
  5. At a **stop codon** (termination) the subunits separate, leaving a free amino acid chain. ✅
- **Leader sequence:** the beginning of the chain carries a **leader sequence** that determines where the protein goes: the nucleus, the mitochondria, another organelle, or out of the cell. ✅ 🟢 More precisely: the signals for the ER (secretion) and for mitochondria usually sit at the start of the chain, but nuclear localization signals can lie elsewhere in the protein.

## Step 4 — Post-Translational Modification
- The raw chain is **not yet functional**. Other proteins fold it into secondary and tertiary structure, and sometimes it joins others for a quaternary structure. ✅
- No more replication, transcription or translation is involved at this stage. ✅
- **Export route:** proteins destined to leave pass through organelles such as the **Golgi apparatus**, are packaged into a **vesicle** and released by **exocytosis** to act at a distant site in the body. ✅

## Q&A Highlights (End of Lecture)
**Keeping cells healthy, and PUFAs (Bikman's answer)**
- His chosen answer: protect membrane integrity by eating "real fats" (animal fats plus fruit fats like olive, coconut and avocado, which can be pressed from flesh) rather than refined seed oils, which he says were essentially absent ~120 years ago and arrived in the 1950s. ✅ as his stated position. 🟡 Evidence check: US soybean oil consumption rose more than 1,000-fold between 1909 and 1999, a rise spread across the century rather than starting in the 1950s (Blasbalg 2011).
- **Mechanism he gives:** polyunsaturated fats are incorporated into membranes and **peroxidise** readily (oxidative stress begins with an oxygen radical that passes its reactivity to the lipid). Lipid peroxides move freely through fatty membranes, can damage the mitochondrial electron transport system (creating more reactive oxygen species) and can cross the **nuclear envelope** to cause **DNA breaks**. ✅ as his stated mechanism. 🟡 Evidence check: the chemistry is sound, since polyunsaturated fats are the lipids most prone to peroxidation and their breakdown products can damage DNA (Yin 2011). The health outcome is not shown: in pooled international cohort data, people with higher blood and tissue levels of linoleic acid, the main seed-oil fat, had a lower risk of cardiovascular events (Marklund 2019), while the Minnesota trial found no survival benefit from replacing saturated fat with corn oil (Ramsden 2016).
- 🟡 This is an argued position in a Q&A, not a data-backed segment: no studies are cited in the lecture. His views on lab-grown or fake meat (fat sourced from cheap seed oils) are likewise opinion.

**Why do introns exist?**
- Bikman: **we do not yet know their purpose.** His own *speculation*: non-coding length may act as a statistical **buffer**, so a reactive lipid is more likely to hit a non-coding region, which can then be spliced out with no effect on the coding information. 🟡 explicitly speculative. 🟢 Evidence check: introns do have known roles. They allow alternative splicing, which affects about 95% of human multi-exon genes and lets one gene make several proteins (Pan 2008), and they carry regulatory sequences.
- He frames science as answering *how* and *what*, not *why*, and states his own view of biology as intelligent design. Not a scientific claim of the lecture; noted for completeness.

**Epigenetics**
- Epigenetics: changes such as **methylation** (also mentioned: carbonylation) that interfere with reading or expressing the code, even when the underlying DNA sequence is intact. They can arise at several points (post-transcriptional or post-translational, per Bikman) and are influenced by factors such as metabolic status. ✅ 🟢 Evidence check: the standard epigenetic marks are DNA methylation and chemical tags on histones (such as acetylation and methylation), plus some regulatory RNAs; carbonylation is not usually counted as an epigenetic mark.
- **Dutch famine study:** children exposed in the womb to the 1944-45 Dutch Hunger Winter at the end of WWII, born to healthy parents, were later more prone to overweight and diabetes. Because this happened within one generation it cannot be a genetic mutation or evolution; Bikman reads it as an epigenetic "be thrifty, store everything" signal about scarce nutrition. 🟢 Evidence check: exposed offspring did show more obesity when exposed early in pregnancy (Ravelli 1976) and worse glucose tolerance (Ravelli 1998), and six decades later had different DNA methylation of the IGF2 gene (Heijmans 2008). That epigenetic changes cause the metabolic effects is plausible but not proven.
- **Radiation exposure** is another example of an exposure that can alter expression (too much or too little of a given protein). ✅

## Key Corrections to Carry Forward
- 🔴 Assuming the central dogma is "genes make you" in a single step: there are **three transformations** (replication for copying, transcription DNA → mRNA, translation mRNA → protein) plus a fourth stage, **post-translational folding**, before anything is functional.
- 🔴 Assuming the RNA made from DNA goes straight to the ribosome: it is first **spliced** (introns removed) into a mature mRNA small enough to pass the nuclear pores.
- 🔴 Assuming RNA is a base-for-base DNA copy: RNA uses **uracil where DNA has thymine**.
- 🔴 Assuming one codon pattern per amino acid: the code is **redundant** (leucine has six codons) even though each codon specifies only one amino acid.
- 🔴 Assuming inherited disease risk or metabolic tendencies must be a change in the DNA sequence: the Dutch famine example shows **epigenetic** changes can shift outcomes within one generation without altering the genes.

## Practical Takeaway
The practical content of this lecture is the framework itself: any protein you care about (insulin, receptors, enzymes, transport proteins) is the end product of transcription, splicing, translation and folding, and each stage is a point where things can go wrong or be regulated. The single diet-related claim, to favour ancestral fats over refined seed oils to protect membranes and DNA from peroxidation, is Bikman's stated opinion with a plausible mechanism, not something proven within this lecture.

## Questions to Learn From (Socratic) — with Answer Options
**1. Given that DNA polymerase can only build new DNA in the 5'→3' direction while the helicase opens the double helix in a single direction — what explains the lagging strand being copied in pieces?**
A. The lagging strand is a different molecule that needs its own RNA polymerase, which works slowly and in bursts
B. Ligase cuts the lagging strand into short segments to proofread each one before the polymerase continues
C. \* The polymerase can only work in one direction, so on the lagging strand it must keep jumping ahead to newly opened DNA and building short Okazaki fragments, which ligase later seals into one continuous strand
*Why C: the direction rule plus the anti-parallel structure produces the leading/lagging asymmetry; the fragments are a consequence, and ligase is the joiner, not the cutter.*

**2. Given that a newly transcribed mRNA is too large for the nuclear pores and contains non-coding stretches — what must happen before it can go to the ribosome?**
A. \* The spliceosome removes the introns and rejoins the exons, producing a mature mRNA a third to a quarter of the original length that can slip out of the nucleus
B. The mRNA must be converted back into DNA so it can pass through the pore, then re-transcribed in the cytosol
C. The ribosome enters the nucleus and translates the mRNA there, so no exit is needed
*Why A: splicing removes the non-coding introns and joins the coding exons; DNA never leaves the nucleus and translation happens in the cytosol.*

**3. Given that leucine can be encoded by six different codons — what does this tell you about the genetic code?**
A. Each codon can specify several amino acids, so the ribosome has to guess
B. \* It is redundant in one direction: each codon specifies a single amino acid, but a single amino acid can be specified by several codons
C. Only leucine has multiple codons, because it is the main amino acid of muscle building
*Why B: Bikman stresses that every amino acid has multiple possible codons (in fact methionine and tryptophan have only one), while the ribosome reads codons in triplets, each pairing with one tRNA anticodon and its single amino acid.*

**4. Given Bikman's argument about refined seed oils and DNA — which chain of reasoning is he actually making (and what should you remember about its evidential status)?**
A. Seed oil fats are too big to enter cells, so they block ribosomes from translating proteins
B. Polyunsaturated fats cannot be incorporated into membranes, so they circulate freely and bind DNA directly
C. \* Membranes enriched in polyunsaturated fats are prone to peroxidation, and the resulting mobile lipid peroxides can cross the fatty nuclear envelope and pass oxidative stress to DNA, causing breaks; it is his stated mechanism and opinion, with no studies cited in the lecture
*Why C: the lecture's argument depends on PUFAs entering membranes and the lipid nature of the nuclear envelope; the 🟡 flag is that this is presented as a reasoned position in Q&A, not as presented evidence.*
