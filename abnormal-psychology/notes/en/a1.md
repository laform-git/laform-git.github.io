# Pihl — Abnormal Psychology Part I, Ch.1: Defining Mental Illness
*Pihl's opening lecture argues that "mental illness" is a vote-defined, ever-expanding label system, so the course teaches a way of looking at the field rather than a pile of facts.*

*Source: Dr. Robert O. Pihl, Peterson Academy course "Introduction to Abnormal Psychology Part I," Lecture 1 (transcript). First of six chapters; the course frames the field's definitions, prevalence, and why its categories deserve skepticism.*

## Why This Matters
This lecture sets the lens for the whole course: before learning any disorder, ask what the label actually explains, who decided it exists, and how its prevalence was measured. Pihl's three-sentence verdict on the field is deliberately provocative: etiology (causation) is unknown, treatment is trial and error, and drugs have been spectacularly unsuccessful. 🟡 Those are his framing claims as a lecturer, offered as "defendable," not settled consensus; read them as a prompt for critical thinking. It also pairs naturally with your existing Chemical Imbalance Myth notes.

## Scope-Setting Claim — Perspective Over Facts
Pihl cites Whitehead: "knowledge is more perishable than fresh fish"; a way of thinking is not. His examples of information overload: roughly 3,000 books published per day, and one week of the New York Times equaling what an 18th-century person took in over a lifetime. ✅ The course goal is therefore a synthesis habit, not memorization.

Course roadmap he gives: definitions and prevalence, then models of abnormality, then classification and the current nomenclature, then assessment methods (interviews, psychological, biological, biochemical and genetic testing), then causation, then three disorders as examples: schizophrenia (the paradigm for the medical model), dementia, and depression ("the common cold of abnormal psychology").

## Why "Abnormal" Is Hard to Define

| Approach | How it works | Pihl's objection |
|---|---|---|
| Statistical | Abnormal = N standard deviations from the mean (about 68%, 95%, 99.7% inside 1, 2, 3 SD) | Behavior is not normally distributed (where do delusions plot?), and the two tails are not equivalent (IQ 130 vs. IQ under 70 are not treated as equally abnormal) |
| Majority vote | Definitions of mental illness come from group consensus | Votes can be wrong (his cartoon: a vote that "proves" a cow); Einstein's quip on human stupidity |
| Whatever the DSM says | Abnormal = what the "psychiatric bible" lists | Raises the question of how much attention the manual deserves |

Pihl also notes that abnormal psychology, psychopathology, and mental illness are effectively interchangeable terms. ✅

## The DSM Expansion
- **1952:** first North American diagnostic nomenclature, **127 disorders**.
- **DSM-5-TR ("came out last year" in the lecture):** **544 disorders**. ✅
- His rhetorical question: have we become more demented, or is mental illness "communicable"? He wants the process examined, not the graph accepted.
- **Bandwagon pattern:** new disorders, therapies, and ideas rise quickly in enthusiasm, often crash, then settle to a baseline.
- **How to create a disorder:** write explicit operational criteria, get a circle of colleagues to agree it is a problem, and then it is politics. His joke example: a "congenital" disorder of small, emotionally labile, immature people who cannot eat vegetables (children).
- **Allen Frances** (chair of the DSM-4 task force) said in 2013 of DSM-5: don't buy it, don't use it, don't teach it. Pihl adds that Frances had wanted to chair the DSM-5 committee ("sour grapes"), so he treats the motive skeptically while granting the arguments are fine. 🟡 The motive claim is Pihl's characterization.

## Disorder Inflation and Its Costs
- **Everyone ends up disordered.** Pihl's irony: psychiatrists name stigma as the field's biggest problem; if everyone is mentally ill, stigma is "handled."
- **Reification:** words like schizophrenia and autism are made-up labels; using the word does not explain the behavior. ✅
- **Circular explanation:** Why did he commit murder? He is mentally ill. How do we know? He committed murder. Nothing explained. ✅
- **Blocked causation:** the DSM vocabulary has led research "up blind alleys" with terms too narrow or too broad; funders are starting to notice.
- **PTSD example:** **636,120** possible symptom combinations under DSM-5; in a study of roughly 3,500 PTSD cases, about 2,500 profiles were unique. 🟡 Pihl recalls the study from memory ("I think").
- **FBI mass-murderer study:** only about 25% could be classed as mentally ill; social and other factors matter more. ✅

## Prevalence and Cost

| Figure | Value (as stated) |
|---|---|
| WHO Global Burden of Disease 2019 (7,000 researchers, 156 countries) | 1 in 8 people, about **970 million**, live with a mental disorder |
| Anxiety and depression combined | about 300 million (depression about 208 million) |
| Bipolar / conduct disorder / schizophrenia | about 40 million / 40 million / 24 million (about 1 in 300) |
| Lifetime to age 40 | about **50%** will have a mental illness |
| Life expectancy cost | 10 to 20 years |
| Economic cost | up to $500B (US), $50B (Canada), $2.5 trillion world; projected $16 trillion by 2030; about one-third of hospital stays |

- Substance use was not included in the 2019 study; he notes it is more prevalent than most other disorders (46 million US adults met criteria in 2021).
- **COVID:** depression rose with the pandemic. In a post-COVID study, people with **two or more pre-COVID psychological symptoms** (depression, anxiety, worry, perceived stress, loneliness) had about a **50% higher risk** of post-COVID difficulties, comparable to the physical risk factors (smoking, diabetes, cholesterol). ✅
- **Youth:** 2016 to 2020 survey data showed rises in anxiety, depression, and conduct disorder, with drops in physical activity and parental mental health. The Seattle schools lawsuit against social media platforms is cited as a summary of that concern. 🟡 He offers it as a well-argued legal document, not as scientific proof of causation.
- Funding for mental illness research "pales" next to cancer and cardiovascular disease.

## Three Beliefs Tested
**1. Are the mentally ill more violent?** Pihl's line: they are no more violent than the general population, but the data must be nuanced.
- Old past-year violence rates: no disorder 2.1%, schizophrenia 12.7%, major depression 11.7%, mania/bipolar 11%, alcohol abuse 24.6%, drug abuse 34.7%.
- A 204-study meta-analysis found psychosis associated with a 49 to 68% increase in odds of violence, but that blurs the picture: negative-symptom (withdrawn, passive) patients are less aggressive than average, positive-symptom (hallucinations, delusions) patients more so, and **history of violence and substance abuse** explained the most.
- People with severe mental illness are far likelier to be **victims**: about 11 times overall, 14 times for personal theft, about 12 times for violent crime, 4 times for property crime. ✅

**2. Is autism an epidemic?** ("One in six to eight kids" he dismisses; CDC figures cited: 1 in 27 boys, 1 in 118 girls; Canada 1 in 42 males, 1 in 65 females; US about 1 in 44 versus France about 1 in 140-something.)
- Drivers of the rise he names: autism added to the DSM-3 in 1980; Asperger's added 1994; widened criteria; the old NOS ("autism-light") category; awareness, advocacy (Rimland, Jenny McCarthy), school funding programs; and **diagnostic substitution** (children once labeled intellectually deficient now labeled autistic).
- Comorbidity: many diagnosed autistic people carry anxiety, depression, sleep problems, raising the "autism, the other problems, or both?" question.
- **Correlation joke:** organic food sales and microwaves track the autism curve perfectly. The lesson: always ask whether a correlation makes sense.
- Causes: genes explain maybe 14% of variance in one very large study; prenatal toxin and infection evidence is limited; more prevalent in cities and among the very poor, possibly due to diagnostic access. Male-to-female ratio about 4:1 (range 3.2 to 4.9:1); a UK study linked **fetal testosterone** to later autistic traits, giving the "extreme male brain" idea some support. 🟡
- His conclusion: environmental factors and parental age cannot explain an epidemic; awareness and broader criteria likely explain most of the rise. It does not mean the disorder is not real; "worry about the edges." 🟡 His assessment, with the effect of DSM-5 changes still unknown.

**3. Child homicide by parents:** a chart shows far higher rates by step-parents than biological parents, contradicting the "familial stress" explanation; his glib aside about marriage is a joke, not a claim. 🟡 Old graph, details not given.

## Measuring Prevalence — Why Numbers Disagree
- **Structured interviews** use branching explicit questions ("Ever been depressed?" then follow-ups), but there is no gold standard like Huntington's disease, where you can count genetic repeats.
- **Definitions rest on professional judgment and operational criteria.**
- **Where you sample changes the answer:** hospital populations (one survey of every patient reported five classes, among them suicidal/depressed, angry, psychotic, angry geriatrics, antisocial addicts, and social isolates) reflect who is dangerous or cannot be cared for, not the 544 DSM categories. Offices: waits of months in Canada.
- **Most are untreated:** a WHO survey of 85,000 people found about 30% diagnosable per year with two-thirds untreated (US 67%, Europe 74%). Untreated by disorder: schizophrenia one-third, depression/bipolar/OCD/panic about half, alcohol three-quarters; oddly, more people with no disorder than with a severe one sought treatment in each country.
- **Culture:** bipolar lifetime prevalence is far higher in the US than elsewhere; ADHD is far more common in North America than Europe, in males, and in children.

## The ADHD Birth-Date Example
- In regions with an August 31 cutoff, children born just after it enter school a year later and are the **oldest** in class; those just before it are the **youngest** and show the highest ADHD diagnosis and medication rates. ✅
- Pihl's reading (the Elder study): relative immaturity is being labeled as a disorder, an administratively created diagnosis; **about 900,000 US children** may be misdiagnosed and treated. 🟡 Single-study figure as cited.
- Labeled children are drugged, while older peers gain success, opportunity, and advantage; he notes parents "redshirt" kids and that athletes show a birth-date advantage pattern too.

- **"Etiology unknown. Treatment trial and error. Drugs spectacularly unsuccessful."** — his three-sentence summary of the field.
- **127 disorders (1952) to 544 (DSM-5-TR)** — the graph that frames everything.
- **"Why did he do it? He's mentally ill. How do you know? He did it."** — circular explanation.
- **636,120** possible PTSD presentations.
- **"Think of your best friend. If that person seems OK, it's you."** — on about 50% lifetime prevalence by age 40.
- **Severe mental illness: victims far more than perpetrators.**
- **Age relative to school cutoff predicts ADHD diagnosis.**
- **Organic food sales "perfectly" correlate with autism** — his lesson in why you do not trust correlations.

## Key Corrections to Carry Forward
- 🔴 Assuming a diagnosis explains behavior: a label restates the observation; causation needs data, not a word.
- 🔴 Assuming rising prevalence means rising illness: definitions, awareness, funding, and diagnostic substitution can drive the curve.
- 🔴 Assuming the mentally ill are generally violent: risk is concentrated in prior violence and substance abuse, and victimization is far higher.
- 🔴 Assuming prevalence numbers are objective: they depend on sampling site, interview method, and culture.
- 🔴 Assuming a child's ADHD label is purely neurological: relative age within the school year changes diagnosis rates.

## Practical Takeaway
When you meet any disorder statistic or label, ask four questions: who defined it, how was it measured, where was the sample drawn, and what else changed over the same period? Pihl also recommends reading meta-analyses rather than single studies, while remembering that authors choose what to include (he previews that newer meta-analyses including unpublished trials paint antidepressant efficacy differently). 🟡 Preview only; details are promised later.

## Questions to Learn From (Socratic) — with Answer Options
**1. The DSM grew from 127 disorders (1952) to 544 (DSM-5-TR). Which reading of this fits Pihl's argument best?**
A. Society has become roughly four times more mentally ill in 70 years
B. \* The list expands partly because disorders are defined by operational criteria and group agreement, so growth reflects definition and politics as well as possible real change, and the curve should be questioned rather than taken at face value
C. Earlier psychiatrists were simply less skilled at spotting disorders
*Why B: Pihl asks "what are these disorders?" and describes how a disorder is created by criteria plus agreement; he does not claim illness rates tracked the list.*

**2. "He committed murder because he is mentally ill; we know he is mentally ill because he committed murder." What problem does Pihl identify?**
A. \* Circular reasoning: the label is inferred from the behavior it is supposed to explain, so nothing has been explained
B. The statement is false because mentally ill people never commit murder
C. The statement is fine because psychiatric diagnoses are objective
*Why A: he uses it to show that applying a word is not an explanation; the FBI study found only about 25% of such offenders were classifiable as mentally ill.*

**3. Why do children born just after a school's age cutoff show higher ADHD diagnosis and medication rates?**
A. A rare genetic variant is linked to birthdays near the cutoff
B. Summer babies are exposed to more toxins
C. \* They are the youngest and least mature in their class, so age-appropriate immaturity can be labeled as a disorder when compared against older classmates
*Why C: the lecture's study shows rates fall for older-in-class children; Pihl calls it a bureaucratically induced disorder and estimates about 900,000 US misdiagnoses (single-study figure).*

**4. Which statement about violence and severe mental illness best matches the lecture?**
A. Diagnosis alone is the strongest predictor of violence
B. \* Prior history of violence and substance abuse matter most, passive negative-symptom patients may be less aggressive than average, and people with severe mental illness are much more likely to be victims than aggressors
C. People with severe mental illness are never violent
*Why B: Pihl nuances the 49 to 68% meta-analytic odds increase with symptom type and history of violence, and cites victimization rates of about 11 to 14 times higher.*
