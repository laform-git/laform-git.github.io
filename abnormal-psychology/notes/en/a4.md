# Pihl — Abnormal Psychology Part I, Ch.4: Diagnosing Abnormal Behavior
*A tour of how clinicians actually gather diagnostic information (interview, rating scales, psychological tests, biological measures) and why every one of those tools is fallible, with the false-positive / false-negative problem as the unifying lesson.*

*Source: Dr. Robert O. Pihl, Peterson Academy course "Introduction to Abnormal Psychology Part I," Lecture 4 (transcript). Follows Ch.3 (Classifying Mental Disorders): once there is a classification system, how do you decide which box a person belongs in?*

## Why This Matters
Classification (Ch.3) is only useful if the information fed into it is trustworthy. Pihl's argument is blunt: the most-used diagnostic method (the interview) is the least reliable and least valid, ratings are biased by predictable errors, tests are only as good as their reliability, validity and norms, and biological and imaging tools are promising but not yet specific. The practical payoff is a habit of mind: ask what the error rates are, ask what else could explain the picture, and distrust confident single-source conclusions. The same habit transfers to reading your own lab results and any medical test.

## Why Diagnose at All — Multiaxial Assessment and the Case That Hid a Cause
Pihl opens with a feature of the DSM-IV he liked and regrets was dropped: **multiaxial assessment**, which forced a clinician to assess several layers rather than one label.

| Layer | Question asked |
|---|---|
| **Major disorder** | What is the primary clinical diagnosis? |
| **Personality disorder / deficiency** | Is there an underlying personality pattern or deficit that relates to it? |
| **General physical condition** | Any other medical problems? |
| **Psychosocial / environmental problems** | Housing, income, job, family and how it functions |
| **Global assessment** | How severe is the overall impairment? |

✅ **The case:** a mid-40s car-dealership owner (business near bankruptcy) met criteria for major depressive disorder, with a narcissistic personality style, and had recently been diagnosed with diabetes and hypertension. Standard path: antidepressant plus psychotherapy. Pihl's finding: the drugs for diabetes and hypertension had produced impotence, and that was driving the mood disorder, so it was "easily resolved." His claim: "nine times out of 10" that patient would not get that treatment. 🟡 The "nine in ten" figure is Pihl's own estimate, not a cited statistic.

Three framing quotes he offers: "Before I build a wall, I would want to know what I'm walling in and walling out"; "Nosology necessarily precedes etiology" (you classify before you can explain causes); "Names are bestowed, not found."

## Method 1 — The Interview (Most Used, Least Reliable, Least Valid)

| Stage | What happens | Evidence / example Pihl gives |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Rapport** | Build a relationship; without it, information quality drops | Two groups given an IQ test. The group first asked open, friendly questions scored significantly higher than the group asked name/rank/serial-number questions. Also: roughly one-third of prescriptions are never filled, one-third are filled and taken as prescribed, one-third are filled but seldom taken as prescribed |
| **2. Data collection** | Nonverbal behavior, posture, content taken at face value or interpretively; ranges from free (Rogerian) to highly structured | Depressed people are recognizable by how they move and sit; the interview is needed to learn why |
| **3. Hypothesis testing** | The clinician is always testing ideas, and the ideas come from somewhere (referral letter, expectations) | Same normal student, referral letter on neurologist vs psychologist letterhead produced significantly different reports |

- ✅ **No one is a tabula rasa.** Every evaluator carries priors that shape perception.
- ✅ **Authority and suggestion:** clinicians heard an interview with a normal (non-patient) man. Told by a prominent psychiatrist that he "looks neurotic but is really psychotic," 60% called him psychotic and 40% neurotic. With a prominent psychologist, 28% psychotic and 60% neurotic. With no prestige suggestion, 42% neurotic and 57% normal; suggesting normality made everyone find him normal. 🟡 The student-group figures in the transcript are garbled, so they are omitted here.
- ✅ **Presentation controls the diagnosis (Pihl's own study):** actors read identical scripts (psychosis, or mild depression), but half were told to be animated and aggressive and half held back. The psychotic tape was reliably recognized. The aggressive male was mostly labeled a situational disturbance; the passive male neurotic or depressed. The aggressive female split roughly half psychotic or personality disturbance, while the passive female was called depressive by 80%. The aggressive female also received the most antipsychotic prescriptions, which Pihl calls totally inappropriate. Same words, different demeanor, different diagnosis and treatment.
- ✅ **Self-report memory is poor:** in an Australian gambling-site dataset, of 514 recalled 30-day outcomes only 4% were within 10% of the actual figure; 65% underestimated losses and 70% underestimated the number of bets. People remember wins, not losses.

## Method 2 — Rating Scales (A Structured Interview With Numbers)
A rating scale is "nothing more than a structured interview." Named errors:
- **Leniency:** ratings pile up at the positive end. Example: reference letters, where the same eminent psychologist wrote "the best student I have ever had" for two different applicants in one competition (and, Pihl says, you can no longer write a bad letter for legal reasons).
- **Central tendency:** everyone rated average (B-minus to B-plus), so the scale cannot discriminate.
- **Halo / context effect:** about 15-20% expressed a positive attitude to gambling when asked leaving church versus about 80% when asked at home.
- **False quantification:** assigning equal-interval numbers to things that are not truly equal-interval (his IQ example). 🟡 The exact IQ example is garbled in the transcript; the point is that equal numeric gaps do not guarantee equal psychological meaning.

## Method 3 — Psychological Tests
History: first used by the Chinese around 200 BC for civil-service selection, with the same fairness worries we have now.

**A good test is reliable, valid and applicable.**

| Concept | Question | Pihl's notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Reliability** | If I give it again, or in equivalent form, do I get the same result? | Test-retest (undermined by practice effects; take old MCAT/GRE/SAT papers anonymously first), split-half, equivalent forms. Errors from noise, illness, setting, and above all motivation (he once built a machine that paid kids for correct answers, and IQ scores rose) |
| **Validity** | Does it measure what it claims, and predict something meaningful? | The most important property. **Face validity is "garbage"**: looking like it measures something is irrelevant |
| **Applicability** | Does this person resemble the standardization sample the norms came from? | If they could not theoretically have been in the norming group, the norms may not apply |

**IQ as the worked example.** Despite being a "pariah concept" (racism, politics, statistics, fraud accusations; early California court cases), a general factor **g** exists, is 40-80% heritable, and has good predictive validity across many criteria. The armed services still require an IQ test. Pihl's caveats: never rely on one predictor, and the dropout example shows "it ain't just poverty" (IQ and socioeconomic status both matter).

**The prefrontal argument.** g is largely measured in the prefrontal cortex, which emerged late in evolution; Pihl says the human frontal lobe is about four times larger than in bonobos, chimps and gorillas, whose occipital, parietal and temporal lobes are larger than ours. His group's computerized 12-abstract-picture task (remember what you clicked, pick a new one each time):
- Predicted grade-school grades better than IQ.
- Beat IQ and SAT scores for predicting grades in Harvard students (average IQ around 135).
- Correlated about 0.58 with managerial performance in a corporation (rated twice a year for two years). For comparison: interviews 0.13 ("a waste of time," yet every interviewer thinks they are good), personality measures 0.30, and a job tryout also lower than the frontal testing. 🟡 "I think, something like that" is how Pihl gives the 0.58.
- Pharmacists brought before a board for medication errors (sample of 85; he recalls 11 patient deaths, and in about half the cases the error never reached the patient) were older and did worse on frontal tests (associative learning, go/no-go, working memory, word fluency). Aging preserves facts but erodes processing speed, fluid intelligence and working memory, and errors come from doing too many things at once.

**Projective vs objective tests**

| Feature | Objective (e.g. Wechsler, MMPI) | Projective (Rorschach, TAT, house-tree-person) |
|---|---|---|
| **Stimulus** | Structured | Fluid (an inkblot) |
| **Instructions / answers** | Specific; right or wrong | Indefinite; anything goes |
| **Rationale** | Compare to group norms | The projective hypothesis (you project yourself) |
| **Scoring** | Specific | Interpretive |

Pihl's verdict on projectives: the Rorschach is "a parlor game," and the missing-shadow Wechsler item shows how one wrong answer ("the cane doesn't come clear up to the head") scores zero objectively but gets wildly over-interpreted projectively ("I can jump to conclusions... right, wrong, who knows"). Handwriting analysis (graphology) likewise lacks validity. The screening conundrum: the US Preventive Services Task Force recommends anxiety screening for ages 8-18 (about 8% currently have an anxiety disorder) and depression screening for ages 12-18 (suicide is the second cause of death at 10-19), yet its own text says anxiety screening tools are not sufficient to diagnose and the depression measures have "modest effect." Pihl: "we haven't got the technology," hence treatment by trial and error.

## Method 4 — Biological Measures
**Challenge tests** (do something to the person and watch the response)
- ✅ **Dexamethasone suppression test:** a steroid is given in the morning; normally it suppresses cortisol the next morning. Depressives are thought not to suppress, but it works in only about 60% of depressives, mainly severe ones you would recognize anyway, and people with OCD show the same pattern, so specificity is poor.
- ✅ **Alcohol challenge (Pihl's lab):** an intoxicating dose (blood alcohol about 0.08-0.15) and heart-rate response. Multigenerational and one-generation sons of alcoholics respond differently from those with no family history, identifying a high-risk group.
- ✅ **Amino-acid depletion:** a protein-controlled diet the day before, then a drink lacking phenylalanine and tyrosine (depletes dopamine and norepinephrine) or tryptophan (depletes serotonin). Participants could press a bar for alcohol on a progressive-ratio schedule (30, 60, 90 ... up to 1,500 presses) or for water. Depletion reduced drinking, but those who worked hardest for alcohol were the high-heart-rate responders.
- ✅ **Lie detection (polygraph):** measures autonomic arousal (blood pressure, respiration, galvanic skin response). Ancient versions: Chinese rice-in-mouth (no saliva when sympathetic arousal rises), the Roman Mouth of Truth, tremor in still water, hot knife to the tongue. The guilty-knowledge test uses about 10 questions of five alternatives each: all 10 correct is around one in 12 million, six correct about one in a thousand. 🟡 5 to the 10th power is about 9.8 million, so "12 million" is rounded loosely.
- ✅ **Humans are bad at it:** a meta-analysis of 200-plus studies found about 54% accuracy at telling liars from truth-tellers. Judges, police, FBI, CIA, NSA did no better; only the Secret Service did (trained to read faces), plus abused children and people with left-hemisphere strokes (both read faces closely for different reasons).

**Steady-state tests** (measure what the body produces)
- Liver proteins or an enlarged liver can point to alcoholism. Amyloid in CSF is being studied to pre-diagnose Alzheimer's, which Pihl doubts is useful when nothing can be done or predicted.
- **Drug screens:** cheap immunoassays have the highest error rates; some cough medicines mimic cocaine and certain herbal teas mimic heroin. Employers test because absenteeism, accidents and health claims are higher among drug users.
- **Hair analysis:** Pihl's study found elevated lead and cadmium in learning-disabled children and higher levels in murderers than in those jailed for lesser crimes, and it fed the push to remove lead from gasoline and paint.
- **EEG / evoked potentials:** P300 relates to general cognitive level; neurometric diagnosis from EEG is in doubt.

**Brain imaging**

| Tool | What it shows | Examples given |
|---|---|---|
| **CAT** | Computed X-ray slices; fuzzy | A bleed, but no definitive picture |
| **MRI** | Very clear structure (hydrogen atoms aligned in a huge magnet) | Frontal tissue decreases in heavy cannabis users five years later; personality traits mapped to regions |
| **fMRI** | Blood oxygenation as a proxy for activity | Fearful faces light up amygdala, hippocampus and caudate in anxiety-sensitive people; an intoxicating alcohol dose shuts that response down |
| **PET** | Tracer-based metabolism or neurotransmitter activity | Extra dopamine release in the nucleus accumbens in the high-heart-rate at-risk group (risk via stimulation, not anxiety); serotonin deficits in ecstasy users |

**Pihl's cautions — bandwagon or breakthrough?** Neural activation is assumed to track blood flow and oxygenation, which is not proven; findings are group averages applied to individuals; the same region can light up for lying and for a math problem; neural abnormalities can be present without causing the disorder; fMRI measures blood flow, not nerve firing.

## The Unifying Lesson — False Positives and False Negatives
Every test (polygraph, drug screen, COVID antigen test, medical exam) has both error types. Pihl's polygraph example: assume 80% accuracy and a 5% base rate of dishonesty across 1,000 people. ✅ **Worked arithmetic (mine, not in the lecture):** 50 liars and 40 caught; 950 honest and 190 wrongly flagged. So most people flagged are honest, even with an 80% accurate test. 🟡 Pihl's wording of which error is called "false positive" versus "false negative" is muddled in the transcript; the standard usage is: false positive = test says yes (e.g. lying, disease) when truth is no. Real-world case: of 1,300 positive rapid antigen tests (900,000 run), 1,100 had PCR confirmation and 42% were false positives, traced to manufacturing errors at two companies. His warning: the missed are "tough luck," but the wrongly flagged get drugs or surgery, increasingly under financial pressure.

## Key Corrections to Carry Forward
- 🔴 Assuming a diagnosis comes from objective measurement: it rests on interviews and ratings shaped by rapport, referral letters, authority, sex and demeanor.
- 🔴 Assuming a test that "looks right" is valid: face validity is irrelevant; prediction to meaningful criteria is what counts.
- 🔴 Assuming brain scans show where a mental function lives: activation is a blood-flow proxy and the same area serves multiple tasks.
- 🔴 Assuming a positive test means the condition is present: without base rates and error rates, a positive result can easily be wrong.
- 🔴 Assuming a mood disorder is only psychological: the multiaxial case shows medical conditions and medication side effects can be the cause.

## Practical Takeaway
For any result you receive (psychological, lab, or imaging), ask four questions: how reliable is it on repeat, does it predict anything meaningful (validity), do the norms fit me, and what are the false-positive and false-negative rates given how common the condition is. And always ask what else, including medications and life circumstances, could explain the picture.

## Questions to Learn From (Socratic) — with Answer Options
**1. A middle-aged man with new diabetes and hypertension drugs becomes depressed. What does the multiaxial case suggest the clinician should do before prescribing an antidepressant?**
A. Treat the depression only, since the medical conditions belong to a different specialty
B. Rely on a rating scale to confirm severity and then proceed
C. \* Review the other axes (general medical condition, medications, psychosocial stressors) because a drug side effect may be the real cause
*Why C: in Pihl's case the drugs produced impotence, which produced the depression, so resolving that fixed the mood problem; the multiaxial approach forces those questions.*

**2. A test is 80% accurate and 5% of 1,000 people are actually lying. Roughly what happens?**
A. \* Most of the people flagged as lying are actually honest, because the many honest people generate more false flags than the few liars generate true hits
B. Almost everyone flagged is truly lying, since 80% is high
C. The test cannot produce false positives if its accuracy exceeds 50%
*Why A: 40 of 50 liars are caught, but 190 of 950 honest people are also flagged, which is why base rates matter for every test.*

**3. Which statement best captures Pihl's view of validity versus face validity?**
A. A test that looks like it measures something is probably valid, so appearance is a good shortcut
B. \* Validity means measuring what you claim and predicting something meaningful, and face validity is irrelevant
C. Validity is secondary to reliability
*Why B: Pihl calls validity the most important property and face validity "garbage."*

**4. Why does Pihl call neuroimaging a possible "bandwagon"?**
A. MRI images are too blurry to show cortical structures
B. Imaging has been shown to be useless in cannabis research
C. \* Activation is a blood-flow proxy, results are group averages, and the same region lights up for different tasks (lying and a math problem)
*Why C: these are the cautions he lists: fMRI measures blood flow, not nerve firing, and region-to-function mapping is ambiguous.*
