## Why This Matters
This lecture is the wide-angle map of addiction before the next lecture gets into mechanism and recovery. It covers how the DSM defines and scores addiction, why drug use is old and cross-species, why policy (prohibition, harm reduction, decriminalization) keeps oscillating, and why dose and route of administration decide what a drug does. It also stretches "addiction" to phones, which links directly to your existing dopamine and habit notes. It is a context lecture: lots of statistics, few mechanisms. ## What Counts as Addiction — DSM Scope
- The DSM lists substance classes: alcohol, caffeine, cannabis, hallucinogens, inhalants, opioids, sedatives/hypnotics/anxiolytics, stimulants (amphetamine, cocaine), tobacco, and "unknown" (a placeholder for drugs not yet invented). ✅
- Behavioral side: only gambling is formally in. Caffeine use disorder and internet gaming disorder are under consideration. Pihl says gambling "was added into the DSM-IV" 🟡 (he may mean the newer edition; not verified here).
- Pihl's own broader definition: anything **uncontrolled, consequential, and involving consumption**, which makes the list "almost endless" and individualized. The DSM is slow, but the list "will grow." ✅
- Dictionary-style definition he cites: being enslaved to a habit or practice such that stopping causes trauma. He includes internet, video games and phones.

- Norway removed phones from classrooms for over a year with a reported marked improvement in academic performance and behavior. 🟡 Cited as a just-published study, no details.
- Tangent: a quote attributed to "Albert" (the lecture does not say Einstein; the attribution is commonly considered apocryphal 🟡) about a "generation of idiots," followed by an expert survey (65 domain experts plus ~50 general forecasters): chance of a catastrophe killing 10%+ of humanity by 2100 was 20% (experts) vs 9% (forecasters); extinction (all but 5,000 left) 6% vs 2%; AI named the biggest risk. ## DSM-5 Model — Chronic Relapsing Brain Disease
- Definition as Pihl gives it: a **chronic relapsing brain disease** characterized by compulsive drug seeking and use despite harmful consequences. "Brain disease" because drugs change brain **structure and function**. ✅
- Vicious circle: craving, binge, withdrawal, which increases craving. Second spiral ("whirlpool"): desensitization, so you need more drug for the same effect (tolerance). ✅

### The 11 criteria and severity scale

| Criterion group | What it asks |
|---|---|
| **Impaired control** | Larger amounts or longer than intended; persistent desire/failed efforts to cut down; lots of time spent obtaining/using; strong craving |
| **Social impairment** | Failure to meet obligations at work, school or home; continued use despite social problems; important activities given up |
| **Risky use** | Use in physically hazardous situations; continued use despite knowing it causes harm |
| **Pharmacological** | Tolerance; withdrawal |

| Checkmarks | Severity |
|---|---|
| 0–1 | No diagnosis |
| 2–3 | Mild substance use disorder |
| 4–5 | Moderate |
| 6+ | Severe |

✅ The grouping into four headings is this note's organization, not Pihl's. He read the 11 items in a single run and said clinical involvement tends to come toward the end of the list.

## Natural History — Drug Use Is Old and Not Uniquely Human
- **Osler's claim** (humans are distinguished by their propensity to take drugs) is called wrong by Pihl: elephants raiding rice-beer barrels in northern India, a moose stuck in a tree after probable fly agaric mushrooms, apes digging intoxicating roots in Africa, Siberian deer eating hallucinogenic mushrooms, birds flying into windows after fermented fruit. Animals also select plants for wounds and healing. ✅ (anecdotes, not data)
- Etymology: "berserk" from Scandinavian *berserkr*, warriors in bearskin, which he linked to the fly agaric story. 🟡
- **Three reasons humans use drugs:** (1) to treat disorders (Greeks used opium for depression; Indian medicine used reserpine from *Rauwolfia serpentina* for surgery and psychosis); (2) a second reason that is garbled in the transcript, something about purgatives and Egyptian remedies 🟡; (3) to **alter experience**.
- Deep history: Rudgley's comparison of Paleolithic cave designs with hallucinogen-trip drawings suggests mushroom use predates history; opium poppy cultivated since the 4th millennium BC; Herodotus on cannabis-using Scythians; Egyptians with beer, wine, opium; Incas with coca; Aztecs with mushrooms.
- **Set-and-setting finding:** Aztec accounts (Florentine Codex; Pihl's dating, "late 1600s," looks off 🟡) describe group consumption followed by individual experiences, roughly **three-quarters negative** (seeing oneself sacrificed) and **one-quarter rewarding**. His point: dose, and what the person brings, shape the outcome.
- Societies always restrict some drugs or restrict who may use them (often only authority figures or holy people for hallucinogens). Egypt passed the first drunkenness laws. US alcohol intake: about 2.5 gallons of alcohol per person (as stated). He tells a Mayflower-beer-fight story as colorful history 🟡.
- 1908 Sears catalog sold opium-containing soothing syrup for babies and paregoric. Society later cut smoking prevalence in young adults (2011 to 2022) even though he calls tobacco "dopaminergically the most addictive," his proof that societal-level change is possible. ✅

## Policy — Legal Problem or Health Problem?
- Society is caught between the two and "accepted it's both." Prisons are bursting: 55% of drug arrests are for possession; over 1 million people in the US are in jail or on parole for drug crimes; 87% of organized-crime cash is from drug money (as stated 🟡). Illegality raises prices, which funds cartels (Mexico, with election-candidate murders cited) and pushes addicts into crime.
- **Deaths:** over 107,000 US drug deaths in 2022, driven by fentanyl (synthetic opioid, designed in the 1950s as an anesthetic).

### Potency escalation

| Drug | Relative amount for a typical effect (as stated) |
|---|---|
| Heroin | Baseline |
| Fentanyl | About 1/50 of heroin |
| Isotonitazene ("iso") | About 1/250 (transcript says "one-250th of the size"; the baseline is ambiguous, probably relative to fentanyl 🟡) |

Smaller, stronger drugs are easier to make, hide and smuggle. Pihl says they can be made "in your basement" with the basic chemicals.

- **Harm avoidance (harm reduction):** naloxone (nasal spray or injection, available at US pharmacies) blocks opioid effects, and in some people it blunts the alcohol high too, because it acts through the dopamine system (his explanation). Fentanyl test strips exist because, in a New York City study, only **13%** of street users said they were using fentanyl; most were unknowingly using it. ✅
- Quality of life in this population tops out around **40 out of 100** on health and functioning measures. Treatment funding is rising. He mentions an unnamed long-acting drug that blocks opioid highs for weeks but is hard to get 🟡 (name not captured).
- Downsides of prohibition he lists: more violence and crime, the poor victimized, traffickers as status symbols, erosion of respect for law, and laws that block harm-reduction work.
- **Cannabis legalization:** 24 states (Canada comparison). A study of almost 900,000 high-school students found no rise in teen use after legalization, but his numbers (28% higher odds of zero use, 26% higher odds among prior users) are garbled 🟡. Federally still Schedule I, so legitimate use is clouded, and he calls the state patchwork hypocritical. Psilocybin for depression is mentioned as a promising treatment.
- **Decriminalization:** Portugal's low death statistics inspired Oregon and British Columbia (cocaine, opioids, methamphetamine, ecstasy). Pihl says in BC (Hastings Street in Vancouver) the problem spread "everywhere," BC is asking the federal government to reverse, Oregon is considering repeal, and Toronto should check BC first. His conclusion: "you need a medical approach but not acceptance," and acceptance became "a green light." 🟡 Opinion and anecdote, no data shown.
- **Labeling matters:** he contrasts alcohol and opium on GI cell death, CNS cell death, neurobiological defects and inevitable physical deterioration: alcohol yes, opium no. 🟡 His claim, and a sweeping one; opioids have serious harms of their own (as his fentanyl section shows). He adds chocolate, which acts on cannabinoid receptors, to question where drug lines are drawn.

## Who Becomes Addicted — The Vietnam Finding
Many US servicemen were heroin-positive in Vietnam, but only a very small percentage went on to heroin abuse after returning home (the VA followed them). Pihl's reading: changed circumstances ended the use, and some people lack the biological potential to become truly addicted. He previews that individual vulnerability is coming. ✅ (the percentage is not given in the lecture)

## Consequences He Emphasizes
- Children: rising numbers of kids losing a parent to overdose; foster-care involvement of children of opioid-using parents, especially ages 4 to 8.
- Cannabis (correlational slide): non-disordered users show worse mental health, cognition, academics and delinquency than non-users; those with cannabis use disorder are worst. 🟡 Association, not shown to be causal.
- **Alcohol (legal, and the big one):** third most prevalent health problem; major cause of ER visits; involved in 39% of traffic accidents (down from 50% with advocacy); one-third of general hospital admissions; about 20% of male and 15% of female deaths; about 50% of violent crime. Pihl's own review: 11 countries, 26 studies, almost 10,000 cases, **61% of violent offenders** and **45% of victims** had been drinking. Alcohol dampens evaluative brain areas, so he says it raises vulnerability to victimization (he notes this is not blaming victims).
- Alcohol deaths are concentrated in sparsely populated areas with heavily Native populations, the same pattern as suicide and high stress. Medical list: anemia, arthritis, convulsions, diabetes, high cholesterol, hypertension, coronary heart disease, GI disease.

## Pharmacokinetics — Dose, Route, and Today's Cannabis
- Drug effect depends on **dose and route**, plus absorption, distribution, metabolism, excretion, and age, gender, diet, environment, body weight, health, and drug interactions. "If you don't consider pharmacokinetics... you don't really understand drugs." ✅
- Route example: fentanyl is now smoked off tinfoil rather than injected, which controls the speed of entry to the bloodstream.
- **Jamaica studies:** in the 1970s cannabis was believed to cause brain damage, but that data came from polydrug street users. Pihl's team tested regular users in Montego Bay hills and Trench Town (Kingston) and found no neuropsychological effects beyond education effects. The cannabis averaged **6.7% THC**, comparable to hashish then. Users got the effect they wanted (relaxed, active, healthy).
- **Today's cannabis** reaches 20 to 30% THC with no regulated dose cap, unlike alcohol. MRI shows frontal-lobe differences by dosage, and poison-control reports of overdose, all from highly dosed edibles, have grown. ✅ (MRI and poison-center details are brief and unsourced.)

## Punchlines & Quotable Facts (Lecture 5)
- **"It's a chronic relapsing brain disease."** The DSM-5 framing: drugs change brain structure and function.
- **2 to 3 checkmarks = mild, 4 to 5 = moderate, 6+ = severe.** The 11-item scoring rule.
- **Only 13% of NYC street users who were using fentanyl knew it.** The case for test strips and naloxone.
- **Fentanyl is about 1/50 the dose of heroin; "iso" about 1/250.** Potency drives deaths and smuggling.
- **"You need a medical approach but not acceptance."** His verdict on BC and Oregon decriminalization.
- **Alcohol: 61% of violent offenders and 45% of victims had been drinking** (his 11-country review).
- **Vietnam: most heroin-exposed servicemen did not continue after coming home.** Context matters.
- **Jamaican cannabis at 6.7% THC vs. 20 to 30% now.** Same plant name, very different dose.

## Key Corrections to Carry Forward
- 🔴 "Only humans use drugs": animals do, in many species. Drug use is ancient and cross-cultural.
- 🔴 "Addiction is just weak will": DSM-5 frames it as a chronic relapsing brain disease with structural and functional brain change (Pihl's presentation of the DSM view).
- 🔴 "Drug effects are about the drug alone": dose, route, set and setting (Aztec accounts, Vietnam returnees) and individual factors all matter.
- 🔴 "Legal means safe": alcohol is legal and, per Pihl, drives a large share of deaths, violent crime and hospital admissions.
- 🔴 "Cannabis then equals cannabis now": potency rose from about 6.7% to 20 to 30% THC, so old safety findings do not transfer cleanly.
- 🔴 "Users know what they are taking": most NYC fentanyl users did not.

## Practical Takeaway
Study-level takeaways, not medical directives: (1) addiction is scored on a spectrum of 11 criteria, so severity is graded rather than all-or-nothing; (2) harm reduction (naloxone, test strips, treatment access) and prevention (keeping drugs from kids) are the two pragmatic levers Pihl endorses; (3) always ask dose, route and context before judging a substance; (4) the phone statistics are a prompt to notice your own checking habits, not a diagnosis.

## Questions to Learn From (Socratic) — with Answer Options
**1. Given the DSM-5 scoring Pihl describes, which description is correct for a person who meets exactly four of the 11 criteria?**
A. No diagnosis, because a diagnosis needs all 11
B. Mild substance use disorder
C. \* Moderate substance use disorder, since 4 to 5 checkmarks is moderate and addiction is graded by count
*Why C: the scale in this lecture is 0 to 1 none, 2 to 3 mild, 4 to 5 moderate, 6+ severe.*

**2. Given that only 13% of New York street users who were using fentanyl knew it, which policy response best fits the finding?**
A. \* Drug-checking tools such as fentanyl test strips plus naloxone access, because the main danger is unknowing exposure to a very potent contaminant
B. Rely on users' self-reports to decide who needs naloxone
C. Treat the finding as showing fentanyl is rare in street supply
*Why A: if most users do not know the drug contains fentanyl, self-report cannot guide safety; testing and rescue medication address the gap directly.*

**3. Given Pihl's Jamaica studies versus today's cannabis, what is the most defensible lesson?**
A. Cannabis is harmless at any dose because the Jamaica team found no brain effects
B. \* Dose matters: findings from roughly 6.7% THC cannabis cannot be assumed to carry over to 20 to 30% products, and MRI and poison-control data suggest higher doses matter
C. THC content is irrelevant because route of administration is the only factor
*Why B: Pihl stresses that today's cannabis is a very different drug in dosage, and that effect is a function of dose and route.*

**4. Given the Vietnam servicemen finding, what does Pihl suggest about addiction?**
A. Anyone who uses heroin for months will always become addicted for life
B. Circumstances play no role in continued use
C. \* Context and individual vulnerability both matter: many heroin-exposed servicemen stopped when circumstances changed, suggesting not everyone has the biological potential to become truly addicted
*Why C: he reads the low relapse rate as evidence that environment drove use and that some people lack the vulnerability, which sets up the individual-differences material to come.*
