# Pihl — Abnormal Psychology Part II, Ch.2: Inside Depression
*Pihl's tour of what is known (and not known) about the causes and treatment of depression: heredity, neurochemistry, brain imaging, inflammation, the classic psychological theories, then the full treatment landscape from CBT and SSRIs to ketamine, psilocybin and ECT.*

*Source: Dr. Robert O. Pihl, Peterson Academy course "Introduction to Abnormal Psychology Part II," Lecture 2 (transcript, ~7,800 words, includes a short audience Q&A at the end). Same lecturer as Part I; follows Ch.1 (A Rise in Depression). Lecture content only; the real-world context in the Personalization section is a study connection, not a diagnosis.*

## Why This Matters
This is the "what is actually going on inside" lecture. Its central message is that there is no single depression: there are multiple depressions with different biology, so no single brain pattern, gene, neurotransmitter or drug explains them all. That framing makes every later claim in the lecture (serotonin, genes, treatments with a ~10% specific effect) easier to hold with the right level of caution. It also connects to topics already in your vault: sleep, stress hormones, lipids and glucose, the gut, vitamin D.

## Opening Frame — Sleep and Repetitive Negative Thinking
- Pihl opens with a longitudinal study (500+ people, roughly six years) tracking sleep patterns, repetitive thinking, and depression, anxiety and eating-disorder indicators. ✅
- Finding: initial **sleep problems** were the most significant predictor; they correlated with **repetitive negative thinking**, and those two together led to depression. ✅ (mostly young participants)
- 🟡 He says "five-year" and "six-year" in the same breath; the exact follow-up length is unclear in the transcript.

## Why Women Are Diagnosed More — Four Competing Explanations

| Explanation | Pihl's treatment |
|---|---|
| **Masked depression** (men's alcohol use is really depression) | Dismissed as "not testable. It's just really an idea." |
| **Cultural and role differences** | Women more likely victimized, chronic role strains; argued to be a factor |
| **Biological** (estrogen, HPA axis, brain dysregulation) | Strongest case; he says there is "strong, powerful biological evidence" |
| **Reproductive-event data** | See list below |

- Oral contraceptives and hormone implants may trigger onset; in users under 19 the data show higher depression scores, with crying, hypersomnia, eating problems, tiredness and energy loss. ✅
- Pregnancy: about 20% have depressive symptoms, 10% develop major depression. Postpartum: 10-15% of new mothers, usually two weeks to six months after delivery. ✅
- Prior depression at postpartum or premenstrually predicts depression at menopause; hormone replacement (especially progesterone) is associated with depressive symptoms in postmenopausal women. ✅

## The Causal-Arrow Problem (Key Methodological Point)
- When you study depressed people biologically, they differ **because they are depressed**: disrupted neurochemistry, an activated stress system (cortisol), an activated immune system (cytokines). These can be mistaken for causes when they are effects. ✅
- His overall model (biology affects psychological vulnerability affects stressful events, interacting with stress hormones and interpersonal behavior) is drawn with one-way arrows, which he says is wrong: **every arrow should point both ways**, because behavior changes biology. ✅

## Genetics and Heredity
- Runs in families: risk is far more predictive when both parents are depressed; a Danish whole-population forest plot and a 7-million-person paternal-depression analysis show the same dose-response with inherited load. ✅
- Twin studies (monozygotic vs dizygotic): higher heritability for any mood disorder, bipolar, and severe depression (three or more episodes). ✅
- Depression's heritability looks similar to schizophrenia's, a little less than bipolar's, which suggests a **generalized heritability factor** rather than "depression genes" or "schizophrenia genes." ✅ (his interpretation)
- Gene-hunting is hard for depression: a new locus per roughly **230 cases for schizophrenia, 400 for bipolar, 1,500 for depression**. ✅
- Some genes found affect the **glutamate** system (the major excitatory neurotransmitter); serotonin and norepinephrine genes have also been implicated. ✅
- **Gene x environment example:** in a few hundred Russian juveniles in Siberian detention, only those with a particular dopamine-genotype variant **and** maternal rejection developed depression. ✅ 🟡 He gives no genotype name or sample details beyond "a couple of hundred."

## Early Warning Signs — Blood Markers and Prenatal Inflammation
- A Scandinavian registry study looked back about **30 years** in people diagnosed late in life: differences in **glucose, triglycerides, cholesterol and two other blood fats** appeared 10+ years before diagnosis. ✅
- Kaiser Permanente study: mothers ill in pregnancy with elevated cytokines (**interleukin-6** or **IL-1RA**) had children with different symptom profiles at ages 9 and 11 (externalizing for one cytokine group, internalizing for the other), and **both groups had higher depression in adolescence**. ✅ 🟡 The transcript does not make clear which cytokine maps to which symptom type; infection type was not specified.
- Pihl's conclusion: "Depression begins before pre-birth in these kids" and that is likely a **discrete subgroup**. ✅ (his reading)

## Neurochemistry — From Norepinephrine to Serotonin and Beyond
1. **Norepinephrine first:** early blood studies showed lower norepinephrine and metabolites in depression; norepinephrine had the largest effect and was briefly seen as the cause. ✅
2. **Serotonin took over** because it governs mood, sleep, appetite (the symptoms of depression) and has an integrating role; tryptophan (its precursor) metabolism was studied. ✅
3. **Drug evidence:** reserpine (hypertension drug that lowers serotonin) can cause depression severe enough to hospitalize; reuptake inhibitors, which keep more serotonin in the synaptic cleft, are antidepressant. ✅
4. **Tryptophan depletion** (Pihl's own lab work): a tryptophan-free amino acid mixture depletes brain serotonin within four to five hours; mood drops in some people (notably relatives of depressed patients), and attention to irrelevant dysphoric stories in a shadowing task worsens. ✅
5. **Verdict:** "the model is partially correct, but it doesn't explain the whole thing"; it likely holds for some people (the ones who respond to SSRIs), not others. ✅
- PET data show less serotonergic activity during sadness in the neocortex, hippocampus, caudate and putamen. ✅

## Brain Imaging — "A Lot of the Brain Is Just Different"
- **ENIGMA** consortium (45 labs, 19 countries): in adolescents, lower hippocampal and amygdala volume and thinner cortex; adults show similar findings plus white-matter differences. ✅ (he briefly mis-stated the lab count and corrected himself)
- Anterior cingulate gray-matter density is reduced and correlates with Beck Depression Inventory scores. ✅
- Summary: mostly a **frontal-lobe** phenomenon cortically (dorsolateral, medial, ventrolateral prefrontal; executive function, planning, goal-directed behavior) plus subcortical differences (cingulate, amygdala, hippocampus, caudate, putamen, thalamus; emotional memory, stress response, drives). ✅ Open question he repeats: cause or result?
- **AI attempt (German lab):** classifying depressed vs. normal from MRI, biochemistry and behavior reached only **62% accuracy**, "considerably lower than expected." ✅ Pihl takes it as support for his multiple-depressions point; he expects larger-sample replications.

## Other Biological Associations
- **Cannabis use disorder** (Danish population data): much higher rates of unipolar and bipolar depression, psychotic and non-psychotic. ✅ He flags a cannabis lecture to follow.
- **Diet:** depression risk rises with artificially sweetened beverages and sweet snacks; he admits it is "hard to put into any kind of explanatory framework. But it's got to mean something." 🟡
- **Neurological disease:** depression accompanies Alzheimer's, Parkinson's, Lewy body dementia, MSA, corticobasal syndrome, traumatic encephalopathy, Huntington's. He says this is **not the same** depression: if you have these diseases and are not depressed, "something's wrong"; treatment is about facing reality and death. ✅

## Psychological and Social Theories — Comparison

| Theory | Originator | Core idea | Pihl's take |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Psychoanalytic loss** | Karl Abraham (1911); popularized by Freud (*Mourning and Melancholia*, 1917) | Stuck at the oral stage, dependency, low self-esteem; after a loss, love/hate emotion turned inward = depression | Loss matters but is "more generic"; supported by orphanage marasmus, Romanian orphanages, Harlow's isolated monkeys (protest then despair) |
| **Low positive reinforcement** | Peter Lewinsohn | Few rewards, few skills to obtain them, social avoidance, a vicious circle | Plausible; forest plot shows depressed people have low reward processing; he suggests phones and influencer comparison, fewer siblings, may lower believable reinforcement 🟡 (his opinion) |
| **Learned helplessness** | Martin Seligman | Shuttle-box dogs that could not escape stop trying even when escape is possible | Fits some depressions, **not all**; norepinephrine depletion is a possible subtype link; he cites COVID as a mass example |
| **Cognitive theory** | Aaron Beck | Negative view of self, world and future; negative expectancies, distortions, no motivation | **Most universally held**; basis for CBT; in his shadowing test, helplessness-themed distractors derailed depressed people most |

- Depression is partly self-perpetuating socially: people avoid depressed individuals, "doing the opposite of what they need." ✅
- His summary chart: family history interacts with prenatal environment, maternal neurochemistry, neurobiology, early postnatal environment, personality and cognition, "arrows going in every direction." ✅

## Treatment Landscape
**Guidelines:** children, insufficient evidence ("they should say, 'I don't know'"); adolescents CBT or interpersonal psychotherapy; adults psychotherapy or second-generation antidepressants; older adults CBT or combined medication, group life review. The major bodies (American College of Physicians, APA, NIMH, VA) stratify by severity using a **10-item questionnaire**, with **CBT the "big winner."** ✅

| Option | What he said |
|---|---|
| **MAO inhibitors** | Break down norepinephrine, dopamine, serotonin; "probably the most effective" but can be lethal, last resort |
| **Tricyclics** | Began as antipsychotics; anticholinergic, pronounced side effects, not lethal |
| **SSRIs** | "Clearly the winners": fewer side effects, but nausea, insomnia, sexual dysfunction; **no effect for at least two weeks**. Bupropion (Wellbutrin) is a second-line option |
| **Lithium (bipolar)** | About 78% improvement; acute effect, lower relapse, lower suicide risk; works on a second-messenger system |
| **Ketamine (Spravato)** | Fast: marked drop on day one; question is durability; side effects dizziness, nausea, dissociation; benefit reported 1-2 weeks after stopping |
| **Psilocybin** | 11 US sites: 51 on psilocybin vs 53 on niacin; depression score drop about **19 vs 7 points**; blinding is hard; currently legal for medical use only in Oregon and New Jersey (his statement) |
| **Zuranolone** | FDA-approved for postpartum depression; unlike others, **not** acting on classic neurotransmitter systems |
| **ECT** | Fastest action; ~100,000 Americans yearly; now anesthesia plus muscle relaxant, non-dominant hemisphere, 25 s to 3 min convulsions, 6-9 sessions over 2-4 weeks |

- **Usage:** antidepressant use is much higher in women at all ages, and **highest in both sexes over 60**. ✅
- Probiotics (about 11 strains) reportedly cut depression dramatically; Pihl's reaction: "It makes no sense" and asks who the depressives were. 🟡
- **Vitamin D:** a Finnish twin study found vitamin D in the first two years of life changed internalizing symptoms at ages 6-8. ✅ (infant study only)

## The Placebo and Effectiveness Argument
- Placebo produces **real brain changes** (placebo vs Prozac scans); in depression a large share respond to placebo. ✅
- Pihl: "The selling of antidepressants has depended on a placebo effect." Drug-specific benefit is about **10%**; with placebo, response can exceed 25%. 🟡 His claim, not a settled consensus.
- He says evidence that antidepressants protect against suicide is not substantial, and some studies associate them with higher suicide and homicide risk. 🟡 Contested; attributed to the lecturer.
- Side effects cause dropouts in active-drug arms. ✅
- Psychedelic effects, he reads, are "mostly extra-pharmacological" and context-dependent; they require long monitored sessions, so expensive and slow to scale; a bad trip is individual and unpredictable, just as adverse pharmaceutical reactions are. ✅

## Punchlines & Quotable Facts (Lecture 2)
- **"There are multiple different depressions, and you're not going to find any kind of unifying brain pattern."**
- **All the arrows should go two ways**: behavior shapes biology as well as the reverse.
- **Sleep problems were the strongest early predictor** in the 500-person longitudinal study.
- **Gene-finding: 1 locus per 230 cases (schizophrenia) vs 1,500 (depression).**
- **AI classification accuracy: 62%**, "considerably lower than expected."
- **Blood-lipid and glucose differences appear 10+ years before diagnosis**, visible 30 years back.
- **"Placebo is an active drug, even though it's not a drug."**
- **Psilocybin vs niacin: 19-point vs 7-point drop** in depression scores.

## Key Corrections to Carry Forward
- 🔴 Assuming depression is one disease with one brain signature: Pihl argues for multiple depressions, so imaging, genes and AI all struggle to find a single pattern.
- 🔴 Assuming low serotonin is the whole explanation: tryptophan depletion supports it for some people only; the model is "partially correct."
- 🔴 Assuming brain or blood differences found in depressed people are causes: they may be effects of being depressed; the arrows run both ways.
- 🔴 Assuming the depression seen in Alzheimer's, Parkinson's or Huntington's is the same condition: Pihl treats it as a different problem needing a different approach.
- 🔴 Assuming an antidepressant's whole benefit is pharmacological: placebo accounts for a large part of the response in his account (🟡 contested claim).

## Practical Takeaway
As a study point: the lecture's most actionable threads are upstream of treatment. Sleep problems and repetitive negative thinking are the earliest predictors in the opening study, and CBT (changing how people think about themselves, the world and the future) is the best-supported first-line approach in the guideline summary. Medication and procedure decisions belong with a clinician; Pihl himself notes the GP encounter is rushed and that individual response is unpredictable.

## Questions to Learn From (Socratic) — with Answer Options
**1. Given that tryptophan depletion lowered mood in some people but not others, and SSRIs help only some patients, what does Pihl conclude about the serotonin theory?**
A. It is fully proven and explains all depression
B. \* It is partially correct and likely true for a subset of depressed people, but does not explain the whole picture
C. It was disproven by the first depletion studies
*Why B: Pihl says the model is "partially correct, but it doesn't explain the whole thing" and likely applies to the people who respond to SSRIs.*

**2. Given that depressed people show raised cortisol, cytokines and altered brain structure, why is it risky to call those differences the cause of depression?**
A. Because those differences are never measurable
B. Because heritability studies already prove the cause is purely genetic
C. \* Because being depressed itself changes the body, so these differences may be effects, which is why Pihl says the causal arrows should point both ways
*Why C: this is his methodological caution; studying depressed people biologically reveals what depression does to the body as much as what causes it.*

**3. Given that the Siberian juvenile study found depression only in children with a particular dopamine genotype who were also rejected by their mothers, what does it illustrate?**
A. \* A gene-by-environment interaction: neither the variant nor the rejection alone produced depression
B. That depression is purely environmental
C. That all depression is explained by one dopamine gene
*Why A: only the combination developed depression, the lecture's example of genes and environment interacting.*

**4. Given that the AI classification study reached only 62% accuracy, what point does Pihl take from it?**
A. That AI will never be useful in psychiatry
B. That depression is always easy to detect on MRI
C. \* That there are multiple depressions without a single unifying brain pattern, though larger, repeated studies are still expected
*Why C: he calls the result early-stage, expects it to be redone with larger samples, and uses it to support his multiple-depressions argument.*
