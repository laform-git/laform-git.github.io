# Pihl — Abnormal Psychology Part I, Ch.3: Classifying Mental Disorders
*Explains how mental disorders get labeled and classified (the DSM's strong reliability, weak validity, industry ties) and the dimensional alternatives RDoC and HiTOP, with Dunedin p-factor evidence.*

*Source: Dr. Robert O. Pihl, Peterson Academy course "Introduction to Abnormal Psychology Part I," Lecture 3 (transcript). Third of six chapters; continues the models-of-psychopathology thread from Ch.2 and moves into how disorders get labeled, how the DSM is built, and what the alternatives (RDoC, HiTOP) look like.*

## Why This Matters
This lecture is the "how do we even name these things" chapter. Pihl's core argument: a diagnostic label is a tool with real consequences (for behavior, for treatment, for insurance billing), and the DSM is strong on *agreement* (reliability) but weak on *truth* (validity). Everything later in the course (depression, anxiety, addiction) sits on top of these labels, so knowing what the labels can and cannot tell you is the foundation for reading any mental-health headline, diagnosis, or study critically.

## Part 1 — The Labeling Model
- **Abnormal depends on where, not just what.** ✅ Pihl's claim: whether a behavior counts as abnormal depends heavily on context and culture (dress, sexual behavior, mannerisms, beliefs). Relativity example: a 5'10" man is average, but a "shrimp" among NBA players; one drink a night makes you an alcoholic "in Salt Lake City."
- **Your own label controls your behavior.** ✅ Cited study: people with equal measured heart disease behaved differently depending on whether they *knew* about it and were being treated. Word choice example: "blind" vs. "visually impaired" (most legally blind people retain some vision and use it).
- **Labels are sticky.** ✅ Rosenhan study (from earlier in the course): the label was permanent. Unlike most diseases, where you get well and it is gone, a mental-illness label tends to follow you. People then assume a **sick role**, which reinforces the permanence.
- **Why label at all?** Pihl gives three reasons: (1) comfort and the logic of medicine (no treatment without diagnosis); (2) science progresses description, measurement, prediction, control, so you must describe first; (3) validity, so that a disorder can be defined and then treated.

## Part 2 — The Complex Medical Model (Pihl's "model about to predominate")

| Feature | What it means | Example Pihl gives |
|---|---|---|
| **Same symptoms, different etiologies** | A symptom label is step one of diagnosis, not the answer | Anemia: pages of types in a medical dictionary, each needing different treatment. "Depressed" says nothing about *why* |
| **Disorders vary in kind and degree** | A continuum / normal distribution that includes normality | Disorders shade into the normal range |
| **Interaction** | Predisposition (e.g., genetic) interacts with stress and problems in living | Heart disease cannot be understood without diet, stress, environment; "Happy People Don't Get Cancer" (less likely, not immune) |

✅ Contrast case, the simple **disease model of alcohol** (AA's "one drink, one drunk"): vulnerability, then drinking, tolerance, withdrawal, uncontrolled loop. Pihl's view: partly true for some, but there are "many alcoholisms," just as many schizophrenias and depressions.

**The Bowery study (as told by Pihl):** heavy, daily-intoxicated men were crossed on what they *got* (vodka or tonic) versus what they were *told* they got. How much more they chose to drink tracked what they were **told**, not what was in the glass. ✅ Pihl's conclusion: no uncontrolled physical need; cognition mattered, and some alcoholics can be taught controlled drinking. 🟡 Single study described verbally; effect size and sample not given.

## Part 3 — Rules for Good Labels
1. **High reliability**, meaning diagnosticians agree. Rosenhan's benchmark: **r = 0.90**, which accounts for about 80% of the variance. ✅
2. **Validity**, meaning the label points to something real that can be treated (you cannot have validity without reliability).
3. **Advantages must exceed disadvantages.** Labels should be used judiciously. Pihl notes labels are also used because they are comforting and because **diagnosis numbers equal insurance payment**. ✅

## Part 4 — Classification and the DSM: "The Good, the Bad, and the Ugly"
**Is the DSM an imperialistic assault by psychiatry or a necessary scientific step? Pihl: "yes, it's both."** ✅ Controlling the language is a bid to be "captain of the ship"; it is also a genuine attempt to make labels agreed-upon.

**The DSM definition of a mental disorder has these features (Pihl's points):**
- **Clinically significant:** intended to mean brought to a medical professional's attention (the "imperialist" part).
- **A syndrome:** a cluster of behaviors, never a single one.
- **Results in distress or dysfunction:** negative consequences for the person, family, or social functioning. You can hallucinate and still have no problem if life goes on and no one is harmed.
- **Not expectable:** not a function of an explicit event or situation (Pihl calls this the most important, because it fights the interaction idea).
- **Not mere societal deviance:** meant to keep criminal and certain sexual behaviors out (e.g., a past push to list sexual aggression was resisted so it would not be moved from law into mental illness).
- **Circularity problem:** "Why do they have a disorder? Because of a dysfunction. How do you know? Because it is a disorder." ✅

**Tom Insel (NIMH director 2001-2015), quoted by Pihl:** the DSM is "at best, a dictionary." Strength = reliability (clinicians use the same words); weakness = validity. Unlike heart disease, lymphoma, or AIDS, diagnoses rest on **symptom-cluster consensus, not objective laboratory measures**. ✅ "A Bible of how to name," not how to live.

## DSM History at a Glance

| Edition | What Pihl says |
|---|---|
| Pre-DSM | Hippocratic humors (excess black bile = depression, phlegm, yellow bile, red blood = carefree); Romans adapted them |
| **DSM-I (1952)** | Built on Meyer's idea of reactions to stressors in biologically vulnerable people, plus heavy Freudian influence; roughly 120-something disorders |
| **DSM-II (1968)** | Competitive with WHO's ICD; theoretical and unreliable (psychiatrists watching one interview would not agree) |
| **DSM-III (1980)** | Built to fix reliability via **operational, purely behavioral definitions**, which is why nothing in the DSM refers to the body. "Wise, but now limiting" |
| **DSM-5 / 5-TR** | Dropped NOS ("not otherwise specified"); moved toward spectrums (severe to mild), which blurs where disorder begins. Now **544 disorders** (Pihl's figure) |

**Why new editions appear (Pihl):** legitimate = new science and fixing old problems. Less admirable = competition with WHO (largely resolved), **extending psychiatry's role** into "problems in living" (better taken to a bartender, clergy, or friend), and adding new disorders. **Process:** APA appoints a task force chair, who forms work groups, drafts, field tests, review, publication.

## Big Pharma and the DSM
- **"Siamese twins"** is Pihl's image for psychiatry and pharma; he suggests psychiatry may morph into neurology over 40-50 years.
- Reported figures: half of active psychiatrists received industry payments, but **3% received 80% of the money** ("opinion leaders"). About half of major psychiatry journal papers are pharma-sponsored. **69% of DSM-5 task force and work-group members** reported industry funding; **three-quarters of the 13 work groups** had a majority with ties; some DSM-IV committees were at 100%. 🟡 Pihl's spoken payment total is garbled in the transcript ("812,000 payments worth... 10 billion, 10 million"), so treat the dollar figure as unverified.
- Industry's defense (as Pihl presents it): pharma rewards field leaders, and committees are staffed with field leaders, so overlap is natural. Pihl's question: how much does one influence the other?

## Reliability — Has It Been Fixed?
- **Early definitions were theoretical.** DSM-II's anxiety was literally "a warning from within" (psychoanalytic).
- **Rosenhan-era schizophrenia** definition: disturbance in thinking, mood, or behavior; the pseudo-patients had only the "dull, empty thud" hallucination, and any one symptom sufficed. ✅
- **Current schizophrenia criteria:** two or more of delusions, hallucinations, disorganized speech, grossly disorganized or catatonic behavior, negative symptoms, for a significant portion of at least **one month**; **dysfunction**; signs persist at least **six months**; and **exclusions** (schizoaffective, mood disorders, substances, medical conditions such as post-surgical psychosis). ✅ Pihl's catatonia story is from his 1960s psychiatric-aide days (a catatonic concert pianist; treatments then included wet sheets, restraints, ECT).
- **"Knowing too much" curve:** reliability rises with information, then falls once clinicians split hairs. Culture matters too: the same patients were manic-depressive in Britain and schizophrenic in the US. ✅
- **Two masters:** the DSM is also the "billable bible," so every behavior needs a label, forcing garbage-can categories (NOS, maybe spectrums).
- **Borderline personality disorder:** about 20% of diagnoses at one point, reliability about **0.55**. Pihl's point: five of nine criteria can be met by someone in the days after a breakup, but "personality" means a *consistent pattern*, which a snapshot cannot show.
- **DSM-5 field-trial reliability:** Pihl calls it unimpressive; none near 0.90. Major depression about **0.28** and anxiety about **0.20** (as spoken); autism and ADHD respectable; non-suicidal self-injury even negative. His explanation: anxiety and depression are highly intercorrelated physiologically, genetically, and psychologically. 🟡 Field trials were admitted to be rushed for a publication date, and exact values come from slides read aloud.

## Alternatives to the DSM

| | **RDoC** (NIMH, 2010) | **HiTOP** |
|---|---|---|
| **Unit** | Constructs (e.g., fear) studied across many units of analysis | Symptoms and behaviors grouped into dimensions |
| **Method** | Fill a matrix: genes, molecules, circuits (amygdala), physiology (startle), behavior, self-report, fear conditioning | Hierarchy from general psychopathology down to internalizing/externalizing, thought disorder, detachment, and subtypes |
| **Stance** | Dimensional, agnostic about DSM categories | Dimensional; DSM disorders sit at the bottom of the hierarchy |
| **Pihl's verdict** | Insel later: neurobiological focus "has not translated into public good" (Pihl: either it is not working or the data are not yet assembled for clinicians) | Fits the Dunedin evidence below |

## The Dunedin Study and the p Factor
- Dunedin, New Zealand: assessed at least eight times from age 11 to 38 (Pihl says over 25 years). ✅
- Among people meeting criteria for one disorder, about **66% met criteria for a second, 53% a third, 41% a fourth.** 🟡 Transcript shows "66" without a percent sign once; later repeated as 66%.
- **p factor** (general psychopathology, analogous to general intelligence) relates to comorbidity, severity, persistence, poor family history, poor brain function, poor childhood development, and adult impairment. ✅
- High p predicted **lower brain health at age 3**, lower IQ over time, and an **"older" brain at 38**. The correlation heatmap shows anxiety and depression, and schizophrenia and bipolar, are very similar; cannabis and conduct disorder are strongly related.
- A **Sankey diagram** showed diagnoses shifting over time, which undercuts the idea that a label stays on you permanently. ✅

## Punchlines & Quotable Facts (Lecture 3)
- **"It's both"**: the DSM is an imperialistic assault and an enlightened scientific step.
- **The DSM is "at best, a dictionary"** (Tom Insel): reliable naming, weak validity, no lab measures.
- **Bowery drinkers drank according to what they were told, not what they got.**
- **Reliability target 0.90; depression about 0.28, anxiety about 0.20** in DSM-5 field trials (as spoken).
- **Disorders: roughly 120-something in DSM-I, 544 now** (Pihl's figures).
- **"Psychiatry and Big Pharma are Siamese twins."**
- **Of people with one disorder, about two-thirds have a second** (Dunedin).

## Key Corrections to Carry Forward
- 🔴 Assuming a diagnosis explains the cause: the label names a symptom cluster (like "anemia"), not the why. Treatment needs the etiology.
- 🔴 Assuming the DSM is a validated biological catalogue: it is operational, behavior-based, with no objective lab measures. High reliability was the goal; validity is the weakness.
- 🔴 Assuming more DSM categories means more knowledge: Pihl argues growth partly reflects expanding psychiatry's role, billing needs, and industry ties.
- 🔴 Assuming disorders are discrete and permanent: dimensional evidence (p factor, comorbidity, Dunedin Sankey) suggests overlapping, shifting patterns.
- 🔴 Assuming alcohol dependence is a purely physical, uncontrollable loop: the Bowery study (as told) shows beliefs altered intake.

## Practical Takeaway
When you meet a diagnostic label in a headline, a study, or a conversation, ask three questions Pihl's lecture hands you: what symptoms define it, how reliably do clinicians agree on it, and what is *not* explained (the cause). Treat labels as useful descriptions, not final explanations, and notice how the words themselves ("blind" vs. "visually impaired") shape behavior.

## Questions to Learn From (Socratic) — with Answer Options
**1. In Pihl's anemia analogy, why is saying someone is "depressed" only step one of a diagnosis?**
A. Because depression always resolves on its own once it is named
B. Because anemia and depression share exactly the same biological cause
C. \* Because the label describes a symptom cluster but not the cause; like anemia, there are many forms and etiological pathways, and a meaningful intervention requires explaining the "why"
*Why C: Pihl's complex medical model holds that the same symptoms can have different etiologies, and each may need a different treatment.*

**2. What did the Bowery drinker study (as Pihl tells it) suggest about the "one drink, one drunk" disease model?**
A. \* How much men chose to drink afterward depended on what they were told they had been given, not on the alcohol actually consumed, suggesting cognition, not just an uncontrollable physical need, shaped drinking
B. Men given vodka always drank more regardless of what they were told
C. Men given tonic stopped drinking permanently
*Why A: the told-versus-given crossing separated expectation from pharmacology; behavior followed expectation. 🟡 Based on Pihl's verbal summary only.*

**3. Tom Insel called the DSM "at best, a dictionary." What does this imply?**
A. It is a highly valid biological test panel with weak reliability
B. \* It standardizes naming (reliability) through symptom-cluster consensus but lacks validity because no objective laboratory measures define the diagnoses
C. It is a manual for how people should live
*Why B: Insel contrasted it with heart disease, lymphoma, or AIDS; Pihl added that it is a bible of naming, not of how to live.*

**4. What did the Dunedin findings and the p factor add to the case against treating disorders as separate, permanent boxes?**
A. \* Many people meeting criteria for one disorder met criteria for others (about 66%, 53%, 41% for a second, third, fourth), and a general factor tracked severity, persistence, and brain health, while diagnoses shifted over time
B. People with one disorder never developed another
C. The p factor applies only to children and disappears by age 38
*Why A: comorbidity, overlapping correlations, and the Sankey diagram all support dimensional approaches such as HiTOP.*
