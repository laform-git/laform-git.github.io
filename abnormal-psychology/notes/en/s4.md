# THC & Neurotransmitters
*THC works through one master receptor, CB1, which modulates dopamine, GABA, glutamate, acetylcholine, serotonin, and norepinephrine all at once — explaining the broad, diffuse cost of chronic use.*

## Core Insight

THC does not act on most neurotransmitters directly. It works through **CB1 receptors** — which sit on the terminals of many different neurons — and from that single control point it **modulates the release of nearly every major brain chemical.** CB1 is a master dimmer switch wired into the whole system. This is why cannabis produces such a broad, diffuse set of effects: one receptor, many downstream consequences. This note covers each affected neurotransmitter, what it normally does, and how THC's interference shows up in behavior, alertness, mood, memory, and sleep.

## The Master Mechanism — CB1 as a Dimmer Switch

✅ Rock solid

CB1 receptors are the most abundant G-protein-coupled receptors in the brain. They sit **presynaptically** — on the sending end of neurons — and their job in the natural (endocannabinoid) system is **retrograde inhibition**: they tell the upstream neuron to release *less* of its neurotransmitter. This is a normal, healthy fine-tuning mechanism.

THC hijacks this system. Because CB1 sits on so many different neuron types, THC flooding these receptors turns down the release of multiple neurotransmitters at once. It is not a scalpel — it is a system-wide volume reduction with a few paradoxical increases.

## 1. Dopamine — Wanting, Motivation, Reward

✅ Rock solid (acute increase) / 🟢 Strong evidence (chronic blunting)

**Normal role:** Drives wanting, motivation, pursuit, and movement. The "go get it" chemical (not pleasure itself).

**THC's effect:**
- **Acutely:** THC disinhibits dopamine neurons in the VTA. It shuts off GABA brake neurons, which lets dopamine **flood** the nucleus accumbens — the reward/wanting engine. This is the euphoria and the reinforcement.
- **Chronically:** The reward system downregulates. Baseline dopamine drops, receptors involute. Result: low motivation, flat mood, anhedonia (nothing feels rewarding) when not using.

**Behavioral signature:** Acute craving and reward; chronic amotivation, apathy, and the "nothing is interesting" flatness.

## 2. GABA — The Brake / Calming Chemical

✅ Rock solid

**Normal role:** The brain's primary **inhibitory** neurotransmitter. It calms, slows, and puts the brakes on neural activity. Anti-anxiety.

**THC's effect:** THC suppresses GABA release in the VTA (which is how dopamine gets disinhibited — see above). Elsewhere, the net cannabinoid effect on the GABA system is complex, but the disinhibition in the reward pathway is central to how THC produces its high.

**Behavioral signature:** The removal of the GABA brake in the reward circuit is the core of THC's reinforcing, dopamine-releasing effect.

## 3. Glutamate — The Accelerator / Excitatory Chemical

🟢 Strong evidence

**Normal role:** The brain's primary **excitatory** neurotransmitter. Essential for learning, memory formation, and synaptic plasticity (the physical basis of learning).

**THC's effect:** CB1 activation suppresses glutamate release, particularly in the hippocampus. Reduced glutamate signaling impairs the formation of new memories and blunts synaptic plasticity.

**Behavioral signature:** Impaired learning, difficulty forming new memories, reduced mental sharpness. Chronic heavy use is associated with lasting effects on learning capacity, especially in developing brains.

## 4. Acetylcholine — Focus, Memory, and Dreams

✅ Rock solid (role) / 🟢 Strong evidence (THC suppression)

**Normal role:** The "focus, memory, and dreaming" neurotransmitter. First neurotransmitter ever discovered.
- **Attention & focus:** sharpens signal over noise; enables concentration and filtering out distractions.
- **Memory encoding:** the hippocampus runs on ACh; essential for converting experience into stored memory.
- **Learning:** tags experiences as worth retaining.
- **REM sleep:** the brain enters a high-acetylcholine state during REM. ACh is a primary driver of REM and of dreaming.
- **Body:** drives all voluntary muscle contraction; runs the parasympathetic "rest and digest" system; **signals the bladder muscle (detrusor) to contract and empty.**

**THC's effect:** CB1 receptors sit on acetylcholine neuron terminals in the hippocampus and cortex. THC activating CB1 **suppresses acetylcholine release.**

Mechanism in one line: THC → activates CB1 → dampens ACh release → reduced memory encoding, blunted attention, mental fog, suppressed REM.

**Behavioral signature:**
- Next-day "vague confusion," word-finding difficulty, mental haze
- Short-term memory impairment during use
- Suppressed REM and dreaming (ACh drives REM)
- **Bladder wrinkle (🟡 contested):** ACh drives bladder contraction; THC suppresses ACh; so THC may partly reduce bladder-contraction signaling.

## 5. Serotonin — Mood, Satisfaction, Wellbeing

🟡 Contested / dose-dependent

**Normal role:** Present-moment satisfaction, mood stability, contentment, and emotional regulation. The "enough / at peace" chemical (contrast with dopamine's "want more").

**THC's effect:** Complex and dose-dependent. Low doses may transiently increase serotonin signaling (part of the acute relaxation/mood lift); higher or chronic doses can dysregulate the serotonin system, associated with anxiety, low mood, and emotional blunting over time.

**Behavioral signature:** Short-term calm or mood lift; longer-term mood instability, anxiety, and reduced baseline contentment with chronic use.

## 6. Norepinephrine — Alertness, Arousal, Stress Response

🟡 Contested

**Normal role:** Alertness, vigilance, physical arousal, and the fight-or-flight response. Drives focus under pressure.

**THC's effect:** Cannabinoids interact with the noradrenergic system in complex ways. Acute THC can raise heart rate and produce anxiety/paranoia in some users (a norepinephrine-linked stress response), while producing sedation in others. Individual and dose variability is high.

**Behavioral signature:** For some, acute anxiety, racing heart, paranoia; for others, sedation and blunted arousal. The next-day "woozy/lightheaded" feeling has a vascular/autonomic component here.

## 7. Endocannabinoids (Anandamide & 2-AG) — The Native System THC Imitates

✅ Rock solid

**Normal role:** The body's own cannabinoids. Anandamide (the "bliss molecule") and 2-AG regulate mood, appetite, pain, stress recovery, and the fine-tuning of all the systems above. They are made on demand and cleared quickly — precise, local, temporary.

**THC's effect:** THC is a clumsy, long-lasting imitation of these molecules. It floods CB1 receptors globally and persistently (THC is fat-soluble and lingers), rather than acting locally and briefly. With chronic use, the brain **downregulates its own endocannabinoid system and CB1 receptors** to compensate.

**Behavioral signature:** Tolerance (needing more for the same effect) and withdrawal (anxiety, irritability, insomnia, low mood, appetite loss) when stopping — because the native system has been dialed down and needs weeks to recover. CB1 receptor density recovers to near-baseline in roughly 4 weeks of abstinence.

## The Synthesis — Why the Next-Day "Woozy" State Happens

The diffuse next-day fog is a **multi-system trough** hitting at once:
- **Dopamine** below baseline (the Lembke overshoot) → emptiness, low motivation
- **REM debt** unpaid (ACh suppressed that night) → unrestored, foggy
- **Acetylcholine** dampened → vague confusion, word-finding trouble
- **Residual THC** still binding CB1 (fat-soluble, slow release) → mild lingering woozy/dizzy
- **Autonomic/vascular** effects → lightheadedness on position change

Not one clean symptom — a whole orchestra slightly out of tune at the same time.

## Correction

❌ **Oversimplified belief:** THC mainly affects dopamine / "just gets a person high."

✅ **Corrected:** THC acts on a single master receptor (CB1) that modulates dopamine, GABA, glutamate, acetylcholine, serotonin, norepinephrine, and the native endocannabinoid system all at once. The high is only the surface. The memory fog, dream suppression, mood effects, and even the bladder effect are all downstream of this one broad mechanism.

## Practical

Recovery is the whole orchestra retuning: as THC clears and stays out, dopamine baseline rises, acetylcholine normalizes (sharper memory, vivid dreams), glutamate-driven learning recovers, and the native endocannabinoid system comes back online (roughly 4 weeks for CB1 density, 6–8 weeks for the fuller picture). The vivid dreams, returning focus, and even the sharper detection of "fog" are all signs of the system healing — not problems.
