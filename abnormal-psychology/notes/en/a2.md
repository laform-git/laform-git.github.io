# Pihl — Abnormal Psychology Part I, Ch.2: Shifts in Mental Health Care
*Traces how mental illness care moved from demons to cages to hospitals to drugs, and why the real lesson is that description, measurement and honest science come before any label or treatment.*

*Source: Dr. Robert O. Pihl, Peterson Academy course "Introduction to Abnormal Psychology Part I," Lecture 2 (transcript, including a Q&A section at the end). Follows Ch.1 (Defining Mental Illness). Pihl lectures in a loose, opinionated, anecdotal style; many figures are quoted from memory of slides and some transcript numbers are garbled (flagged 🟡 where it matters).*

## Why This Matters
This lecture is the "how did we get here and how much should we trust the labels" chapter. It explains why mental health care looks the way it does today (deinstitutionalization, drug-first treatment, jails as the new asylums), why diagnoses are so common yet so fuzzy (comorbidity is the rule), and it gives a reusable science toolkit (describe, measure, predict, control) for judging any health claim. It is a study lecture, not a personal one: the best use is learning to ask "what was actually measured, and by whom?" before accepting a label, a statistic or a treatment story.

## Deinstitutionalization — Where the Beds Went
- **Before the 1960s** most severely mentally ill people lived in protected institutions; the lecture quotes roughly **600,000 US inpatients then versus under 100,000 now**. ✅
- **Why it happened:** patients were being "warehoused, not treated," plus the arrival of major antipsychotic drugs. Drugs were seen as a replacement for institutions, and institutions were expensive, so the swap was "very seductive."
- **Where many patients ended up:** the largest de facto mental institutions in the US are described as the **LA County Jail and Cook County Jail (Chicago)**. 🟡 (Pihl's claim, no source given in the lecture.)
- **Arrest disparity:** a sociologist (Pihl recalls "Linda Teplin") rode with police and found mentally ill people were arrested at **about double the rate for the same infractions**. 🟡 (name recalled from memory.) Mental health courts are the encouraging response: more therapeutic, focused on placement and treatment.
- **Homelessness:** counting substance abuse (a full DSM category), Pihl puts mental illness at **close to 50% of homeless people**.
- **The antipsychotic trade-off:** they work, but have major side effects and poor palatability, so many patients avoid them. Pihl's alternative from his own clinical work: anticipate episodes (as in bipolar disorder) and use drugs therapeutically at the right moment rather than continuously.

## How Common Is Mental Illness? — Prevalence Numbers
| Survey / figure | Number quoted |
|---|---|
| One-month prevalence of any disorder (ECA study, 1980s) | 15% |
| Lifetime prevalence (1980s ECA) | 32% |
| Lifetime prevalence (1990 survey) | 48% |
| 12-month prevalence (2003) | 41% |
| Highest-prevalence category | Substance abuse, then anxiety, then depression |

- **Method:** from 1990 Congress mandated structured interviews diagnosed against DSM-III-R, replicated in 2001-2003, all built off the 1980s ECA study. ✅
- **Bias problem:** surveys depend on people agreeing to take a long interview. Homeless, institutionalized and refusing people drop out, so the sample is skewed. A researcher who modeled the non-participants from a 2020 survey (alcohol 5.3%, cannabis 1.68%, opioid 0.68%, stimulant 0.85%) arrived at **much higher true rates**. ✅ 🟡 (adjusted figures not given.)
- **Age of onset versus treatment delay:** anxiety starts around age 11 but treatment comes in the 20s-30s; substance abuse starts around 20 with a 5-9 year delay; impulse control starts around 11 and treatment comes after 4-13 years ("they get treated right away because they're breaking up the house"). Mood disorders were shown at about 30, which Pihl immediately corrects: depression is **bimodal**.
- **Peak prevalence ages** (the age range of the class): anxiety about 30%, depression 15%-20%, substance abuse 16.7%, impulse control 26.8%. 🟡 (garbled in transcript, treat as approximate.)

## Comorbidity — The Rule, Not the Exception
Comorbidity means more than one disorder in the same person, and Pihl calls it **the rule rather than the exception**. Depression travels with anxiety and with alcohol abuse; having one personality disorder predicts having more (odds ratio over 15). ✅

**What it might mean** (Pihl: "we don't know"):
1. One disorder causes another (worry about being depressed becomes anxiety).
2. Both flow from a third problem (a heart disease or cancer diagnosis producing depression and anxiety).
3. Reciprocal causation, where the two feed each other; shared genes producing a "panoply" of conditions.
4. Overlapping symptoms (aggression is not a disorder, but a symptom in many).
5. We may simply not have separate disorders.

**Factor-analytic view:** disorders cluster rather than stand alone.
- **Internalizing:** depression and anxiety (high affinity: if depressed, likely anxious).
- **Externalizing:** alcohol dependence, drug dependence and antisocial personality.
- A third factor involves personality disorders. Pihl describes a **three-factor structure**, and the idea of a general **"P factor"** (general pathology), by analogy with g for intelligence, on which everyone varies. The open question is what determines which symptoms someone develops. ✅

**Why it matters for treatment:** Pihl's own judgment is that treatment in this field is mostly trial and error because it maps poorly onto specific diagnoses. It may map better onto the broad factors (are you internalizing or acting out?), which changes how a psychotherapist should approach the case.

## Who Gets What — Gender Patterns
- Males: more antisocial personality and alcohol abuse. Females: more major depression and simple phobias. Schizophrenia roughly equal (slightly more in women); cognitive impairment nearly equal. ✅
- Females outnumber males in most adult disorders; the balance changes once **childhood disorders** (where males far exceed females) are included.
- Years lived with disability: women higher for anxiety and depression; men higher for antisocial personality, ADHD, conduct disorder and drug use.
- **Depression onset is bimodal:** a peak from about age 15-16 (with girls rising well above boys) and a second peak after about 85. Self-esteem is equal around age 14 but significantly different at 18, and still different at 28. ✅

## Treatment in Practice — Drugs First
- Most people receive **medication in three families**: anti-anxiety, antidepressant, antipsychotic, with little variation inside each. Psychiatric "management" mostly means managing the drug; psychiatrists rarely have time to know you. 🟡 (opinion.)
- **Antidepressants (as presented):** efficacy about **15% over placebo**, with a placebo response of **20-40%**. Pihl's reading: the real "effect" is bigger than 15% once the large placebo response is counted, but a 40% placebo rate says something striking about depression and about how the drug works. 🟡 (single slide figure; see Open Threads.)
- Use statistics: prescriptions **up 34% from 2016 to 2022**; **13.2% of adults** have used antidepressants (17.4% of women, 8% of men). ✅
- **Why no genuinely new psychiatric drugs:** most are "me too" drugs (copy a drug, alter a molecule, re-patent). A true new drug takes roughly 18 years and about **$2.6 billion**, with only about **7% of psychiatric candidates reaching market** (neurology about 6%; the often-quoted 12% is "optimistic"). 🟡 (stage durations in the transcript are garbled, only the total and cost are reliable.)

## Rosenhan (1973) — "On Being Sane in Insane Places"
- Eight pseudo-patients (Pihl says nine people in total including Rosenhan: psychologists, a psychiatrist, a house painter, a housewife, a graduate student) called **12 hospitals** on both coasts, claiming to hear voices saying "empty, hollow, thud." All were **admitted**. ✅
- Once inside they behaved normally. All were diagnosed **schizophrenic** (one manic-depressive), stayed **19 days on average (range 7-52)** and were given **2,100 pills** in total. All were discharged as "schizophrenia in remission." ✅
- **Label stickiness:** "once you have it, that's you." Notes about writing were logged by nurses as "writing behavior." **35 other patients** noticed ("you're not sick, you're a journalist"), the staff did not. Staff ignored questions like "when am I going out?" **71% of the time (psychiatrists) and 88% (nurses)**, with not even eye contact. 🟡 (exact framing of the control comparison is thin in transcript.)
- **Hypothesis myopia:** believing a concept so strongly you can't see what's in front of you.
- **Pihl's twist:** the psychiatrists were not necessarily wrong. Their job is to help the person who presents, not to detect fakes (faking is itself a named syndrome, Munchausen), and in 1973 the schizophrenia criteria were loose enough that **hearing voices alone qualified**. The real issue is **definition and description**.

## The Science Ladder — Describe, Measure, Predict, Control
Pihl's organizing frame: science is a pyramid.

| Level | Meaning | Pihl's example / warning |
|---|---|---|
| **Description** (base) | Say what you observe, without explaining it | Students "describing" a rat on a table jumped straight to theories (fear, nocturnal) |
| **Measurement** | Quantify it | Circular logic: hears voices because schizophrenic, schizophrenic because hears voices. Wastewater study, below |
| **Prediction** | Probability statements; study people at high risk | High-risk family pedigrees (alcoholism); study those who escape too |
| **Control** | Produce or eliminate a disorder | The "gold standard" at the top |

- **Universally valid statements:** he ran a personality test, then handed back reports actually copied from an astrology magazine; **80-90% of people rated them accurate**. "Astrology lives on that," and so do some therapists. ✅
- **Measurement lesson (Po Valley, Italy, 2005):** surveys suggested about **15,000 cocaine doses a month** among ~5 million people; analysis of river wastewater for benzoylecgonine (the cocaine metabolite) implied about **40,000 doses a day**. Lesson: people under-report illegal behavior. ✅
- **Prediction traps:** a psychiatrist who "diagnosed schizophrenia by gait" was in fact seeing the **drug-induced shuffle of earlier phenothiazine treatment** (dopamine blockade, Parkinson-like); dogs "diagnosing" schizophrenia in a Boston-area study were smelling a **fungal infection** common on the same ward. Study people before the disorder, not after treatment and illness have changed them. ✅
- **High-risk example (alcoholism):** sons of alcoholic fathers show physiological hyperreactivity (dampened by alcohol), cognitive differences, hyperactive temperament and impaired behavioral regulation; alcohol both calms the overreaction and gives a dopamine-driven positive effect.
- **Assumptions of the scientific approach:** determinism (necessary conditions produce behavior), measurable things, **operational definitions**, empiricism (observable, repeatable). Aristotle (heart) was "wrong for the right reasons," Plato (brain) "right for the wrong reasons."

## Seductive Wrong Answers — Why Controlled Tests Matter
- **Attachment therapy (tragic):** born of Romanian/Russian orphan adoptions (Ceausescu's pronatalist policy). A "rebirthing" session in which a child was wrapped and weighted with pillows ended in a **child's death**. Lesson: psychotherapy can do active harm, not just waste time. ✅
- **Full moon and lunacy:** the positive studies reflect **Type I error** (ask enough questions and something comes up "significant"). Pihl's transcript says "0.5 level"; the intended threshold is the usual **0.05**. 🟡
- **Facilitated communication:** a split-screen test showed the facilitator's hand, not the child, produced the answers when different pictures were shown. Yet it entered universities, schools and courtrooms. "Beware of great need."
- **Sugar and hyperactivity:** 25 preschoolers and 23 six- to ten-year-olds, rated sugar-sensitive by parents, got sugar, saccharin or aspartame in randomized three-week blocks, with 39 outcome measures. **No effect.** ✅
- **Theory as a plan:** Poincaré's "a pile of bricks is not a house." A good theory's language must be independent of the theory (anxiety as a "warning from the ego" is untestable) and reducible to physical, measurable terms.

## Five Historical Models of Abnormality
| Model | Core idea | Notes from lecture |
|---|---|---|
| **Possession** | Evil spirits | Trephination (healed skull holes, ~5000 BC); *Malleus Maleficarum* (1486) laid out how to find and treat witches; context: collapsing feudalism, rising Church, Black Plague (up to 60% dead) |
| **Animal** | Mentally ill seen as non-human | Cages, chains, straw; an English hospital charging a penny on Sundays, about 96,000 visitors in a year; Pinel (credited with unchaining patients, but learned it from a colleague three years earlier) |
| **Simple illness** | Illness like any other | Rights: care and exemption from roles; obligation: seek help. Szasz, *The Myth of Mental Illness* |
| **Complex medical** | Alive and well today | Multiple causal factors |
| **Labeling** | Labels shape the person | Relevant, "not so much" (Rosenhan-style effects) |

**Simple illness model — Pihl's critique:**
- Assumes illness is discontinuous with normal behavior; Pihl argues for **one distribution**. Dostoevsky: "it's by isolating our neighbors that we convince ourselves of our sanity."
- Assumes a growing list of separate illnesses (the DSM's logic), and that treatment belongs to physicians. Upside: it took mental illness **out of the hands of clergy and politics**, though political misuse persists (he cites petitioners in China locked up as mentally ill).
- **Looks only inside the person:** the Denver asthma-children study (Purcell). About half recovered on arrival; removing parents from the home and using babysitters improved the others, implicating the child-parent relationship rather than allergens. ✅
- **Implies physical intervention:** lobotomy (Moniz, Nobel Prize; "Ice Pick" Freeman), wet-sheet wraps, heavy ECT. Today's parallel: ADHD gets stimulants that adjust dopamine; "so much easier than parenting? Who knows?" 🟡 (opinion.)
- **Shapes institutions:** old leprosariums became asylums.
- **Where you are treated matters:** first-episode schizophrenia recovery looked **far better in less industrialized, more collective cultures** than in Western ones (more roles to play, structured communities). 🟡 (the transcript phrase "a back culture" and the city-by-city figures 12/2/6/5 are garbled; the recovery rate quoted as "20 years, 20%" is unclear.)

## Q&A Highlights (end of lecture)
- **Why is the placebo so strong?** Belief; and GPs readily prescribe an antidepressant after a few reported symptoms. Pihl's own survey of women on psychotropic drugs (sampled from voter rolls) found they struggled to **define a problem** in a problem-solving task, not "just feeling bad." 🟡 (one study, details not given.)
- **How reliable is a diagnosis?** "Two totally opposite answers": a diagnosis can **solve** a problem (an understandable explanation helps; many psychotherapies do this) or be **destructive** depending on what it asks you to do. He recounts ADHD children once slated for bilateral amygdalotomy, in an era before CT scans, and urges not rushing to a purely pharmacological conclusion: "find somebody who's got a good head who can shepherd you through the medical morass."

## Key Corrections to Carry Forward
- 🔴 Assuming deinstitutionalization "worked" because patients were treated elsewhere: the lecture's account is that care largely moved to streets and jails, with drugs replacing beds.
- 🔴 Assuming survey prevalence numbers are exact: non-participation biases them, and modeled estimates for substance use run much higher.
- 🔴 Assuming each diagnosis is a separate disease: comorbidity and factor analysis suggest overlapping, general dimensions (internalizing, externalizing, a P factor).
- 🔴 Assuming self-report is a good measure of illegal or stigmatized behavior: the wastewater study shows the gap can be huge.
- 🔴 Assuming an expert's clinical eye or a smart "diagnostic" shortcut is valid: the gait and dog examples were reading drug effects and fungal infection.
- 🔴 Assuming a label is a neutral description: once applied it can be hard to remove (Rosenhan), though it can also genuinely help (Q&A).

## Practical Takeaway
When you meet a health claim, run Pihl's ladder: Was it **described** without interpretation? **Measured** in a way independent of the theory? Is there a **control** condition (sugar study, facilitated communication, placebo)? And who is the measuring instrument (the facilitator, the survey respondent, the clinician)? Labels and treatments deserve the same scrutiny, with openness to the fact that sometimes a good explanation or a well-chosen drug really does help.

## Questions to Learn From (Socratic) — with Answer Options
**1. Pihl says comorbidity is "the rule rather than the exception." What is the most defensible conclusion about what this means?**
A. Each co-occurring disorder is proven to be a separate, independent disease
B. Depression always causes anxiety, never the reverse
C. \* The meaning is unresolved: one disorder may cause another, a third problem may drive both, symptoms may overlap, or the categories may not be truly separate, which is why factor analyses find broad internalizing and externalizing clusters
*Why C: the lecture lists several possible explanations and states "we don't know." Options A and B claim a certainty the lecture explicitly denies.*

**2. In the Po Valley study, survey data implied about 15,000 cocaine doses a month while wastewater analysis implied about 40,000 a day. What is the main lesson?**
A. \* Self-report is unreliable for illegal behavior, so independent, objective measurement can reveal much larger true rates
B. Wastewater always overestimates drug use
C. Surveys are accurate if the sample is large enough
*Why A: Pihl's point is that you "won't learn much asking people" about illegal activity; sample size cannot fix systematic under-reporting.*

**3. Why was the Rosenhan result not simply proof that psychiatrists were incompetent, according to Pihl?**
A. Because the pseudo-patients really were mentally ill
B. \* Because the psychiatrists' job is to help the person who presents rather than to hunt for fakes, and 1973 criteria allowed a schizophrenia diagnosis on hearing voices alone, making definition the real problem
C. Because the hospitals never actually admitted anyone
*Why B: he explicitly says it is not the psychiatrist's job to look for fakes, and that the "problem of definition" and "hypothesis myopia" are the core issues.*

**4. A psychiatrist seemed able to "diagnose schizophrenia by the way patients walked," and dogs seemed able to pick out patients from a lineup. What explains these results?**
A. Schizophrenia has a unique walking pattern and a distinctive smell
B. The psychiatrist and the dogs were using chance alone
C. \* The patients had been treated with phenothiazines (dopamine-blocking drugs that cause a shuffling gait) and lived on a ward with a fungal disorder, so the "diagnostic signs" were treatment and environment effects, which is why high-risk people should be studied before illness and treatment
*Why C: both examples show how studying already-ill, already-treated people sends researchers up "blind alleys," Pihl's argument for prospective high-risk designs.*
