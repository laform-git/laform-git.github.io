# Pihl — Abnormal Psychology Part II, Ch.6: Breaking the Cycle
*Pihl's closing lecture: drug-taking is driven by a dopamine-system susceptibility plus personality-matched motives (anxiety sensitivity, hopelessness, impulsivity, sensation seeking), so prevention works best when it is targeted rather than generic.*

*Source: Dr. Robert O. Pihl, Peterson Academy course "Introduction to Abnormal Psychology Part II," Lecture 6 (transcript). Final lecture of the six-part series (A Rise in Depression, Inside Depression, Recognizing Risk, Reducing Risk, The Scope of Addiction, Breaking the Cycle). Much of it is Pihl's own lab research, presented as lecturer-reported findings.*

## Why This Matters
This lecture answers the question the whole course has been building toward: why do some people who "should" become addicted never do, and why do others slide in? Pihl's answer is that people use drugs for different reasons, that those reasons map onto identifiable personality profiles, and that a half-hour intervention matched to the profile beat a generic one. It also reframes the opioid crisis, pharmaceutical marketing, and prescribing habits as part of the learning environment around drug use. For your notes, it is the bridge between the dopamine material already in your vault and a practical, testable prevention model (SURPS).

## Why Study the At-Risk, Not the Addicted
Pihl's central methodological point: if you study alcoholics, you are "studying the explosion, not the triggering mechanism." Any brain or personality difference could be a *result* of heavy use. So his lab does **high-risk research**: find people likely to develop a problem who do not have it yet, give them a drug under controlled conditions (a "drug challenge"), and record what happens. This also lets you ask why some at-risk people *don't* develop the problem, and it exposes feedback loops and developmental timing (for example, schizophrenia's strong genetics but late-teen onset). ✅

The initiation, individual-difference and maintenance stages are treated as separate questions with different biology.

## The Family-History Alcohol Challenge — What Pihl Found
Subjects: sons of multigenerational, male-line alcoholism (cross-fostering studies support inheritance, which is stronger in males but present in females) and anxiety-sensitive people. Baseline sober heart rate, then an alcohol challenge, with heart rate, cognition, learning, drinking behavior and brain activity measured.

- **Heart rate as reward readout:** heart rate rises with reward. It rose significantly with alcohol in the anxiety-sensitive and multifamily-history groups, not in family-history-negative controls. ✅
- **Countdown-shock test:** controls' heart rates rise in anticipation of shock. In the two at-risk groups, heart rate went *down* when intoxicated, meaning alcohol dampened the response to an aversive event. Pihl reads this as negative reinforcement. ✅
- **Sex differences:** in multigenerational groups, women showed a decrease and men a dramatic increase. 🟡 Brief, graph-based passage.
- **Pre-existing differences:** 11 and 12-year-old boys from this background already show frontal-lobe weaknesses (word fluency, Wisconsin Card Sort, self-ordered pointing), plus histories of conduct disorder and hyperactivity. ✅
- **Subjective and pharmacokinetic:** they feel better for longer, their blood alcohol stays high for over an hour (normals peak, then fall below baseline), they recall more positive words after drinking, and show higher plasma endorphin. 🟡 The "more risk-taking but better impulse control" result is stated too tersely to interpret.
- **Naltrexone blocked the heart-rate response**, which Pihl takes to mean alcohol is acting through the endogenous opioid and dopamine system. ✅
- **PET scan:** alcohol triggers dopamine release in the **nucleus accumbens**, especially in these at-risk people. ✅

## Dopamine, DRD2 and Why Some Drugs "Take Control"
- Amphetamine, nicotine, alcohol and ketamine all release dopamine, THC only a little. Pihl says morphine, opioids and fentanyl would look the same "only more so." Two dopaminergic systems both originate in the nucleus accumbens and energize the prefrontal and cingulate cortex: signals of action and of possible reward (and punishment). ✅
- **Addicted individuals respond differently** from non-addicted ones, and use leads to *decreased* functioning of the dopamine system, hence the need for more drug (neuroadaptation). ✅
- **Genetics:** a recent genome-wide association study of over a million drug users, in which Pihl says the DRD2 (dopamine receptor) gene was the most prevalent hit. 🟡 He could not read the drug name on the slide. Other genes named: alcohol dehydrogenase, a serotonin gene, a GABA receptor gene. He states "unequivocally" that most drug users carry a dopamine-related physiological susceptibility. ✅
- **Treatment logic:** methadone or buprenorphine act on the same system, long-acting and legal and medical. Pihl frames the choice as abstinence or accepting a managed, cleaner substitute (he notes physicians dependent on opiates often functioned fine with a clean supply).
- **Gambling** looks the same: underactive mesolimbic reward system. He cites a 75% recent growth in gambling problems. Among pathological gamblers: 26% divorced, 34% lost a job, 50% stole money, 18% arrested, 6% suicidal thoughts, and tolerance develops. DSM criteria listed (preoccupation, escalating bets, failed cut-backs, chasing losses, lying, illegal acts, lost relationships); the lecture says five or more are needed. 🟡 Thresholds vary by DSM edition; not verified here.
- **Opioid crisis:** Pihl lays blame on medicine and the legal system: OxyContin prescribed even after wisdom-tooth removal, Purdue Pharma's multibillion-dollar fines, and a pill-mill clinic story from his own neighborhood. 🟡 Anecdote.

## Dependence Risk and Heritability by Drug

| Drug | Estimated dependence among those who tried | Twin/heritability note (as stated) |
|---|---|---|
| **Nicotine** | About one-third | Not discussed |
| **Heroin** | 23% | Not discussed |
| **Cocaine** | 17% | Lesser twin effect, so some users have no heritable predisposition |
| **Alcohol** | 15% | Genes: DRD2, ADH, serotonin, GABA |
| **Stimulants** | 11% | Weak for dependence |
| **Cannabis** | 9% | Definite twin effect for abuse and dependence |
| **Psychedelics** | 5% | Big heritability effect (hallucinogens) |
| **Sedatives** | Not given | Big twin effect |

Caffeine and antidepressants have no dependence liability and do not touch mesolimbic dopamine. ✅ Comorbidity matters: people with both bipolar disorder and alcohol use disorder show distinct right frontal gyrus and insula findings versus either alone, so "you have to be careful about who you're studying." A reactivity meta-analysis is summarized as "the fundamental finding is that there's no finding." 🟡

## Two Personalities, Two Reasons for Drinking
The scanning study (double-blind, placebo-controlled): 11 anxiety-sensitive and 11 sensation-seeking men, and the same for women; alcohol dosed to about 0.08 to 0.10 blood alcohol, scanned 15 minutes later while viewing fearful faces and doing the Montreal Stress Task (math plus social pressure).

- **Anxiety-sensitive people (placebo):** hippocampus, amygdala and caudate hyperactive to fearful faces. **Drunk: that response disappears.** Alcohol removes what bothers them, which is why it becomes a problem for them. ✅
- **Sensation-seekers:** no such effect either way. Sensation-seeking women showed much more brain activation under alcohol than anxiety-sensitive women. ✅
- Math stress: men reacted strongly, women did not (Pihl jokes about why). 🟡
- **Female prisoner study:** high in sensation seeking, reward sensitivity and stimulant use; low in punishment sensitivity, anxiety sensitivity and hopelessness ("so what do we do? We punish them"). In a 3-year follow-up, 11 of 23 were interviewed and about half now had a drug or alcohol diagnosis. ✅ The anxiety-sensitive subjects with the biggest dampening of the fear response were the ones who later became addicted. 🟡 Very small sample.

## How We Learn to Use Drugs
- **Pharma and prescribing:** Pihl says companies spend 6 to 8 billion a year on consumer ads (only the US and New Zealand allow direct-to-consumer, "that I know of"), spend more on marketing than R and D, push physician-education events, and help create disease categories (from 100-some disorders in 1952 to 500-some). 🟡 Lecturer's assertions, partly opinion. His caveat: no one is "pure of heart," so industry needs monitoring rather than outrage.
- **GP visits:** he cites studies that 60% of GP patients are not ill; the default response is a prescription (an antidepressant, since placebos are illegal). Younger, more authoritarian physicians prescribe more; women get 2 to 3 times more prescriptions, perhaps because they want to talk and there is no time. 🟡
- **180-woman study (voter lists):** regular and, to a lesser degree, irregular anti-anxiety drug users were significantly worse than non-users at defining a problem, its causes and solutions in taped scenarios. Pihl's reading is that the cognitive style came first and the prescription followed. 🟡 He admits he is "hypothesizing backwards"; this is correlation.
- **Adolescent risk factors:** low grades, low religiosity, early alcohol use, low self-esteem, psychopathology, poor parent relationship, low social conformity, sensation seeking, perceived peer and adult drug use. Risk of substance abuse climbs steadily with the number present. ✅
- **Price beats persuasion:** smoking fell mostly because cigarettes got expensive.
- **Set and setting:** cannabis in Montreal was a spiritual, sexual, cohesive experience; in California, thought disruption and withdrawal. In his "pad" lab, groups of men sat silent while groups of women chattered; offered $5, subjects judged distances accurately. His conclusion: at low doses it was "the world's most used placebo." ✅ for expectancy as a theme, 🟡 as an old, informal result.
- Parents teach it too: we learn early that pills solve problems and often do not learn to discriminate.

## Treatment — What Works and What Doesn't
- **Rarely treated** (8% and 38%), and doctors treat the comorbid disorder first: if you are depressed and alcoholic you will be treated for depression. Relapse is the rule. About 80% are never treated. ✅
- **Generic school prevention can backfire:** DARE-style police talks increased use. Early drinking strongly predicts later dependence, so a negative environment for alcohol (not athletes selling booze) matters. ✅
- **Most drug programs work by substitution** with another dopaminergic drug. Behavioral approaches with positive effect sizes: contingency management, CBT, CBT plus contingency management, and relapse prevention. 🟡 Pihl says he does not believe the effect sizes for polysubstance, cocaine, opioid and cannabis use.
- **Medications for alcohol use disorder:** number needed to treat vs placebo of 11 for acamprosate and 18 for naltrexone (disulfiram also listed). Pihl says the trials were designed wrongly: a drug challenge would test naltrexone's effect directly rather than over weeks. 🟡
- **Psilocybin** in alcohol use disorder: significant positive findings, part of a broader pattern across mental illness. 🟡 Early evidence, described without detail.
- A report he cites says there are no good prevention measures. Pihl: "they're wrong."

## The Targeted Prevention Model (Conrod and SURPS)
Study of **300 substance-abusing women** recruited by ad (too many responded, ad pulled after a day or two), all diagnosable with addiction, given an extensive battery. Factor analysis gave four factors, cluster analysis five profiles:

| Profile | Traits | Risk relative to the others (as stated) |
|---|---|---|
| **Hopeless / introverted** | High hopelessness, low sensation and impulsivity | 3x major depressive episode, 2x panic, about 3x social phobia, about 3x opioid dependence |
| **Anxiety-sensitive (fearful)** | High fearfulness, low on all else | 2x simple phobia, almost 6x somatization, 3x anxiolytic abuse |
| **Sensation-seeking** | High sensation seeking only | No specific pathology; 4x alcohol dependence |
| **Impulsive** | High impulsivity and sensation seeking | 3x antisocial, 2 to 3x alcohol, about 2x cocaine dependence |
| **Low personality risk** | Few women; each had an addicted partner | Not given |

**"The kind of drugs that people are using is fitting their kind of pathology."** ✅

- **Brief intervention (30 minutes):** educational film vs mismatched training vs **motivationally matched** (depressed women taught how not to be depressed). Over six months, half the matched group were no longer dependent. 🟡 Lecturer-reported; he wonders if the whole day of self-discovery mattered.
- **SURPS (Substance Use Risk Profile Scale)** measures hopelessness, anxiety sensitivity, impulsivity and sensation seeking, and is used internationally. Impulsive and sensation-seeking kids (stimulants, externalizing) need different interventions from hopeless or anxiety-sensitive ones. ✅
- **School program:** teachers/counselors deliver short group interventions after assessment; follow-up shows slower growth in illicit drug use through 24 months, less binge drinking, and fewer depression, panic, suicidal-ideation, anxiety, conduct problems and (in a British sample) less shoplifting. It lowers rather than eliminates use. ✅
- Pihl's recommendation: assess risk early at school and family level, diagnose the etiology, target it. Prevention is far more effective than treating established physical and psychological effects. He closes by calling for societal consensus.

## Punchlines & Quotable Facts (Lecture 6)
- **"You're studying the explosion, not the triggering mechanism."** — why high-risk research beats studying addicts.
- **"The kind of drugs that people are using is fitting their kind of pathology."**
- **Anxiety-sensitive brains "light up" to fearful faces; drunk, the response is gone** — the mechanism of alcohol as self-medication.
- **Heart rate fell when at-risk drinkers faced a countdown to shock** — intoxication neutralizes the aversive.
- **"The world's most used placebo"** — Pihl on cannabis at low doses and social setting.
- **Half of the matched-intervention women were no longer dependent at six months — after 30 minutes.**
- **"If you can sleep without the aid of drugs... then you're a dog. Bark on."** — his closing joke on how impossible drug-free perfection is.

## Key Corrections to Carry Forward
- 🔴 Assuming all drug users share one motive: the same drug is taken for different reasons (relief from fear, relief from hopelessness, thrill, impulsivity), and each profile needs a different intervention.
- 🔴 Assuming studying addicts reveals the cause: brain and personality differences in dependent people may be consequences of use; at-risk, pre-problem samples are needed to separate cause from effect.
- 🔴 Assuming generic, scare-based prevention helps: DARE-style programs increased use per Pihl; targeted, profile-matched ones reduced it.
- 🔴 Assuming a diagnosis comes with its own treatment: in practice comorbid disorders get treated first and the addiction often goes untreated.
- 🔴 Treating dopamine as the whole story: Pihl is clear there are also heritable, personality, learning and environmental routes, and the genetic evidence is mixed (DRD2 prominent, reactivity studies inconclusive).

## Practical Takeaway
For understanding substance use, ask *what does the substance do for this person* (calm fear, lift hopelessness, add thrill, feed impulsivity) rather than asking only whether they use. For prevention, early, short, profile-matched approaches (SURPS-style assessment) get the strongest results in Pihl's data. This is a study framework and lecturer-reported research, not clinical advice.

## Questions to Learn From (Socratic) — with Answer Options
**1. Pihl argues that studying alcoholics tells you little about cause. What research strategy does he use instead, and why?**
A. Follow only people already diagnosed, since they show the clearest effects
B. \* High-risk research: study people likely to develop a problem before they have one and record their reactions to a drug challenge, because findings in addicted people could be consequences of heavy use rather than causes
C. Use animal studies exclusively, since human samples are too varied
*Why B: this is his stated reason for studying "the triggering mechanism, not the explosion."*

**2. In the fear-faces scan study, what happened in anxiety-sensitive people when they were drunk, and what does it imply?**
A. Their amygdala response intensified, making alcohol more distressing
B. Nothing changed, so anxiety sensitivity is unrelated to drinking
C. \* Their hippocampal, amygdala and caudate hyperactivity to fearful faces disappeared, suggesting alcohol works as relief from distress and carries addiction risk for them
*Why C: this is Pihl's central demonstration of a personality-matched motive; sensation-seekers showed no such effect.*

**3. What did the Conrod 30-minute brief intervention show, and what is the catch?**
A. \* Matching the intervention to the woman's own profile (for example, depressive vs anxious) was associated with about half no longer being dependent at six months, though it is lecturer-reported and the full day of assessment may have contributed
B. A generic educational film worked best because it reached everyone
C. Teaching an anxiety skill to depressed women worked equally well, so matching did not matter
*Why A: the mismatched and film groups were controls; Pihl himself wonders whether the whole day of self-learning drove the effect.*

**4. Which statement best matches Pihl's view on generic school prevention and treatment access?**
A. Police-led school talks reduce use, and most addicted people receive treatment
B. Treatment is plentiful, but prevention is not worth trying
C. \* Generic programs such as DARE increased use in the studies he cites, only a small minority are ever treated (8% for abuse, 38% for dependence), and he favors early targeted prevention
*Why C: it combines his DARE point, the treatment-gap statistics, and his recommendation to intervene on predisposing factors.*
