# PERFECT SLEEP — 15 GERD and Sleep

*Long-standing GERD is a real sleep factor that is rarely examined as one.*

## Silent reflux is the point of this note

The assumption is that reflux disturbs sleep by causing a waking with heartburn. The more common and more damaging pattern is the opposite: **acid reaches the oesophagus, produces a brief arousal, and sleep resumes without conscious waking.**

The result is the same signature as apnea ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 14 Sleep-Disordered Breathing]]) — repeated arousals preventing descent into N3 and truncating REM, producing unrefreshing sleep with no remembered cause. Someone can have significant nocturnal reflux and report no night-time symptoms at all.

**A person with no heartburn complaints can still be losing sleep architecture to reflux.**

## Why night reflux is worse than day reflux

Three defences are lost lying down and asleep:

1. **Gravity.** Upright, acid drains back. Supine, it does not.
2. **Swallowing.** Swallowing clears the oesophagus and delivers saliva. **Swallowing nearly stops during sleep**, so acid sits in contact with the mucosa far longer per episode.
3. **Saliva.** Bicarbonate-rich saliva neutralises acid, and salivary production falls markedly during sleep.

So each night-time reflux event lasts longer and does more damage than a daytime one — which is why nocturnal reflux carries a disproportionate share of the complications.

## The loop with sleep

It runs both ways, like most things in this series.

- Reflux fragments sleep.
- **Sleep deprivation increases oesophageal sensitivity to acid** — the same exposure produces more symptoms and more arousals when underslept.

## Position — an asymmetry worth knowing

This is the most actionable and least known item in the note.

**Left side reduces reflux. Right side increases it.**

The reason is anatomical. The stomach sits to the left, and the junction with the oesophagus enters from the right. Lying on the **left**, the junction sits above the pool of gastric contents — acid has to travel uphill. Lying on the **right**, the junction sits below the pool, and the lower oesophageal sphincter also relaxes more frequently in that position. Studies consistently show more reflux episodes and slower acid clearance on the right side.

**Supine** is intermediate but poor, because there is no gravity gradient at all.

Left-side sleeping also happens to be the position favoured for glymphatic clearance ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 03 Glymphatic Clearance]]) and generally better for airway patency ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 14 Sleep-Disordered Breathing]]). Three benefits, one habit.

## Head-of-bed elevation

Raising the head of the bed by six to eight inches reduces nocturnal acid exposure substantially. The mechanism is gravity acting across the whole night.

**Extra pillows do not work and may make it worse** — they bend the body at the waist, raising abdominal pressure while leaving the stomach–oesophagus relationship unchanged. The whole bed must be tilted: blocks or risers under the head-end legs, or a wedge running the length of the torso.

## Timing and food

- **Three hours from last bite to lying down** is the standard interval, and it is the same interval already required for fluid ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 05 Night Fluid]]) and core temperature ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 04 The Clock]]). One rule serving three purposes.
- **High-fat meals** delay gastric emptying, leaving the stomach full longer.
- Common triggers: alcohol, chocolate, mint, caffeine, citrus, tomato, and spice — all relax the lower oesophageal sphincter or irritate directly. **Mint is worth naming** because it is often assumed to be soothing and does the opposite.
- Large meal volume is itself a trigger regardless of content.

## The interaction with apnea

Worth knowing even where apnea has been excluded. The two conditions aggravate each other: the negative chest pressure generated during an obstructive event actively **pulls** gastric contents upward. In someone with both, treating one improves the other.

## Medication note

Acid suppression is a physician decision, not a self-management one. Two things worth knowing:

- Long-term proton pump inhibitor use has ongoing debate around magnesium and B12 absorption — relevant for anyone also taking a magnesium stack like the one in [[PERFECT SLEEP — 08 Substances and Stack]].
- Positional and timing measures are first-line and have no such trade-offs. They are worth exhausting before escalating.

## WHAT TO HOLD ONTO

- Silent nocturnal reflux fragments sleep without a conscious waking. No heartburn does not mean no reflux.
- Swallowing and saliva both nearly stop during sleep, so each night-time episode lasts far longer than a daytime one.
- **Left side reduces reflux; right side worsens it.** Anatomy, not preference.
- Raise the head of the bed six to eight inches. Extra pillows do not work.
- Three hours from last bite to bed — the same rule already required for fluid and temperature.
- Mint relaxes the sphincter. It is a trigger, not a remedy.
- Sleep deprivation increases acid sensitivity, so the loop runs both ways.
- Where reflux history exists but night symptoms have never been assessed, that gap is worth closing.
