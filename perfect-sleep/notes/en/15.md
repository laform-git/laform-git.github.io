# PERFECT SLEEP — 15 GERD and Sleep

*Long-standing GERD is a real sleep factor that is rarely examined as one.*

## Silent reflux is the point of this note

The assumption is that reflux disturbs sleep by causing a waking with heartburn. An often-missed pattern is the opposite: **acid reaches the oesophagus, produces a brief arousal, and sleep resumes without conscious waking.** 🟢 GERD can disturb sleep through night-time heartburn or through short arousals the sleeper does not remember (Schey 2007).

The result is the same signature as apnea ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 14 Sleep-Disordered Breathing]]) — repeated arousals preventing descent into N3 and truncating REM, producing unrefreshing sleep with no remembered cause. Someone can have significant nocturnal reflux and report no night-time symptoms at all.

**A person with no heartburn complaints can still be losing sleep architecture to reflux.**

## Why night reflux is worse than day reflux

Three defences are lost lying down and asleep:

1. **Gravity.** Upright, acid drains back. Supine, it does not.
2. **Swallowing.** Swallowing clears the oesophagus and delivers saliva. **Swallowing nearly stops during sleep**, so acid sits in contact with the mucosa far longer per episode.
3. **Saliva.** Bicarbonate-rich saliva neutralises acid, and salivary production falls markedly during sleep.

So each night-time reflux event lasts longer and does more damage than a daytime one — which is why nocturnal reflux carries a disproportionate share of the complications, including oesophagitis (Orr 2010). 🟢

## The loop with sleep

It runs both ways, like most things in this series.

- Reflux fragments sleep.
- **Sleep deprivation increases oesophageal sensitivity to acid** — the same exposure produces more symptoms when underslept. ✅ After one night of 3 hours or less, GERD patients felt infused acid sooner and more intensely; healthy controls did not change (Schey 2007).

## Position — an asymmetry worth knowing

This is the most actionable and least known item in the note.

**Left side reduces reflux. Right side increases it.** 🟢

The reason is anatomical. The stomach sits to the left, and the junction with the oesophagus enters from the right. Lying on the **left**, the junction sits above the pool of gastric contents — acid has to travel uphill. Lying on the **right**, the junction sits below the pool, and the lower oesophageal sphincter also relaxes more frequently in that position. Studies consistently show more acid exposure and slower acid clearance on the right side (Khoury 1999), with about twice as many sphincter relaxations (van Herwaarden 2000). In a randomised sham-controlled trial, a wearable that vibrated when patients rolled onto their right side doubled the rate of symptom response (44% vs 24%; Schuitenmaker 2022). Sleep side is not among the ACG 2022 recommendations.

**Supine** is also poor, because there is no gravity gradient at all; in one study it had the most reflux episodes, although the right side had the longest acid exposure (Khoury 1999).

Side sleeping (either side) was the most efficient position for glymphatic clearance in anaesthetised rodents (Lee 2015; 🔴 not shown in humans) ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 03 Glymphatic Clearance]]), and side sleeping is generally better for airway patency ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 14 Sleep-Disordered Breathing]]). Choosing the left side keeps all three benefits.

## Head-of-bed elevation

Raising the head of the bed, usually with blocks of about six to eight inches (15–20 cm), reduces nocturnal acid exposure (Kaltenbach 2006). The ACG 2022 guideline suggests it for night-time symptoms (conditional recommendation, low-quality evidence; Katz 2022). 🟢 The mechanism is gravity acting across the whole night.

**Extra pillows are not a substitute** 🔴 — the usual reasoning is that they bend the body at the waist, raising abdominal pressure while leaving the stomach–oesophagus relationship unchanged. The whole bed must be tilted: blocks or risers under the head-end legs, or a wedge running the length of the torso.

## Timing and food

- **Two to three hours from last bite to lying down** is what the ACG 2022 guideline suggests (conditional recommendation, low-quality evidence; Katz 2022). In a case-control study, a dinner-to-bed time under 3 hours carried about seven times the odds of GERD (Fujiwara 2005). 🟢 Three hours is also the interval already required for fluid ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 05 Night Fluid]]) and core temperature ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 04 The Clock]]). One rule serving three purposes.
- **High-fat meals** delay gastric emptying, leaving the stomach full longer.
- Common triggers: alcohol, chocolate, mint, caffeine, citrus, tomato, and spice — all relax the lower oesophageal sphincter or irritate directly. **Mint is worth naming** because it is often assumed to be soothing and does the opposite. 🟡 Several of these lower sphincter pressure in physiological studies, but there is no trial evidence that cutting them improves GERD across the board (Kaltenbach 2006); the ACG suggests avoiding the foods that trigger your own symptoms (Katz 2022).
- Large meal volume is itself a trigger regardless of content.

## The interaction with apnea

Worth knowing even where apnea has been excluded. The two conditions aggravate each other: the negative chest pressure generated during an obstructive event actively **pulls** gastric contents upward. 🟡 In someone with both, treating the apnea helps the reflux: in a cohort of 331 apnea patients, CPAP users cut night-time reflux symptoms by about half (Green 2003). 🟢 Evidence that treating reflux improves apnea is weaker.

## Medication note

Acid suppression is a physician decision, not a self-management one. Two things worth knowing:

- Long-term proton pump inhibitor use can cause low blood magnesium — the FDA has warned about this since 2011, usually after more than a year of use (FDA 2011) ✅ — and two or more years of use is associated with vitamin B12 deficiency (Lam 2013) 🟢. Relevant for anyone also taking a magnesium stack like the one in [[PERFECT SLEEP — 08 Substances and Stack]].
- Positional and timing measures are first-line and have no such trade-offs. They are worth exhausting before escalating.

## WHAT TO HOLD ONTO

- Silent nocturnal reflux fragments sleep without a conscious waking. No heartburn does not mean no reflux.
- Swallowing and saliva both nearly stop during sleep, so each night-time episode lasts far longer than a daytime one.
- **Left side reduces reflux; right side worsens it.** Anatomy, not preference.
- Raise the head of the bed six to eight inches. Extra pillows are not a substitute.
- Two to three hours from last bite to bed (ACG); three hours also suits the fluid and temperature rules.
- Mint relaxes the sphincter. It is a trigger, not a remedy.
- Sleep deprivation increases acid sensitivity, so the loop runs both ways.
- Where reflux history exists but night symptoms have never been assessed, that gap is worth closing.
