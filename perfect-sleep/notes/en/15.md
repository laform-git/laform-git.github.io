# PERFECT SLEEP — 15 GERD and Sleep

*Long-standing GERD is a real sleep factor that is rarely examined as one.*

**In plain words:** reflux means stomach acid coming up into the food pipe. At night it can wake the brain briefly without you knowing, so you can lose deep sleep to reflux with no heartburn at all. Sleeping on your left side, raising the head of the bed (not just adding pillows), finishing food two to three hours before bed and avoiding your own trigger foods all help. Poor sleep also makes the food pipe more sensitive to acid, so the two feed each other.

**Words in this note:**
- **GERD**: gastroesophageal reflux disease, chronic heartburn
- **REM**: rapid eye movement sleep, the dreaming stage
- **ACG**: American College of Gastroenterology
- **CPAP**: a mask machine that keeps the airway open with air pressure during sleep
- **FDA**: U.S. Food and Drug Administration

## Silent reflux is the point of this note

The usual assumption is that reflux disturbs sleep by waking you with heartburn. An often-missed pattern is the opposite: **acid reaches the oesophagus (the food pipe), causes a brief mini-waking, and sleep carries on without you ever fully waking.** 🟢 GERD (long-term reflux disease) can disturb sleep through night-time heartburn or through short mini-wakings the sleeper does not remember (Schey 2007).

The result looks the same as apnea ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 14 Sleep-Disordered Breathing]]): repeated mini-wakings stop the drop into deep N3 sleep and cut REM (dream sleep) short, giving sleep that does not refresh, with no waking remembered. Someone can have a lot of reflux at night and report no night-time symptoms at all.

**A person with no heartburn can still be losing sleep structure to reflux.**

## Why night reflux is worse than day reflux

Three defences are lost when you are lying down and asleep:

1. **Gravity.** Upright, acid drains back down. Lying flat, it does not.
2. **Swallowing.** Swallowing clears the food pipe and brings saliva down. **Swallowing almost stops during sleep**, so the acid stays in contact with the lining much longer each time.
3. **Saliva.** Saliva is rich in bicarbonate (a natural antacid) and neutralises acid, and the mouth makes much less saliva during sleep.

So each reflux event at night lasts longer and does more damage than one in the day. That is why reflux at night causes an outsized share of the complications, including oesophagitis (inflammation of the food pipe) (Orr 2010). 🟢

## The loop with sleep

It runs both ways, like most things in this series.

- Reflux breaks up sleep.
- **Lack of sleep makes the food pipe more sensitive to acid**: the same amount of acid causes more symptoms when you are short of sleep. ✅ After one night of 3 hours or less, GERD patients felt acid dripped into the food pipe sooner and more strongly; healthy people did not change (Schey 2007).

## Position — an asymmetry worth knowing

This is the most useful and least known point in the note.

**The left side reduces reflux. The right side increases it.** 🟢

The reason is anatomy. The stomach sits on the left, and the food pipe joins it from the right. Lying on the **left**, the join sits above the pool of stomach contents, so acid has to travel uphill. Lying on the **right**, the join sits below the pool, and the valve at the bottom of the food pipe (the lower oesophageal sphincter) also relaxes more often in that position. Studies consistently show more acid exposure and slower acid clearing on the right side (Khoury 1999), with about twice as many valve relaxations (van Herwaarden 2000). In a randomised trial with a fake-device comparison group, a wearable device that vibrated when patients rolled onto their right side doubled the share whose symptoms improved (44% vs 24%; Schuitenmaker 2022). Sleep side is not among the ACG 2022 recommendations.

**Lying on the back** is also poor, because there is no slope for gravity to work with at all; in one study it had the most reflux episodes, although the right side had the longest acid exposure (Khoury 1999).

Side sleeping (either side) was the best position for the brain's nightly cleaning in rodents under anaesthesia (Lee 2015; 🔴 not shown in humans) ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 03 Glymphatic Clearance]]), and side sleeping is generally better for keeping the airway open ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 14 Sleep-Disordered Breathing]]). Choosing the left side keeps all three benefits.

## Head-of-bed elevation

Raising the head of the bed, usually with blocks of about six to eight inches (15–20 cm), reduces acid exposure at night (Kaltenbach 2006). The ACG 2022 guideline (American College of Gastroenterology) suggests it for night-time symptoms (a conditional recommendation, based on low-quality evidence; Katz 2022). 🟢 It works through gravity, all night long.

**Extra pillows are not a substitute** 🔴. The usual reasoning is that they bend the body at the waist, raising the pressure in the belly while leaving the stomach and food pipe in the same position. The whole bed must be tilted: blocks or risers under the legs at the head end, or a wedge that runs the length of the upper body.

## Timing and food

- **Two to three hours from the last bite to lying down** is what the ACG 2022 guideline suggests (a conditional recommendation, based on low-quality evidence; Katz 2022). In a study comparing patients with healthy people, a gap under 3 hours between dinner and bed went with about seven times the odds of GERD (Fujiwara 2005). 🟢 Three hours is also the gap already needed for fluid ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 05 Night Fluid]]) and for body temperature ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 04 The Clock]]). One rule serving three purposes.
- **Fatty meals** slow the emptying of the stomach, leaving it full for longer.
- Common triggers: alcohol, chocolate, mint, caffeine, citrus, tomato and spice. All of them relax the valve at the bottom of the food pipe or irritate it directly. **Mint is worth naming** because people often think it soothes, and it does the opposite. 🟡 Several of these lower the valve's pressure in lab studies, but there is no trial evidence that cutting them improves GERD for everyone (Kaltenbach 2006); the ACG suggests avoiding the foods that trigger your own symptoms (Katz 2022).
- A large meal is itself a trigger, whatever is in it.

## The interaction with apnea

Worth knowing even where apnea has been ruled out. The two conditions make each other worse: the suction created in the chest during a blocked-airway event actively **pulls** stomach contents upward. 🟡 In someone with both, treating the apnea helps the reflux: in a group of 331 apnea patients, those using CPAP (a machine that keeps the airway open) cut night-time reflux symptoms by about half (Green 2003). 🟢 The evidence that treating reflux improves apnea is weaker.

## Medication note

Reducing stomach acid with medicine is a decision for the doctor, not something to manage alone. Two things worth knowing:

- Long-term use of proton pump inhibitors (strong acid-reducing medicines like omeprazole) can cause low blood magnesium. The FDA has warned about this since 2011, usually after more than a year of use (FDA 2011) ✅. Two or more years of use is linked to vitamin B12 deficiency (Lam 2013) 🟢. This matters for anyone also taking a magnesium mix like the one in [[PERFECT SLEEP — 08 Substances and Stack]].
- Position and timing are the first steps and have no such trade-offs. They are worth trying fully before stepping up.

## WHAT TO HOLD ONTO

- Silent reflux at night breaks up sleep without a full waking. No heartburn does not mean no reflux.
- Swallowing and saliva both nearly stop during sleep, so each night-time episode lasts far longer than one in the day.
- **The left side reduces reflux; the right side makes it worse.** Anatomy, not preference.
- Raise the head of the bed six to eight inches. Extra pillows are not a substitute.
- Two to three hours from last bite to bed (ACG); three hours also suits the fluid and temperature rules.
- Mint relaxes the valve. It is a trigger, not a remedy.
- Lack of sleep makes the food pipe more sensitive to acid, so the loop runs both ways.
- Where there is a history of reflux but night symptoms have never been checked, that gap is worth closing.

**The point to remember: sleep on your left side with the head of the bed raised, and leave a few hours between your last bite and bed.**
