# PERFECT SLEEP — 07 Food

*Dinner composition and timing, caffeine, alcohol — judged only by effect on the night.*

**In plain words:** what you eat and drink in the evening reaches into the night. Salt and a big protein portion at dinner make more urine overnight, a large or rich meal raises body heat and reflux, and a very sweet dinner may cause a 3am waking. Finish dinner about three hours before bed, stop coffee early in the day, avoid alcohol at night, and drink the same water earlier rather than less of it.

**Words in this note:**
- **ACG**: American College of Gastroenterology
- **GERD**: gastroesophageal reflux disease, chronic heartburn
- **REM**: rapid eye movement sleep, the dreaming stage

## The 3am waking that is not indigestion

🔴 A theory, not an established mechanism. A sugary dinner, or one full of refined carbohydrate (white flour, white rice, sweets), makes blood sugar (glucose) rise, then insulin rise, and in some people a **dip** in blood sugar a few hours later. The body corrects a real dip by releasing adrenaline and cortisol (stress hormones).

That adrenaline release is a wake-up signal. ✅ When blood sugar is pushed low during sleep in experiments, most healthy people wake, and the waking follows the rise in adrenaline (Schultes 2007). If a dip caused by dinner happens, it could produce the classic **3am waking: alert, sometimes with a racing heart, and unable to get back to sleep.** It feels like anxiety because in the body it is the same chemistry.

People blame this on stress or on something they ate not agreeing with them. 🟡 Whether an ordinary sweet dinner drops blood sugar far enough to wake someone without diabetes has not been shown. The wider evidence is mixed. In a large group of women past menopause, diets with a high glycemic index (foods that raise blood sugar quickly) were linked to more insomnia (Gangwisch 2020). Yet in a small lab study, a high-glycemic rice dinner four hours before bed shortened the time to fall asleep (Afaghi 2007). If the mechanism is real, it matters more in someone who already has insulin resistance (the body responding poorly to insulin) ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 12 Metabolic Status and Sleep]]), because the blood sugar swings are bigger.

## What loads the night, in rough order of impact

**1. Salt.** 🟢 The best-documented food cause of overnight urine: cutting salt reduced nocturia (waking at night to urinate) in a study without a comparison group (Matsuo 2019). The order of this list is a judgement, not a measured ranking. Every gram of sodium must leave the body and takes water with it; eaten at dinner, that loss happens overnight. There is also a slower effect where salt is stored in the skin and released later, so the weekly pattern matters, not just one meal.

**2. A large protein portion.** Protein produces urea (a waste product), and urea has to be passed out with water. For someone with a high daily protein goal, the lever is **spreading it out, not cutting it**. Move the bigger portion to lunch.

**3. How much you eat.** A large meal keeps digestion busy and raises body temperature at the hour it should be falling ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 04 The Clock]]). The heat the body makes while digesting is real and works against the falling curve.

**4. Fatty and heavily spiced food.** It slows the emptying of the stomach and raises the risk of reflux (stomach acid coming up into the food pipe); the ACG guideline suggests avoiding the particular foods that trigger it for you (Katz 2022). In someone with GERD (long-term reflux) ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 15 GERD and Sleep]]), this breaks up sleep even on nights when it does not cause a full waking.

**5. Sugar and refined carbohydrate.** The stress-hormone effect described above.

## Timing

Roughly three hours from the end of dinner to bed. 🟢 The ACG reflux guideline advises avoiding meals two to three hours before lying down (Katz 2022). In a study comparing patients with healthy people, a gap under three hours between dinner and bed was linked to about seven times higher odds of reflux disease (Fujiwara 2005). This one rule serves three purposes at once: the stomach empties (less reflux), fluid has time to clear (less night urine), and the heat of digestion finishes before body temperature needs to fall.

One more point that is often missed: **eating late shifts the body's other clocks.** The liver and gut have their own daily clocks, which are set mostly by mealtimes rather than by light. Eating late pulls them out of step with the master clock in the brain. 🟢 In humans, moving meals five hours later delayed the daily blood-sugar rhythm by about 5.7 hours and a clock gene in fat tissue by about 1 hour, while melatonin, the sign of the master clock, did not move (Wehrens 2017). The effect of this on sleep is less well established than the effects of light, but the mechanism is solid, and it is one more reason for a regular, earlier dinner.

## What actually helps

- A **moderate** meal, finished three hours before bed.
- Salt and the bigger protein portion moved to earlier in the day.
- Not going to bed hungry. 🟡 An empty stomach may break up sleep in its own right, and low-calorie dieting has been shown to raise cortisol (Tomiyama 2010).
- Carbohydrate is not the enemy at dinner; *refined* carbohydrate that causes a sharp swing is. A moderate portion of slower carbohydrate (like beans or whole grains) with protein and fat softens the curve.

## Caffeine — the honest version

**The advice to delay morning coffee.** The common advice, made popular by podcasts rather than trials, is to wait 90 minutes to two hours after waking. 🔴 No trial has tested whether delaying the first coffee improves anything. The two reasons given are not equally believable:

- **The cortisol argument**, that caffeine during the natural morning rise of cortisol weakens it and causes a crash, is popular but thin. It is mostly guessed rather than shown. Caffeine does raise cortisol, but in daily coffee drinkers that morning effect mostly disappears (Lovallo 2005).
- **The adenosine argument is better grounded, but still untested as advice.** ✅ Adenosine, a chemical that makes us sleepy, builds up while awake and clears during sleep; on waking a little is left over. Caffeine does not remove it; it blocks the switch it acts on. Drinking coffee straight away hides the leftover grogginess instead of letting it clear, and it comes back as the caffeine wears off. 🔴 That a delay prevents an afternoon crash is a guess.

**The evening rule matters far more.** ✅ Caffeine's half-life (the time for half of it to leave the body) averages about five hours but ranges from roughly 1.5 to 9.5 hours between people (IOM 2001). At five hours, a 2pm coffee still has roughly a quarter of its dose working at midnight, making sleep lighter even if falling asleep is easy. 🟢 400 mg taken six hours before bed still disturbed sleep noticeably (Drake 2013), and a study pooling 24 studies advises having a standard coffee at least 8.8 hours before bed (Gardiner 2023).

**A practical exception:** if waiting 90 minutes means missing the morning light, drink the coffee outside and stop fine-tuning. The light is worth more than the delay.

## Alcohol — the most sleep-destructive item on the list

Spelled out in full, because understanding why it is out is worth as much as knowing that it is.

**On the hormone:** ✅ it directly blocks the release of vasopressin (the hormone that holds back urine at night). Not indirectly, not through the clock: it blocks the signal. The valve opens, the kidney stops concentrating urine, and watery urine follows. The block is strongest while blood alcohol is rising and fades after (Eisenhofer 1982). In someone who already has nocturia, this adds straight on top of the existing problem.

**On sleep structure:** ✅ alcohol is a sedative, so you fall asleep faster and the first half of the night is more solid, with more deep sleep. Then, as the body breaks it down (roughly one drink per hour), sleep is disturbed in the second half of the night (Ebrahim 2013). The first REM period (dream sleep) comes later and total REM falls at moderate and high doses, sleep breaks up, the heart rate stays up, and waking at 3 or 4am feeling wired is typical. 🟢 In over 4,000 people monitored at home, even small amounts cut the body's overnight recovery, measured by heart-rate variability, by about 9%, and large amounts by about 39% (Pietilä 2018). **Total time asleep can look fine while the quality is ruined.**

**On temperature:** it widens the skin vessels, so the skin feels warm while the normal overnight drop in inner temperature is disturbed.

**🟢 No amount of alcohol improves overall sleep quality.** 🔴 Three hours before bed is a rule of thumb, not a tested cutoff, if you drink at all; closer to none in the evening is better.

## Hydration is not simply "drink less"

🟡 Short sleep and not drinking enough go together: in US and Chinese adults, sleeping 6 hours was linked to higher odds of not drinking enough than sleeping 8 (Rosinger 2019). The direction may run from short sleep to dehydration, through the vasopressin rhythm, rather than the other way round. The rule is **the same total water, earlier**: drink freely until the late afternoon, then drink less over the last three hours. Cutting total water to avoid nocturia swaps one problem for another.

## WHAT TO HOLD ONTO

- The alert 3am waking after a sweet dinner is the body's correcting adrenaline, not indigestion.
- Salt is the single biggest food cause of overnight urine. Protein, through urea, is second.
- Move salt and the big protein portion to lunch. Do not cut total protein.
- Three hours from last bite to bed helps reflux, fluid and temperature all at once.
- Caffeine's evening tail matters far more than the morning delay habit.
- Alcohol: no amount improves sleep. None is the target.
- Do not fix nocturia by drinking less water. Fix it by drinking the same water earlier.

**The point to remember: a moderate, early dinner with less salt, no alcohol and no late coffee gives the night its best chance.**
