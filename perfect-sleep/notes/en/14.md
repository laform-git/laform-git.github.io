# PERFECT SLEEP — 14 Sleep-Disordered Breathing

*Included for two reasons even where the sleeper does not have it: it can override much of [[PERFECT SLEEP — 05 Night Fluid]] when present, and a bed partner may have it.*

## Why this note matters even where apnea is ruled out

Two separate reasons, and the second is the one that usually gets missed.

1. **Apnea can override the vasopressin mechanism.** If it were present, much of [[PERFECT SLEEP — 05 Night Fluid]] would stop working, and the fluid work could fail for a reason that has nothing to do with fluid.
2. **A bed partner's apnea fragments the other person's sleep.** This is a real and measurable external arousal source, and it is not addressed by anything else in this series.

## What obstructive apnea does

The upper airway collapses during sleep. Breathing stops or becomes severely reduced, oxygen falls, carbon dioxide rises, and the brain produces a brief **arousal** to reopen the airway. Breathing resumes and sleep continues.

The arousal usually does not reach conscious waking. That is the defining feature: **someone can have hundreds of arousals a night and report sleeping through.** What they report instead is unrefreshing sleep and daytime fatigue with no obvious cause.

The cost to architecture is severe — repeated arousals prevent the descent into N3 and truncate REM, so the night delivers very little of either ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 01 Sleep Architecture]]).

## The nocturia connection — the part almost nobody knows

This is the most important thing in the note.

During an obstructive event, the person strains to breathe against a closed airway. That generates large negative pressure in the chest, which **stretches the atria of the heart**. Stretched atria release **atrial natriuretic peptide (ANP)**, a hormone whose job is to make the kidney excrete sodium and water. 🟢 ANP is raised during apneic sleep and falls when CPAP prevents the apneas (Krieger 1989). Findings for the related hormone BNP are inconsistent: in one CPAP study, night-time urine fell while BNP did not change (Miyazato 2017).

So apnea drives overnight urine production **directly, by a hormonal route that works independently of vasopressin** — in older adults, night-time urine and ANP rose with apnea severity (AHI above 15) while vasopressin did not track it (Umlauf 2004). Fluid timing, salt reduction and leg elevation cannot be expected to fix it; treating the apnea can.

The clinical consequence is striking: **nocturia is a useful red flag for undiagnosed obstructive sleep apnea.** In 1,007 sleep-clinic patients it was about as sensitive as snoring (85% vs 83%) but much less specific (22% vs 43%), so it flags apnea without proving it (Romero 2010). A meta-analysis found apnea carries about 40% higher risk of nocturia, significant in men (Zhou 2020). 🟢 Treating apnea with CPAP reduces nocturia in pooled before-and-after studies (Wang 2015), though controlled trials are few. 🟢

This is why apnea must be excluded before concluding that a nocturia problem is prostatic or hormonal. **It is the first fork in the road, not a footnote.**

## Risk factors and why they matter here

- Male sex, increasing age
- Central and neck adiposity — links to [[PERFECT SLEEP — 12 Metabolic Status and Sleep]]
- **Testosterone therapy can worsen or unmask apnea**, particularly at higher doses ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 11 Hormones and Sleep]]). 🟡 In a randomised trial in obese men with severe apnea, testosterone mildly worsened night-time oxygen levels at 7 weeks, but the effect had faded by 18 weeks (Hoyos 2012). Relevant for anyone on testosterone gel or injections.
- Alcohol relaxes the airway and raises the AHI, more so in people who already snore or have apnea (Kolla 2018). ✅ Sedatives such as benzodiazepines may also worsen it; the evidence for sedating antihistamines is weak ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 09 Sleep Medications and Aids]]). 🟡
- Supine sleeping position worsens it — gravity closes the airway more readily on the back.
- Nasal obstruction and mouth breathing.

## Signs worth watching for, even after apnea has been excluded

Stated because risk factors change, and testosterone therapy is one of them.

- Loud snoring, or witnessed pauses in breathing
- Waking gasping or choking
- Morning headache, dry mouth on waking
- Daytime sleepiness disproportionate to hours slept
- Nocturia that **does not improve** as the prostate issue resolves

That last one is the specific tripwire for someone being treated for BPH. If wakings plateau or fail to follow the expected improvement after a prostate procedure, apnea is the first thing to re-examine — not the last.

## Central sleep apnea

A different condition: the airway is open but the brain intermittently fails to signal a breath. Associated with heart failure, opioid use and high altitude. Rarer, and treated partly differently: the underlying cause comes first, and the choice of breathing device is not the same as for obstructive apnea. Mentioned only for completeness.

## Upper airway resistance syndrome

Worth knowing as the gap in the diagnostic net. Airflow is reduced enough to cause repeated arousals, but not enough to register as apneas or hypopneas on a standard scoring threshold. A sleep study can come back "normal" while the person is being fragmented all night. If unexplained fragmentation persists with a normal study, this is the thing to ask about.

**The numbers, per AASM guidance.** ✅ The diagnostic definition the AASM uses (ICSD-3) is an obstructive respiratory disturbance index (RDI) of 5 or more events per hour of sleep with typical symptoms, or 15 or more even without symptoms. The RDI counts these respiratory effort-related arousals (RERAs); the AHI counts only apneas and hypopneas (Kapur 2017). Severity by AHI: mild 5 to under 15, moderate 15 to under 30, severe 30 or more (AASM 1999). Home sleep tests cannot see arousals and tend to underestimate the true AHI, so the AASM recommends in-lab polysomnography when a home test is negative, inconclusive or technically inadequate (Kapur 2017). A "normal" home test does not rule this out.

## The shared bed

The dimension this series would otherwise miss entirely. **When one partner has apnea and the other does not**, the unaffected partner's sleep is disrupted through three distinct routes:

**1. Snoring and gasping as noise.** Apneic snoring is not steady — it is loud, then silent, then a sudden loud resumption. **Intermittent and unpredictable noise is far more arousing than continuous noise**, because the brain habituates to steady sound and cannot habituate to irregular sound. 🟡 This makes apneic snoring disproportionately disruptive compared with its decibel level.

**2. Movement.** Arousals at the end of each event come with body movement and position shifts. In a shared bed these transmit directly, and **mattress choice matters more than it seems** — motion isolation is a genuine intervention here. 🔴

**3. CPAP, if in use.** Modern machines are quiet, around 30 decibels, but mask leak produces irregular hissing, which falls under the same intermittent-noise problem above. Worth resolving as a mask-fit issue rather than tolerating.

**Interventions, in order of impact:**

- The affected partner's apnea being properly treated is the largest single lever — for that partner's health first, not only the other's sleep. 🟢 In a small study, when the snoring partner was put on CPAP, the bed partner's sleep efficiency rose from 74% to 87% — about an extra hour of sleep (Beninati 1999).
- **Continuous masking noise** — a fan or white noise — works by raising the floor so that intermittent sounds are less distinct against it. It is widely used, but a systematic review rated the evidence that continuous noise improves sleep as very low quality, and it may itself disturb sleep or hearing (Riedy 2021). 🟡
- Earplugs.
- Motion-isolating mattress.
- Separate beds or rooms. Worth naming plainly rather than treating as a failure — it is a legitimate option (🔴 research on couples sleeping apart is limited; this is a judgement, not a finding) when one partner has a treated or untreated sleep disorder.

## WHAT TO HOLD ONTO

- Apnea arousals usually do not reach consciousness. Someone can be fragmented hundreds of times and report sleeping through.
- Apnea drives nocturia via atrial stretch and natriuretic peptide — a route independent of vasopressin. Fluid measures cannot be expected to fix it; treating the apnea can.
- Nocturia is about as sensitive as snoring as a warning sign of undiagnosed apnea, though far less specific.
- Testosterone therapy can unmask it. If wakings fail to improve as the prostate resolves, re-examine apnea first.
- A normal sleep study does not exclude upper airway resistance syndrome. A negative home test does not exclude apnea: the AASM advises in-lab polysomnography.
- A partner's apnea fragments the other person's sleep. Intermittent noise is more arousing than steady noise — the rationale for continuous masking noise, though the evidence that white noise improves sleep is weak.
- Sleeping apart is a legitimate option, not a failure.
