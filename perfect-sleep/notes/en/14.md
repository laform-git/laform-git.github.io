# PERFECT SLEEP — 14 Sleep-Disordered Breathing

*Included for two reasons even where the sleeper does not have it: it can override much of [[PERFECT SLEEP — 05 Night Fluid]] when present, and a bed partner may have it.*

**In plain words:** in sleep apnea, the throat closes again and again during sleep, and the brain briefly wakes to open it, often hundreds of times a night without you knowing. Besides wrecking deep sleep, apnea makes the heart release a hormone that produces more urine at night, so waking to urinate can be a warning sign of apnea. A partner's snoring can also break your sleep. Apnea should be ruled out before blaming night urination on the prostate.

**Words in this note:**
- **REM**: rapid eye movement sleep, the dreaming stage
- **ANP**: atrial natriuretic peptide, a heart hormone that makes the kidneys release salt and water
- **CPAP**: a mask machine that keeps the airway open with air pressure during sleep
- **BPH**: benign prostatic hyperplasia, a non-cancerous enlarged prostate
- **AASM**: American Academy of Sleep Medicine

## Why this note matters even where apnea is ruled out

Two separate reasons, and the second is the one usually missed.

1. **Apnea can override the vasopressin mechanism.** (Vasopressin is the hormone that holds back urine at night.) If apnea were present, much of [[PERFECT SLEEP — 05 Night Fluid]] would stop working, and the work on fluid could fail for a reason that has nothing to do with fluid.
2. **A bed partner's apnea breaks up the other person's sleep.** This is a real, measurable source of waking from outside, and nothing else in this series deals with it.

## What obstructive apnea does

The upper airway (the throat) collapses during sleep. Breathing stops or becomes very shallow, oxygen falls, carbon dioxide rises, and the brain makes a brief **arousal** (a mini-waking) to reopen the airway. Breathing starts again and sleep goes on.

The mini-waking usually does not reach full awareness. That is the key feature: **someone can have hundreds of mini-wakings a night and say they slept straight through.** What they notice instead is sleep that does not refresh them and daytime tiredness with no clear cause.

The cost to the structure of sleep is severe. Repeated mini-wakings stop the drop into deep N3 sleep and cut REM (dream sleep) short, so the night gives very little of either ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 01 Sleep Architecture]]).

## The nocturia connection — the part almost nobody knows

This is the most important thing in the note.

During a blocked-airway event, the person strains to breathe against a closed throat. That creates strong suction in the chest, which **stretches the upper chambers of the heart (the atria)**. Stretched atria release **atrial natriuretic peptide (ANP)**, a hormone whose job is to make the kidney pass salt and water. 🟢 ANP is raised during sleep with apnea and falls when CPAP (a machine that keeps the airway open with air pressure) prevents the apneas (Krieger 1989). Findings for a related hormone, BNP, are inconsistent: in one CPAP study, night-time urine fell while BNP did not change (Miyazato 2017).

So apnea drives urine production overnight **directly, through a hormone route that works separately from vasopressin**. In older adults, night-time urine and ANP rose with how severe the apnea was (AHI, the number of breathing events per hour, above 15), while vasopressin did not follow it (Umlauf 2004). Timing fluids, cutting salt and raising the legs cannot be expected to fix it; treating the apnea can.

The medical consequence is striking: **nocturia (waking at night to urinate) is a useful warning sign of sleep apnea that has not been diagnosed.** In 1,007 patients at a sleep clinic, it picked up apnea about as often as snoring did (85% vs 83%) but gave many more false alarms (it correctly ruled people out only 22% of the time, vs 43% for snoring). So it points toward apnea without proving it (Romero 2010). A study pooling many studies found apnea carries about 40% higher risk of nocturia, clearly so in men (Zhou 2020). 🟢 Treating apnea with CPAP reduces nocturia in pooled before-and-after studies (Wang 2015), though controlled trials are few. 🟢

That is why apnea must be ruled out before deciding that a nocturia problem comes from the prostate or from hormones. **It is the first fork in the road, not a footnote.**

## Risk factors and why they matter here

- Being male, getting older
- Fat around the belly and neck: links to [[PERFECT SLEEP — 12 Metabolic Status and Sleep]]
- **Testosterone therapy can worsen apnea or bring it out**, especially at higher doses ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 11 Hormones and Sleep]]). 🟡 In a randomised trial in obese men with severe apnea, testosterone slightly lowered night-time oxygen levels at 7 weeks, but the effect had faded by 18 weeks (Hoyos 2012). This matters for anyone using testosterone gel or injections.
- Alcohol relaxes the airway and raises the AHI, more so in people who already snore or have apnea (Kolla 2018). ✅ Sleeping pills such as benzodiazepines may also make it worse; the evidence for sleep-inducing antihistamines is weak ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 09 Sleep Medications and Aids]]). 🟡
- Sleeping on the back makes it worse: gravity closes the airway more easily.
- A blocked nose and breathing through the mouth.

## Signs worth watching for, even after apnea has been excluded

Listed because risk factors change, and testosterone therapy is one of them.

- Loud snoring, or pauses in breathing that someone else notices
- Waking up gasping or choking
- Headache in the morning, dry mouth on waking
- Daytime sleepiness out of proportion to the hours slept
- Nocturia that **does not improve** as the prostate problem clears up

That last one is the specific alarm for someone being treated for BPH (an enlarged prostate). If the wakings stop improving, or do not improve as expected, after a prostate procedure, apnea is the first thing to look at again, not the last.

## Central sleep apnea

A different condition: the airway is open, but the brain sometimes fails to send the signal to breathe. It is linked to heart failure, opioid painkillers and high altitude. It is rarer and treated partly differently: the underlying cause comes first, and the choice of breathing machine is not the same as for obstructive apnea. Mentioned only to be complete.

## Upper airway resistance syndrome

Worth knowing as the hole in the net. Airflow is reduced enough to cause repeated mini-wakings, but not enough to count as apneas or hypopneas (partial blockages) under the standard scoring rules. A sleep study can come back "normal" while the person's sleep is being broken all night. If unexplained broken sleep continues with a normal study, this is the thing to ask about.

**The numbers, per AASM guidance.** ✅ The diagnostic definition the AASM (American Academy of Sleep Medicine) uses (ICSD-3) is an obstructive respiratory disturbance index (RDI) of 5 or more events per hour of sleep with typical symptoms, or 15 or more even without symptoms. The RDI counts these breathing-effort mini-wakings (RERAs); the AHI counts only apneas and hypopneas (Kapur 2017). Severity by AHI: mild 5 to under 15, moderate 15 to under 30, severe 30 or more (AASM 1999). Home sleep tests cannot see mini-wakings and tend to undercount the true AHI, so the AASM recommends a full overnight lab study (polysomnography) when a home test is negative, unclear or technically poor (Kapur 2017). A "normal" home test does not rule this out.

## The shared bed

The part this series would otherwise miss completely. **When one partner has apnea and the other does not**, the other partner's sleep is disturbed in three separate ways:

**1. Snoring and gasping as noise.** Snoring with apnea is not steady. It is loud, then silent, then suddenly loud again. **Noise that comes and goes unpredictably wakes the brain far more than steady noise**, because the brain gets used to steady sound and cannot get used to irregular sound. 🟡 That makes apnea snoring far more disturbing than its loudness alone would suggest.

**2. Movement.** The mini-waking at the end of each event comes with body movement and changes of position. In a shared bed these pass straight across, and **the choice of mattress matters more than it seems**: a mattress that does not pass on movement is a real help here. 🔴

**3. CPAP, if used.** Modern machines are quiet, around 30 decibels, but air leaking from the mask makes an irregular hiss, which is the same come-and-go noise problem as above. Worth fixing as a mask-fit problem rather than putting up with it.

**What helps, in order of impact:**

- Getting the affected partner's apnea properly treated is the biggest single help, for that partner's health first, not only the other's sleep. 🟢 In a small study, when the snoring partner was put on CPAP, the bed partner's sleep efficiency (the share of time in bed spent asleep) rose from 74% to 87%, about an extra hour of sleep (Beninati 1999).
- **Steady masking noise**, a fan or white noise, works by raising the background so that come-and-go sounds stand out less. It is widely used, but a systematic review rated the evidence that steady noise improves sleep as very low quality, and it may itself disturb sleep or hearing (Riedy 2021). 🟡
- Earplugs.
- A mattress that does not pass on movement.
- Separate beds or rooms. Worth saying plainly instead of treating it as a failure: it is a legitimate option (🔴 research on couples sleeping apart is limited; this is a judgement, not a finding) when one partner has a sleep disorder, treated or not.

## WHAT TO HOLD ONTO

- Mini-wakings from apnea usually do not reach awareness. Someone can have their sleep broken hundreds of times and say they slept through.
- Apnea drives nocturia through heart stretch and a hormone (natriuretic peptide), a route separate from vasopressin. Measures aimed at fluid cannot be expected to fix it; treating the apnea can.
- Nocturia is about as good as snoring at flagging undiagnosed apnea, though it gives far more false alarms.
- Testosterone therapy can bring it out. If wakings fail to improve as the prostate problem clears, look at apnea again first.
- A normal sleep study does not rule out upper airway resistance syndrome. A negative home test does not rule out apnea: the AASM advises a full lab sleep study.
- A partner's apnea breaks up the other person's sleep. Come-and-go noise wakes the brain more than steady noise, which is the reason for steady masking noise, though the evidence that white noise improves sleep is weak.
- Sleeping apart is a legitimate option, not a failure.

**The point to remember: if you snore, wake tired or keep getting up to urinate, ask for a sleep apnea test before anything else.**
