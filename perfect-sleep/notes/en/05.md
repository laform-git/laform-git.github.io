# PERFECT SLEEP — 05 Night Fluid

*How much urine the body makes overnight — the production tap. The mechanical tap (prostate, post-void residual) belongs to urology and is named here once, then left alone.*

**In plain words:** at night a hormone called vasopressin tells the kidneys to make less urine, so you can sleep without waking. Its timing is set by the body clock, so morning light may help. Other things raise night urine: fluid that pools in the legs from sitting all day, salt and heavy protein at dinner, alcohol, cold, and broken sleep itself. A three-day bladder diary shows whether the body makes too much urine at night or the bladder holds too little.

**Words in this note:**
- **THC**: the part of cannabis that makes you high

## The night valve

Vasopressin, also called antidiuretic hormone (the hormone that holds back urine). It is made in the hypothalamus, a control centre deep in the brain, and released from the back part of the pituitary gland.

In the kidney it acts on the collecting duct, the last tube the urine passes through. It puts water channels called **aquaporins** into the wall of the duct. With those channels in place, water is pulled back out of the forming urine into the body, so urine comes out small in amount and concentrated. Without them, the duct is in effect waterproof in the wrong direction: water stays in the urine, and the amount rises.

The whole question of how much urine is made overnight comes down to **how many of those channels are put in place**, and vasopressin controls that.

Normally, urine production falls sharply during sleep. That is what lets most people go seven or eight hours without waking. 🟢 A rise in vasopressin at night is one reason (George 1975); the kidneys filtering less and passing less salt at night are others (Song 2025). In many older adults the night-time rise in vasopressin is weak or missing (Asplund 1991).

## The surge is scheduled by the clock

This link is almost never spelled out. The night rise of vasopressin is not a standalone event. It follows the body clock (it is **circadian**), timed by the master clock in the brain, the suprachiasmatic nucleus ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 04 The Clock]]). The nerve cells that make vasopressin sit next to the clock in the hypothalamus and take their cue from it. 🟢 That urine production follows a daily brain–kidney–bladder rhythm is well supported (Song 2025); the specific predictions below have not been tested directly in humans.

So, in principle (🔴 interpretation, worked out from how the body clock works):

- A sharp, well-set clock produces a strong, well-timed rise.
- A drifting, weakly set clock produces a **flat and late** rise. That means the kidney keeps making urine at daytime rates into the early night.
- A strong contrast between bright days and dark nights may make the rise *bigger*, not just better timed. No human study has measured this.

**🟡 So morning light is a believable way to help nocturia (waking at night to urinate).** It does nothing to the bladder; the idea is that it sets the schedule the hormone follows fourteen hours later. In 1,585 older adults, more morning light was linked to a longer first stretch of sleep before the first bathroom trip, though not to fewer trips (Nakamoto 2026). That is a link seen in a group, not a trial.

## Leg fluid: water arriving hours late

The factor that matters most for people who sit at a desk all day, and the one nobody suspects.

Sitting for eight or ten hours lets gravity push fluid out of the blood vessels into the tissue of the calves and ankles, where it sits all day. Usually you cannot see or feel it.

Then you lie down. Gravity stops holding it there, and over the next hours it drains back into the bloodstream. The amount of fluid in the blood rises, the kidney senses the extra, and gets rid of it. 🟢 In older men, the fluid in the legs during the day and its shift overnight went along with the amount of night urine, and both rose with salt intake (Yoshikawa 2020).

**This is water drunk at lunchtime arriving at the bladder at 2:00am.** Even a good vasopressin rise cannot stop it, because the kidney is not responding to a hormone signal. It is responding to a load of fluid that arrived late.

**The fix is using the leg muscles during the day.** The calf muscles act as a pump on the deep veins. Walking, standing breaks and calf raises at the desk push that fluid back into the blood while you are still upright, so it is cleared in daylight. Raising the legs in the late afternoon, for twenty to thirty minutes with the feet above hip level, does the same without effort. 🟡 Both are standard lifestyle advice for nocturia but barely tested. In a small trial without a comparison group, daytime compression stockings cut night bathroom trips by about 0.5 a night (Kaga 2022). Which option helps more is not known.

**Timing matters more than amount.** Clearing that fluid at 4pm means passing it before bed. Clearing it at 9pm just moves the problem an hour later.

## Osmotic load: salt and protein

Two things in food that force the body to pass water whatever the hormone is doing.

- **Sodium (salt).** Every gram eaten must leave the body, and it takes water with it. Eaten at dinner, that loss happens overnight. There is also a slower effect: salt can be stored in tissues such as the skin and released later. So a salty *pattern* over a week matters, not only one meal. 🟢 In patients who ate a lot of salt, cutting salt reduced nocturia from 2.3 to 1.4 times a night (Matsuo 2019, no comparison group). 🟡 The salt-storage rhythm comes from long studies where people ate a fixed diet (Rakova 2013) and is still being worked out.
- **Urea, from protein.** A big protein meal raises the amount of urea (a waste product of protein), and urea must be passed out with water. A large, protein-heavy dinner can add to night urine. 🟡 The body science is sound; how big the effect is on nocturia has not been studied directly.

Neither means eating less. Both mean **moving salt and the bigger protein portion to earlier in the day.** With a high daily protein goal, spreading it out is the lever, not cutting it.

## What suppresses the hormone, or overrides it

- **Alcohol.** ✅ Blocks the release of vasopressin, directly. The block is strongest while blood alcohol is rising, and then vasopressin bounces back (Eisenhofer 1982). That block, plus the fluid in the drinks themselves, causes the bathroom trips and dehydration after drinking ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 07 Food]]).
- **Cold.** Cold squeezes the blood vessels in the skin and limbs, pushing blood inward toward the trunk. The body reads this as too much fluid in the middle and gets rid of it. This is called **cold-induced diuresis** (extra urine caused by cold). 🟢 This extra urine is well documented. The main cause is blood moving to the trunk; whether lower vasopressin plays a part is not established. Sitting in water raises urine output even at a neutral temperature, by 89–107% over an hour in one study, because the water pressure does the same job (Šrámek 2000). The effect grows with the dose: an ice bath is the extreme, while a cool shower that just brings the body back to normal has a much milder effect. Same direction, different size. (See [[PERFECT SLEEP — 06 Heat and Timing]].)
- **Broken sleep itself.** Lost sleep raises night urine. 🟢 In healthy young adults, a night without sleep sharply increased urine through extra salt loss and the loss of the normal drop in blood pressure at night, while vasopressin stayed the same (Kamperis 2010). That creates the loop from [[PERFECT SLEEP — 02 Nocturia and Broken Sleep]]: lost sleep makes more urine, and more urine causes another waking. Making sleep less broken by *any* route feeds back and helps night urine fall. 🔴 The loop is worked out from studies of a whole night without sleep; broken sleep is less studied.

## Things that raise it

- Being short of water, or the blood becoming more concentrated: the main everyday trigger. This matters because a sauna *raises* vasopressin during the session, and then drinking afterwards switches it back off ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 06 Heat and Timing]]).
- **Nicotine.** Worth saying because it is often wrongly grouped with caffeine as something that makes you urinate. ✅ Nicotine **increases** vasopressin release, so it holds urine back, the opposite direction (Husain 1975).

## Caffeine works by a different route

Not through vasopressin. ✅ Caffeine blocks adenosine A1 receptors (a kind of switch) in the kidney tubes, so less salt and water are taken back (Osswald 2011); 🟡 it may also mildly irritate the bladder. 🟢 Regular coffee drinkers get used to this effect, and doses below about 250–300 mg make little extra urine. So for someone who has a steady amount every day, the effect is modest (Maughan 2003).

## The open question: polyuria or capacity

These are two different problems with different fixes, and which one is happening is often guessed rather than measured.

- **Nocturnal polyuria**: too much urine made at night. ✅ The usual cutoff is night urine above about a third of the 24-hour total in adults over about 65, or above a fifth in younger adults (Hashim 2019). The ICS (International Continence Society) notes these cutoffs are not fully proven. This is a hormone and fluid problem, and everything in this note applies.
- **Capacity**: a normal amount of urine at night, but too little room. This comes from urine left behind in the bladder, or from a thickened, irritable bladder wall after years of pushing against a blockage. It is a mechanical problem, for the urologist.

A three-day bladder diary, the ICS standard, settles it ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 19 Measuring It]]). Amounts per trip that are *not small* point toward polyuria, but that is still a guess until it is measured.

## WHAT TO HOLD ONTO

- Vasopressin puts water channels into the kidney. How much urine is made at night depends on how many are put in.
- The rise is timed by the clock that light sets, which makes morning light a believable help for nocturia (🟡).
- Fluid that pools in the legs from sitting at a desk reaches the bladder hours after you lie down. Clear it with calf movement or by raising the legs in the late afternoon.
- Salt and a big protein portion at dinner force water out overnight. Move both earlier.
- Alcohol blocks the hormone directly. Cold and sitting in water raise urine by pushing blood to the trunk. Nicotine raises the hormone. Caffeine works by a different route altogether.
- Lost sleep raises night urine, which breaks sleep further. The loop runs both ways.
- Whether the body makes too much urine or the bladder holds too little is often unanswered, and it can always be measured. Measure it.

**The point to remember: clear the fluid in your legs in the afternoon, eat salt and big protein earlier, skip alcohol at night, and keep a bladder diary to know which problem you have.**
