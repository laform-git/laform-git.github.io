# PERFECT SLEEP — 01 Sleep Architecture

*Foundation note. Words like "fragmentation" and "depth" are meaningless without this. Every other note in the series refers back to it.*

## Sleep is not one state

Sleep is four distinct states doing different jobs. Losing one is not compensated by more of another: the brain does try to repay specific losses later (deep-sleep and REM rebound), but extra N2 does not stand in for missing N3 or REM. This is the single most important idea in the series, because it means **total hours asleep is a poor measure of a night.**

## The stages

**N1 — the doorway.** A few minutes. Muscle tone drops, hypnic jerks happen here. Woken from N1, people often deny they were asleep. Roughly 5% of the night. Its importance is not restorative — it is that **every return to sleep after a full waking passes through it.** (Brief arousals of a few seconds can drop straight back into N2 or N3.)

**N2 — the bulk.** Around 45–55% of total sleep. Marked by two electrical signatures: *sleep spindles*, brief bursts associated with memory consolidation and with gating sensory input away from the sleeping brain, and *K-complexes*, large single waves that appear to suppress arousal from external stimuli. N2 is not filler — people who produce more spindles sleep through more noise (Dang-Vu 2010) 🟢, so this machinery is part of what decides whether a noise — and plausibly a bladder signal 🔴 — breaks through.

**N3 — slow-wave sleep, the deep stage.** Roughly 15–25% of the night in young adults, declining steeply with age, especially in men: in pooled lab data from healthy men, deep sleep fell from about 19% at ages 16–25 to about 3% at 36–50 (Van Cauter 2000 — older, stricter scoring rules; the age trend is confirmed by meta-analysis, Ohayon 2004) ✅. Large synchronised delta waves. The largest growth hormone pulse is tied to it (Van Cauter 2000) ✅. Glymphatic clearance is thought to run here — rodent evidence, contested ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 03 Glymphatic Clearance]]) 🟡. Arousal threshold is at its highest — this is the stage where a signal has to be loud to break through. Being woken from N3 produces severe disorientation.

**REM — rapid eye movement.** 20–25%. Brain activity approaches waking levels while skeletal muscle is paralysed (atonia). Vivid narrative dreaming. Emotional memory processing and consolidation — a leading hypothesis, not settled 🟡. REM is actively switched on by a network in the pons: cholinergic neurons (PPT and LDT) promote it and activate the cortex, while glutamatergic neurons of the sublaterodorsal nucleus drive the muscle atonia through inhibitory neurons in the medulla and spinal cord (Krenzer 2011; Peever 2017) 🟢. PGO waves accompany the eye movements — well documented in cats, only indirectly in humans. So the eye movements, the cortical activation and the atonia are coordinated outputs of one brainstem switch rather than of one signal. The eye movements are best read as a readout of that process, not a participant in consolidation 🔴. Full mechanism in [[PERFECT SLEEP — 08A Sedative Mechanisms: THC, Alcohol and CBD]]. One overlooked detail: **thermoregulation is largely suspended during REM** — the body largely stops defending its temperature (Szymusiak 2018) 🟢, which is part of why a hot room — or a cold one without enough bedding — disrupts the back half of the night more than the front (Okamoto-Mizuno 2012) 🟡.

## Cycle structure — the asymmetry that matters

Cycles run roughly 90 minutes, though 70–120 is normal, four to six times a night. But the cycles are **not identical**:

- **N3 is front-loaded.** Most deep sleep occurs in the first two cycles — the first three hours or so. By the second half of the night there is very little N3 left to have.
- **REM is back-loaded.** REM periods are short early and lengthen progressively. The longest REM period of the night is usually the last one before waking.

The consequence is direct: **when sleep is lost determines what is lost.** Going to bed two hours late costs deep sleep. Waking two hours early costs REM. They are not interchangeable losses.

## The two-process model

Sleep timing is governed by two independent systems (Borbély 1982):

- **Process S — homeostatic pressure.** Sleep pressure builds every waking hour and dissipates during sleep, most visibly as deep-sleep slow-wave activity; adenosine is the best-studied chemical carrier of it (Borbély 2016) 🟢. The longer awake, the stronger the drive.
- **Process C — circadian alerting.** The clock produces a wakefulness signal that *rises* through the day, counteracting the growing sleep pressure, peaks in the evening and then drops sharply around habitual bedtime (Edgar 1993) 🟢.

Sleep happens in the gap where the alerting signal falls away and pressure remains high. This model explains things that otherwise seem contradictory: the late-evening "second wind" (a final circadian alerting push before it falls), why a nap can make bedtime harder (it discharges Process S), and why lying in on a weekend both reduces pressure and shifts the clock.

## What trackers actually measure

Consumer devices infer stages from movement and heart-rate variability. They do not measure brain activity.

- Sleep versus wake detection: overall agreement with laboratory polysomnography is often 90% or more, but that figure is carried by sleep — devices catch over 90% of sleep epochs yet correctly flag only about 20–55% of wake epochs, so they overcount sleep and miss brief wakings (Chinoy 2021; Schyvens 2025) ✅.
- Four-stage classification: substantially weaker — roughly 60–80% epoch-by-epoch agreement (the high end comes from a manufacturer-run study) and only fair-to-moderate agreement beyond chance, with wide variation between devices and between nights (Altini 2021; Schyvens 2025) 🟢.

Practical reading: **trust the trends, not the single night.** A tracker reporting 42 minutes of deep sleep on one particular Tuesday is not a measurement. A tracker showing deep sleep declining across three weeks may be.

## WHAT TO HOLD ONTO

- Four stages, different jobs, not interchangeable.
- N3 lives in the first half of the night; REM lives in the second.
- Every return to sleep after a full waking restarts at N1 — sleep does not resume where it left off.
- Arousal threshold is highest in N3 and lowest in N1; N2 and tonic REM sit in between, and phasic REM can be as hard to wake from as deep sleep (Ermis 2010). That decides whether a signal breaks through at all.
- Thermoregulation is suspended in REM, so room temperature hits the back half of the night hardest.
- Total hours is the weakest measure of a night. Structure is the real one.
