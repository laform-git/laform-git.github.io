# PERFECT SLEEP — 01 Sleep Architecture

*Foundation note. Words like "fragmentation" and "depth" are meaningless without this. Every other note in the series refers back to it.*

**In plain words:** sleep is not one thing. It has four stages, each doing a different job, and one stage cannot make up for another. Deep sleep comes mostly early in the night and dream sleep mostly late, so when you lose sleep decides what you lose. Sleep trackers are good at showing trends over weeks, but not at telling you about a single night.

**Words in this note:**
- **REM**: rapid eye movement sleep, the dreaming stage
- **THC**: the part of cannabis that makes you high

## Sleep is not one state

Sleep is four different states, each doing a different job. Losing one is not made up for by getting more of another. The brain does try to pay back certain losses later (a "rebound" of deep sleep or REM), but extra N2 does not stand in for missing N3 or REM. This is the single most important idea in the series, because it means **the total number of hours asleep is a poor measure of a night.**

## The stages

**N1 — the doorway.** A few minutes. The muscles relax, and the sudden jerks as you drift off (hypnic jerks) happen here. People woken from N1 often say they were not asleep. It is roughly 5% of the night. N1 does not restore much. What matters is that **every return to sleep after a full waking goes through it.** (Brief wakings of a few seconds can drop straight back into N2 or N3.)

**N2 — the bulk.** Around 45–55% of total sleep. It has two electrical signs on a brain recording. *Sleep spindles* are short bursts linked to storing memories and to keeping sounds and other signals away from the sleeping brain. *K-complexes* are large single waves that seem to stop outside noises from waking you. N2 is not filler. People who make more spindles sleep through more noise (Dang-Vu 2010) 🟢. So this system is part of what decides whether a noise, and plausibly a signal from the bladder 🔴, breaks through.

**N3 — slow-wave sleep, the deep stage.** Roughly 15–25% of the night in young adults. It drops steeply with age, especially in men. In combined lab data from healthy men, deep sleep fell from about 19% at ages 16–25 to about 3% at 36–50 (Van Cauter 2000, using older, stricter scoring rules; the age trend is confirmed by a study pooling many studies, Ohayon 2004) ✅. N3 shows large, slow brain waves (delta waves) moving together. The biggest release of growth hormone of the night is tied to it (Van Cauter 2000) ✅. The brain's nightly cleaning (glymphatic clearance) is thought to happen here, but the evidence is from rodents and is debated ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 03 Glymphatic Clearance]]) 🟡. This is the hardest stage to wake from: a signal has to be loud to break through. People woken from N3 are badly confused.

**REM — rapid eye movement.** 20–25%. Brain activity is close to waking levels, while the body's muscles are paralysed (atonia). This is when vivid, story-like dreams happen. A leading idea, not settled, is that REM processes and stores emotional memories 🟡. REM is actively switched on by a network in the pons (part of the brainstem). One group of nerve cells (the cholinergic PPT and LDT cells) turns REM on and wakes up the outer brain. Another group (the glutamatergic cells of the sublaterodorsal nucleus) causes the muscle paralysis, working through blocking nerve cells lower in the brainstem and spinal cord (Krenzer 2011; Peever 2017) 🟢. PGO waves (bursts of activity that come with the eye movements) are well documented in cats and only indirectly in humans. So the eye movements, the active brain and the paralysis are coordinated outputs of one brainstem switch, not of one signal. The eye movements are best seen as a sign of that process, not as part of memory storage 🔴. The full mechanism is in [[PERFECT SLEEP — 08A Sedative Mechanisms: THC, Alcohol and CBD]]. One overlooked detail: **the body mostly stops controlling its temperature during REM.** It largely stops defending its temperature (Szymusiak 2018) 🟢. That is part of why a hot room, or a cold one without enough bedding, disturbs the second half of the night more than the first (Okamoto-Mizuno 2012) 🟡.

## Cycle structure — the asymmetry that matters

Sleep cycles last roughly 90 minutes, though 70–120 is normal, and there are four to six a night. But the cycles are **not the same**:

- **Deep sleep (N3) comes early.** Most deep sleep happens in the first two cycles, about the first three hours. By the second half of the night there is very little N3 left.
- **REM comes late.** REM periods are short early on and get longer as the night goes on. The longest REM period is usually the last one before waking.

The result is simple: **when you lose sleep decides what you lose.** Going to bed two hours late costs deep sleep. Waking two hours early costs REM. They are not the same loss.

## The two-process model

When we sleep is controlled by two separate systems (Borbély 1982):

- **Process S — sleep pressure.** Sleep pressure builds with every hour awake and drains away during sleep, most clearly in deep sleep. Adenosine (a chemical that builds up in the brain while awake) is the best-studied carrier of it (Borbély 2016) 🟢. The longer you are awake, the stronger the drive to sleep.
- **Process C — the body clock's wake signal.** The body clock (circadian clock) sends out a wake-up signal that *rises* through the day, pushing back against the growing sleep pressure. It peaks in the evening and then drops sharply around your usual bedtime (Edgar 1993) 🟢.

Sleep happens in the gap when the wake signal falls away and pressure is still high. This model explains things that otherwise seem contradictory: the late-evening "second wind" (a last push of the wake signal before it falls), why a nap can make bedtime harder (it drains Process S), and why sleeping in at the weekend both lowers pressure and shifts the clock.

## What trackers actually measure

Consumer devices guess the sleep stages from movement and from changes in the heartbeat (heart-rate variability). They do not measure brain activity.

- Asleep versus awake: overall agreement with a full lab sleep study (polysomnography) is often 90% or more. But that figure comes mostly from the sleep time. Devices catch over 90% of the time asleep, yet correctly spot only about 20–55% of the time awake. So they count too much sleep and miss brief wakings (Chinoy 2021; Schyvens 2025) ✅.
- Sorting into four stages: much weaker. Roughly 60–80% agreement minute by minute (the high end comes from a study run by a manufacturer), and only fair-to-moderate agreement beyond chance, with wide differences between devices and between nights (Altini 2021; Schyvens 2025) 🟢.

In practice: **trust the trends, not the single night.** A tracker saying you had 42 minutes of deep sleep on one particular Tuesday is not a measurement. A tracker showing deep sleep going down over three weeks may be.

## WHAT TO HOLD ONTO

- Four stages, different jobs, not interchangeable.
- N3 lives in the first half of the night; REM lives in the second.
- Every return to sleep after a full waking starts again at N1. Sleep does not pick up where it left off.
- It is hardest to wake from N3 and easiest from N1. N2 and calm (tonic) REM are in between, and REM with active eye movements (phasic REM) can be as hard to wake from as deep sleep (Ermis 2010). That decides whether a signal breaks through at all.
- Temperature control is switched off in REM, so room temperature hits the second half of the night hardest.
- The total number of hours is the weakest measure of a night. The structure of the night is the real one.

**The point to remember: a good night is about the right stages at the right time, not just the number of hours.**
