# PERFECT SLEEP — 06 Heat and Timing

*Sauna, cold exposure and the evening sequence — judged only by their effect on sleep.*

## The counterintuitive core: the benefit of heat is the cooling afterwards

A sauna or hot bath raises core temperature briefly. That is not the sleep benefit — it is the cost.

The benefit is the **cooling that follows**. The body responds to the heat load by opening skin blood vessels wide — especially in the hands and feet — to dump it, which produces a faster, steeper fall in core temperature afterwards. 🟡 Whether core temperature actually drops *below* its usual curve is less consistent across studies. From [[PERFECT SLEEP — 04 The Clock]], sleep onset is closely tied to the *rate of fall* in core temperature. The post-heat cooling produces exactly that: a steep, well-defined fall.

The catch is that the cooling **needs one to two hours to play out**. This is what makes timing the entire question.

- Sauna at 5:15pm → core spikes, then falls across the whole evening, reaching its low around bedtime. Working with the curve.
- Sauna at 8 or 9pm → still in the rising or early-falling phase at bedtime. Working against it.

✅ A meta-analysis of passive body heating found that a warm bath or shower at 40–42.5 °C taken **one to two hours before bed**, even for 10 minutes, shortened the time to fall asleep and improved self-rated sleep quality and sleep efficiency (Haghayegh 2019). 🔴 Sauna itself has not been studied this way. A full sauna is a larger heat load and reasonably takes longer to settle, but that is extrapolation.

## Sauna and the night valve — three effects, pulling different ways

**During the session: vasopressin rises.** ✅ Sweating loses water, the blood becomes more concentrated, and rising blood concentration is the main everyday trigger for vasopressin release. The hormone rises; sauna-driven hormone changes return to normal within a couple of hours (Kukkonen-Harjula 1988). The body is defending its water.

**After rehydration: it shuts back off.** The lost water is replaced by drinking. That dilutes the blood, the concentration signal disappears, and vasopressin switches off. 🔴 Interpretation, not tested directly: done at 8 or 9pm, **the late rehydration switches the hormone off just before bed, so bedtime arrives with the valve open.** Done at 5pm, the whole sequence completes and resolves long before sleep.

**The rebound volume shift.** Heat dilates every vessel in the skin. On cooling, that volume returns to central circulation, and the kidney reads the fullness as a reason to excrete — the same mechanism as the leg-fluid return in [[PERFECT SLEEP — 05 Night Fluid]], stacked on top of it. 🔴 Plausible, but not measured after sauna.

## Cold exposure

**On urine output: it works against sleep.** Cold constricts skin and limb vessels, pushing blood centrally. The body reads excess central volume and excretes — cold-induced diuresis. ✅ Immersion reliably raises urine output: by 89–107% over an hour, largely from water pressure, with cool water at 20 °C adding little beyond neutral water (Šrámek 2000). 🟡 A role for vasopressin suppression is not established. 🔴 Five minutes of cold at 9pm plausibly pushes urine output up at exactly the wrong time; short cold showers have not been studied for this.

**On arousal: norepinephrine.** ✅ Cold immersion produces a large norepinephrine release — the alerting chemical: up 530% after an hour at 14 °C (Šrámek 2000). Excellent at 6am. 🔴 At 9pm it plausibly injects wakefulness into the runway to sleep; how long the effect outlasts a brief cold shower is not well measured.

**On temperature: it blocks the very thing the heat was for.** The sleep benefit of heat comes from the slow fall afterwards, achieved by dumping heat through wide-open skin vessels. Cold slams those vessels shut, trapping core heat rather than releasing it. The surface feels cooled while the core drop is blunted.

**But dose matters enormously.** All three effects scale with intensity. An ice bath is the extreme case. A cool shower that merely returns the body toward baseline produces a fraction of the vasopressin suppression and a fraction of the norepinephrine surge. In a setup using cool rather than cold water, the shower is a **minor** contributor — the sauna itself is doing far more to the night.

**Nothing here argues for dropping contrast therapy.** 🟡 The evidence for its benefits is modest: a 2025 meta-analysis of cold-water immersion trials found less stress 12 hours later and some improvement in sleep quality and quality of life, no effect on mood, and a short-term *rise* in inflammation markers (Cain 2025). It argues for moving it. Late afternoon, ideally before 6pm, keeps every benefit and removes all three nighttime costs.

## Sauna before dinner, not after

Three reasons:

1. **Fluid clears earlier.** Rehydrating at 5:30 rather than 9:00 means the water is processed well before bed.
2. **Digestion does not compete.** Heat drives blood to the skin; digestion wants it in the gut. Doing both at once does neither well, and a full stomach in a sauna is unpleasant anyway.
3. **The temperature curve lines up.** Core spikes at 5:30 and falls across the evening, arriving at its low around bedtime.

## An example evening sequence

- **~5:15pm** — sauna
- **After** — cool (not cold) shower
- **~5:30–6:00pm** — rehydrate
- **~6:30pm** — dinner
- **~7:00pm** — kitchen closed
- **~9:30–10:00pm** — bed

That gives roughly three hours of digestion and a long fluid runway, and puts the temperature low at bedtime rather than the temperature peak.

## Night sweats

Waking warm and sweating on some nights is common. Bedding is a more likely culprit than air temperature: a heavy duvet at 68°F can be warmer than a sheet at 72°F. Lighter bedding and breathable sheets often fix what thermostat changes do not.

But night sweats have other causes worth ruling out. 🟢 Night sweats are common and usually nonspecific (Mold 2012). Recognised causes include infection — for example in the recovery window after a urological procedure — obstructive sleep apnea, some medications, and low or falling sex-hormone levels, as in androgen-deprivation therapy (Lin 2025). In one cohort, frequent night sweats affected about 31% of men with untreated sleep apnea versus 9% of men in the general population, and fell with CPAP treatment (Arnardottir 2013). 🟡 Testosterone therapy itself is not an established cause; swings in level between injections are a plausible one. **For someone in either situation, worth raising with the physician rather than assuming it is the duvet.**

## Thermostat versus reality

🟡 The common 65–68°F recommendation is popular guidance, not a law, and it has little direct evidence behind it. In older adults monitored at home, sleep was most efficient at a bedroom temperature of 68–77°F (20–25 °C), with wide differences between people (Baniassadi 2023). With normal bedding, heat disturbs sleep stages more than cold does (Okamoto-Mizuno 2012). A thermostat reads air at one spot, usually a central wall; a bedroom can sit several degrees off that reading.

What actually matters is whether the body can dump heat, which depends on bedding, sleepwear and humidity as much as air temperature. **The real test is not the number** — it is whether the sleeper wakes warm, kicks covers off, or has cold hands and feet at bedtime. Cold extremities mean the heat dump is not happening, and that is the more important problem of the two. ✅ Widening of the blood vessels in the hands and feet is the best physiological predictor of falling asleep quickly (Kräuchi 1999).

## WHAT TO HOLD ONTO

- The sleep benefit of heat is the faster cooling afterwards, not the heat. It needs one to two hours to land; for sauna this is extrapolated from bath and shower studies.
- Late-evening sauna plus rehydration likely switches vasopressin off just before bed (🔴 untested).
- Cold raises urine output, releases norepinephrine, and blocks the heat dump — all three wrong for 9pm. Dose-dependent, so a cool shower is minor.
- Sauna first, then dinner. Never the reverse.
- Example sequence: sauna late afternoon, dinner about an hour later, kitchen closed about three hours before bed.
- Cold hands at bedtime are a worse sign than a warm room. Both are fixable.
- Night sweats after a procedure, around hormone therapy, or with snoring deserve a medical question, not just lighter bedding.
