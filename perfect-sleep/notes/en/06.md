# PERFECT SLEEP — 06 Heat and Timing

*Sauna, cold exposure and the evening sequence — judged only by their effect on sleep.*

**In plain words:** a sauna or hot bath helps sleep not because of the heat, but because of the cooling afterwards, and that cooling takes one to two hours. So heat belongs in the late afternoon, before dinner, not just before bed. Cold showers and ice baths are alerting and make more urine, so they also belong earlier in the day. Night sweats with snoring, after a procedure or around hormone treatment are worth asking a doctor about.

**Words in this note:**
- **CPAP**: a mask machine that keeps the airway open with air pressure during sleep

## The counterintuitive core: the benefit of heat is the cooling afterwards

A sauna or hot bath raises the body's inner (core) temperature for a short while. That is not the sleep benefit. It is the cost.

The benefit is the **cooling that follows**. The body answers the heat by opening the blood vessels in the skin wide, especially in the hands and feet, to get rid of it. That makes the core temperature fall faster and more steeply afterwards. 🟡 Whether core temperature actually drops *below* its usual curve is less consistent across studies. From [[PERFECT SLEEP — 04 The Clock]], falling asleep is closely tied to *how fast* core temperature is falling. The cooling after heat gives exactly that: a steep, clear fall.

The catch is that the cooling **needs one to two hours to work through**. That is why timing is the whole question.

- Sauna at 5:15pm → the core temperature jumps, then falls through the whole evening, reaching its low around bedtime. Working with the curve.
- Sauna at 8 or 9pm → still rising, or only starting to fall, at bedtime. Working against it.

✅ A study pooling many studies of body heating found that a warm bath or shower at 40–42.5 °C taken **one to two hours before bed**, even for 10 minutes, shortened the time to fall asleep and improved how well people felt they slept and how much of their time in bed they spent asleep (Haghayegh 2019). 🔴 The sauna itself has not been studied this way. A full sauna is a bigger heat load and would reasonably take longer to settle, but that is an educated guess.

## Sauna and the night valve — three effects, pulling different ways

**During the session: vasopressin rises.** ✅ Vasopressin is the hormone that holds back urine. Sweating loses water, the blood becomes more concentrated, and more concentrated blood is the main everyday trigger for vasopressin. The hormone rises; the hormone changes caused by the sauna return to normal within a couple of hours (Kukkonen-Harjula 1988). The body is protecting its water.

**After drinking: it switches back off.** You replace the lost water by drinking. That dilutes the blood, the signal disappears, and vasopressin switches off. 🔴 Interpretation, not tested directly: done at 8 or 9pm, **the late drinking switches the hormone off just before bed, so bedtime arrives with the valve open.** Done at 5pm, the whole sequence finishes long before sleep.

**The fluid shift on the way back.** Heat widens every blood vessel in the skin. When you cool, that blood returns to the centre of the body, and the kidney reads the fullness as a reason to make urine. This is the same mechanism as the fluid returning from the legs in [[PERFECT SLEEP — 05 Night Fluid]], added on top of it. 🔴 Believable, but not measured after a sauna.

## Cold exposure

**On urine: it works against sleep.** Cold squeezes the blood vessels in the skin and limbs, pushing blood to the centre. The body reads too much fluid in the middle and gets rid of it. This is cold-induced diuresis (extra urine caused by cold). ✅ Sitting in water reliably raises urine output, by 89–107% over an hour, mostly from the water pressure; cool water at 20 °C added little beyond neutral water (Šrámek 2000). 🟡 Whether lower vasopressin plays a part is not established. 🔴 Five minutes of cold at 9pm would likely push urine output up at exactly the wrong time; short cold showers have not been studied for this.

**On alertness: norepinephrine.** ✅ Sitting in cold water releases a large amount of norepinephrine, the brain's alerting chemical: up 530% after an hour at 14 °C (Šrámek 2000). Excellent at 6am. 🔴 At 9pm it would likely add wakefulness right when you are winding down to sleep; how long the effect lasts after a short cold shower is not well measured.

**On temperature: it blocks the very thing the heat was for.** The sleep benefit of heat comes from the slow fall afterwards, which happens by releasing heat through wide-open skin vessels. Cold slams those vessels shut, trapping heat inside instead of letting it out. The skin feels cool while the inner drop is smaller.

**But the dose matters hugely.** All three effects grow with how cold it is. An ice bath is the extreme. A cool shower that just brings the body back toward normal causes only a fraction of the drop in vasopressin and a fraction of the rise in norepinephrine. With cool rather than cold water, the shower plays a **minor** part; the sauna itself is doing far more to the night.

**Nothing here says to give up hot-and-cold therapy.** 🟡 The evidence for its benefits is modest: a 2025 study pooling trials of cold-water immersion found less stress 12 hours later and some improvement in sleep quality and quality of life, no effect on mood, and a short-term *rise* in signs of inflammation (Cain 2025). It says to move it. Late afternoon, ideally before 6pm, keeps every benefit and removes all three night-time costs.

## Sauna before dinner, not after

Three reasons:

1. **Fluid clears earlier.** Drinking to replace water at 5:30 rather than 9:00 means the water is dealt with well before bed.
2. **Digestion does not compete.** Heat sends blood to the skin; digestion wants it in the gut. Doing both at once does neither well, and a full stomach in a sauna is unpleasant anyway.
3. **The temperature curve lines up.** The core temperature jumps at 5:30 and falls through the evening, reaching its low around bedtime.

## An example evening sequence

- **~5:15pm** — sauna
- **After** — cool (not cold) shower
- **~5:30–6:00pm** — drink to replace water
- **~6:30pm** — dinner
- **~7:00pm** — kitchen closed
- **~9:30–10:00pm** — bed

That gives roughly three hours to digest and a long time for fluid to clear, and puts the temperature low point, not the peak, at bedtime.

## Night sweats

Waking warm and sweaty on some nights is common. Bedding is a more likely cause than the air temperature: a heavy duvet at 68°F can be warmer than a sheet at 72°F. Lighter bedding and breathable sheets often fix what changing the thermostat does not.

But night sweats have other causes worth ruling out. 🟢 Night sweats are common and usually have no single clear cause (Mold 2012). Known causes include infection (for example while recovering from a procedure on the urinary tract), obstructive sleep apnea (breathing that stops and starts during sleep), some medicines, and low or falling sex-hormone levels, as in androgen-deprivation therapy (treatment that lowers testosterone) (Lin 2025). In one group studied, frequent night sweats affected about 31% of men with untreated sleep apnea versus 9% of men in the general population, and fell with CPAP treatment (a machine that keeps the airway open with air pressure) (Arnardottir 2013). 🟡 Testosterone therapy itself is not an established cause; swings in the level between injections are a believable one. **For anyone in one of these situations, it is worth raising with the doctor rather than assuming it is the duvet.**

## Thermostat versus reality

🟡 The common 65–68°F advice is popular guidance, not a rule, and there is little direct evidence behind it. In older adults monitored at home, sleep was most efficient at a bedroom temperature of 68–77°F (20–25 °C), with wide differences between people (Baniassadi 2023). With normal bedding, heat disturbs the sleep stages more than cold does (Okamoto-Mizuno 2012). A thermostat reads the air at one spot, usually on a central wall; a bedroom can be several degrees different.

What really matters is whether the body can release heat, and that depends on bedding, nightwear and humidity as much as on air temperature. **The real test is not the number.** It is whether the sleeper wakes warm, kicks off the covers, or has cold hands and feet at bedtime. Cold hands and feet mean heat is not being released, and that is the more important problem of the two. ✅ Widening of the blood vessels in the hands and feet is the best body sign that someone will fall asleep quickly (Kräuchi 1999).

## WHAT TO HOLD ONTO

- The sleep benefit of heat is the faster cooling afterwards, not the heat itself. It needs one to two hours to work; for the sauna this is worked out from bath and shower studies.
- A late-evening sauna plus drinking afterwards likely switches vasopressin off just before bed (🔴 untested).
- Cold raises urine output, releases norepinephrine, and blocks heat release: all three wrong for 9pm. The effect depends on the dose, so a cool shower matters little.
- Sauna first, then dinner. Never the other way round.
- Example sequence: sauna in the late afternoon, dinner about an hour later, kitchen closed about three hours before bed.
- Cold hands at bedtime are a worse sign than a warm room. Both can be fixed.
- Night sweats after a procedure, around hormone therapy, or with snoring deserve a question to the doctor, not just lighter bedding.

**The point to remember: take your heat and cold in the late afternoon, before dinner, so your body is cooling down just as you go to bed.**
