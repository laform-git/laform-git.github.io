# PERFECT SLEEP — 06 Heat and Timing

*Sauna, cold exposure and the evening sequence — judged only by their effect on sleep.*

## The counterintuitive core: the benefit of heat is the cooling afterwards

A sauna or hot bath raises core temperature briefly. That is not the sleep benefit — it is the cost.

The benefit is the **rebound overshoot**. The body responds to the heat load by opening skin blood vessels wide to dump it, and it over-corrects, driving core temperature **below** baseline afterwards. From [[PERFECT SLEEP — 04 The Clock]], sleep onset is triggered by the *rate of fall* in core temperature. The post-heat overshoot produces exactly that: a steep, well-defined fall.

The catch is that the overshoot **needs one to two hours to play out**. This is what makes timing the entire question.

- Sauna at 5:15pm → core spikes, then falls across the whole evening, reaching its low around bedtime. Working with the curve.
- Sauna at 8 or 9pm → still in the rising or early-falling phase at bedtime. Working against it.

The passive body heating research generally points to a window of **one to two hours before bed** for a hot bath. A full sauna is a larger heat load and reasonably takes longer to settle.

## Sauna and the night valve — three effects, pulling different ways

**During the session: vasopressin rises.** Sweating loses water, the blood becomes more concentrated, and rising blood concentration is the strongest trigger for vasopressin release there is. The hormone spikes. The body is defending its water.

**After rehydration: it shuts back off.** The lost water is replaced by drinking. That dilutes the blood, the concentration signal disappears, and vasopressin switches off. Done at 8 or 9pm, **the hormone has been spent early and bedtime arrives with the valve open.** Done at 5pm, the whole sequence completes and resolves long before sleep.

**The rebound volume shift.** Heat dilates every vessel in the skin. On cooling, that volume returns to central circulation, and the kidney reads the fullness as a reason to excrete — the same mechanism as the leg-fluid return in [[PERFECT SLEEP — 05 Night Fluid]], stacked on top of it.

## Cold exposure

**On the hormone: it works against sleep.** Cold constricts skin and limb vessels, pushing blood centrally. The body reads excess central volume and responds by suppressing vasopressin and excreting — cold-induced diuresis. Cold immersion is one of the more reliable ways to produce a urine output spike. Five minutes of cold at 9pm is switching off the exact valve that should be switching on.

**On arousal: norepinephrine.** Cold immersion produces a large and sustained norepinephrine release — the alerting chemical. Excellent at 6am. At 9pm it is injecting wakefulness into the runway to sleep, and it outlasts the cold sensation itself by a long way.

**On temperature: it blocks the very thing the heat was for.** The sleep benefit of heat comes from the slow fall afterwards, achieved by dumping heat through wide-open skin vessels. Cold slams those vessels shut, trapping core heat rather than releasing it. The surface feels cooled while the core drop is blunted.

**But dose matters enormously.** All three effects scale with intensity. An ice bath is the extreme case. A cool shower that merely returns the body toward baseline produces a fraction of the vasopressin suppression and a fraction of the norepinephrine surge. In a setup using cool rather than cold water, the shower is a **minor** contributor — the sauna itself is doing far more to the night.

**Nothing here argues for dropping contrast therapy.** It has real value for mood, inflammation and alertness. It argues for moving it. Late afternoon, ideally before 6pm, keeps every benefit and removes all three nighttime costs.

## Sauna before dinner, not after

Three reasons:

1. **Fluid clears earlier.** Rehydrating at 5:30 rather than 9:00 means the water is processed well before bed.
2. **Digestion does not compete.** Heat drives blood to the skin; digestion wants it in the gut. Doing both at once does neither well, and a full stomach in a sauna is unpleasant anyway.
3. **The temperature curve lines up.** Core spikes at 5:30 and falls across the evening, arriving at its low around bedtime.

## An example evening sequence

- **~5:15pm** — sauna
- **After** — cool (not cold) shower
- **~5:30–6:00pm** — rehydrate
- **~6:30pm** — dinner
- **~7:00pm** — kitchen closed
- **~9:30–10:00pm** — bed

That gives roughly three hours of digestion and a long fluid runway, and puts the temperature low at bedtime rather than the temperature peak.

## Night sweats

Waking warm and sweating on some nights is common. Bedding is a more likely culprit than air temperature: a heavy duvet at 68°F can be warmer than a sheet at 72°F. Lighter bedding and breathable sheets often fix what thermostat changes do not.

But night sweats have other causes worth ruling out — for example, the recovery window after a urological procedure, or testosterone therapy. Both can produce them. **For someone in either situation, worth raising with the physician rather than assuming it is the duvet.**

## Thermostat versus reality

The 65–68°F recommendation is a research convention, not a law. A thermostat reads air at one spot, usually a central wall; a bedroom can sit several degrees off that reading.

What actually matters is whether the body can dump heat, which depends on bedding, sleepwear and humidity as much as air temperature. **The real test is not the number** — it is whether the sleeper wakes warm, kicks covers off, or has cold hands and feet at bedtime. Cold extremities mean the heat dump is not happening, and that is the more important problem of the two.

## WHAT TO HOLD ONTO

- The sleep benefit of a sauna is the rebound *undershoot* afterwards, not the heat. It needs one to two hours to land.
- Late-evening sauna spends the vasopressin surge early and leaves the valve open at bedtime.
- Cold suppresses vasopressin, releases norepinephrine, and blocks the heat dump — all three wrong for 9pm. Dose-dependent, so a cool shower is minor.
- Sauna first, then dinner. Never the reverse.
- Example sequence: sauna late afternoon, dinner about an hour later, kitchen closed about three hours before bed.
- Cold hands at bedtime are a worse sign than a warm room. Both are fixable.
- Night sweats on testosterone and post-procedure deserve a medical question, not just lighter bedding.
