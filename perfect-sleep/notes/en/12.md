# PERFECT SLEEP — 12 Metabolic Status and Sleep

*Insulin resistance is often the upstream driver of a wider health trajectory. This note covers only how it acts on sleep, and how sleep acts back on it. Marker values and interpretation fall outside this series.*

**In plain words:** poor sleep and insulin resistance (the body responding poorly to insulin) feed each other. Even one short night makes the body handle sugar worse, and insulin resistance in turn disturbs sleep through blood sugar swings, extra fluid, inflammation and breathing problems. Several loops connect the two, so fixing any one link helps more than you would expect. Moving during the day, eating dinner earlier and getting morning light all help both.

**Words in this note:**
- **HbA1c**: a blood test showing average blood sugar over about three months
- **NIDDK**: the NIH institute for diabetes, digestive and kidney diseases
- **BPH**: benign prostatic hyperplasia, a non-cancerous enlarged prostate
- **ApoB**: apolipoprotein B; counts all the cholesterol particles that can clog arteries

## The framing

Sleep quality depends on the state of the body's metabolism and hormones. Before treating a test result on its own, trace it back to where it comes from. This note does that tracing, in both directions.

## Sleep loss causes insulin resistance — fast

This is not a slow, long-term link. It can be measured within days.

- **A single night of short sleep measurably lowers how well healthy people respond to insulin.** In a precise lab test (a clamp study), one night of 4 hours' sleep cut how fast the body cleared sugar from the blood by about 20%, and the amount of sugar that had to be dripped in by about 25% (Donga 2010). One night. 🟢 A study pooling 41 randomised trials confirms that cutting sleep lowers the body's response to insulin (Zhu 2019).
- Six nights of 4 hours in bed made healthy young men handle sugar worse, to a level the authors compared with normal ageing (Spiegel 1999). 🟢
- The effect goes away with catch-up sleep (Spiegel 1999 measured it against six nights of recovery sleep), which is the encouraging half.

The routes: higher cortisol in the evening ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 10 Stress and Cortisol]]) and a stronger "fight or flight" drive (both shown in Spiegel 1999), plus body-wide inflammation and less sugar taken up by the muscles.

**What this means for blood tests:** a fasting blood sugar or fasting insulin taken after a run of broken nights is partly reporting on the sleep. HbA1c is different: it reflects average blood sugar over about the previous three months (NIDDK), so it only shows the effect of poor sleep that has lasted weeks to months. This matters for reading any such test honestly. 🔴 (interpretation of the studies above)

## Insulin resistance worsens sleep — four routes

**1. Unsteady blood sugar overnight.** In insulin resistance, blood sugar swings are bigger and the body's correction is sharper. That correction releases adrenaline and cortisol: the alert 3am waking from [[PERFECT SLEEP — 07 Food]]. Poor blood sugar control means more of those nights. 🔴 Believable but not directly studied: the link between overnight blood sugar swings and 3am waking in people without diabetes is worked out, not measured.

**2. Holding on to salt and water.** This is the least-known route and the one that matters most for nocturia (waking at night to urinate). **Insulin acts directly on the kidney tubes to make them take back salt** ✅ (DeFronzo 1975; Brosolo 2022). So high insulin causes the body to hold on to salt, and the salt holds water. The likely result is more total fluid in the body: more fluid to pool in the legs during the day ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 05 Night Fluid]]) and more to pass overnight. **Metabolic syndrome (a cluster of high blood sugar, high blood pressure, high blood fats and belly fat) is linked to nocturia on its own** (odds about 1.4 times higher, after accounting for other factors, in US national survey data; Kim 2026), by routes that have nothing to do with the prostate. 🟢 for the link; 🔴 that salt held because of insulin is the main route. It is a believable mechanism, not a proven one. (Once blood sugar is high enough to spill into the urine, the sugar itself also increases the amount of urine.)

**3. Inflammation.** Insulin resistance and fat around the organs (visceral fat) cause a low-level, body-wide inflammation. Inflammation messengers (cytokines) change the structure of sleep and add to tiredness whatever the amount of sleep. That is part of why tiredness can last even after the nights improve. 🟡

**4. Airway.** Fat around the organs and the neck raises the risk of obstructive sleep apnea (breathing that stops and starts during sleep) ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 14 Sleep-Disordered Breathing]]). The pathway is real even where there is no apnea now.

## The loop, stated plainly

Insulin resistance → holding on to salt and water → more urine overnight → broken sleep → higher cortisol and a weaker response to insulin → worse insulin resistance.

And alongside it: broken sleep → more hunger and more eating → more sugar for the body to handle → worse insulin resistance. 🟢 Across 41 randomised trials, cutting sleep raised hunger and eating by about 250 kcal a day; the popular "ghrelin (hunger hormone) up, leptin (fullness hormone) down" explanation did not hold up consistently (Zhu 2019).

And: broken sleep → tiredness → no exercise → weaker response to insulin → worse sleep.

**Three separate loops, all meeting at the same two points.** That is why neither problem clears up while the other is left alone, and why a step that breaks any one link helps more than it looks. 🔴 The loop model puts together the findings above; it has not been tested as a model.

## Where to break it

The good thing about a system with several loops is that there are many places to break in.

- **Unbroken sleep**: where a mechanical cause such as BPH (an enlarged prostate) is being treated, the rest of this series works on the other levers.
- **Morning light**: improves the cortisol slope ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 10 Stress and Cortisol]]), which improves the handling of sugar. 🔴
- **Moving during the day**: improves the response to insulin directly (breaking up sitting with short walks lowered blood sugar and insulin after meals; Dunstan 2012) and clears fluid from the legs ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 05 Night Fluid]], [[PERFECT SLEEP — 13 Exercise, Sleep Pressure and Naps]]). The single step that acts on the most loops at once.
- **What is in dinner**: flattening the evening blood sugar curve removes the correcting-adrenaline waking ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 07 Food]]).
- **When you eat**: the body's response to insulin follows the body clock and is better earlier in the day ✅ (Poggiogalle 2018). The same food eaten at 8pm causes a bigger blood sugar rise than at 1pm. An earlier dinner helps blood sugar as well as sleep.

## The circadian–metabolic link

Worth saying because it closes the loop with [[PERFECT SLEEP — 04 The Clock]]. The liver, pancreas and fat tissue each have their own daily clocks. They are set partly by the master clock in the brain and strongly by **mealtimes**.

Eating at hours that do not match the clock set by light puts them out of step with it. Research on shift workers points the same way, though the effect is modest: shift workers have about 9% higher odds of diabetes overall, and about 37% higher in men (Gan 2015). The clearer evidence comes from experiments: when healthy adults ate and slept about 12 hours out of step with their body clock, blood sugar rose even though they made 22% more insulin (Scheer 2009). **Timing itself affects metabolism.** 🟢

That makes a steady wake time, steady light and a steady, earlier dinner into tools for metabolism, not just for sleep.

## WHAT TO HOLD ONTO

- One night of short sleep cuts the body's response to insulin by roughly a fifth. It is that fast, and it recovers.
- Insulin makes the kidney hold on to salt, and salt holds water. **Metabolic syndrome is linked to nocturia apart from the prostate; salt held because of insulin is a believable route, not a proven one.**
- Big overnight blood sugar swings may cause the 3am adrenaline waking (believable, not proven). Better blood sugar control should mean fewer of them.
- Three separate loops meet at sleep and insulin resistance. Breaking any link helps more than it looks.
- The body's response to insulin follows the clock and is better earlier in the day: the same dinner at 8pm costs more than at 6pm.
- Moving during the day is the single step that touches the most loops at once.
- Fasting blood sugar and insulin taken after broken nights are partly reporting the sleep. HbA1c, a three-month average, moves only when poor sleep goes on for a long time.

**The point to remember: sleep and blood sugar rise and fall together, so better nights, daytime movement and an earlier dinner help both at once.**
