# PERFECT SLEEP — 08A Sedative Mechanisms: THC, Alcohol and CBD

*Mechanism note. Note 08 states what these substances do; this one explains how. Read [[PERFECT SLEEP — 01 Sleep Architecture]] first — the stage definitions are assumed throughout.*

## The REM switch — acetylcholine

REM is not a state the brain drifts into. It is actively switched on, and acetylcholine is one of the strongest levers on the switch. 🟢

Deep in the pons sit two cholinergic nuclei, **PPT and LDT** (pedunculopontine and laterodorsal tegmental). Activating them is enough to trigger REM in mice (Van Dort 2015). The core on/off switch itself, though, is now thought to be a pair of mutually inhibitory GABAergic REM-on and REM-off populations in the same brainstem region, with glutamatergic REM-on neurons driving cortical activation and atonia (Lu 2006). Cholinergic firing pushes that switch towards REM; it is not the only way in. 🟢 Together this network produces REM's three signatures:

- **PGO waves** — pontine-geniculate-occipital bursts travelling pons → thalamus → visual cortex. One branch drives the eye muscles, another activates the visual system. The eye movements and the dream imagery are two outputs of the same signal.
- **Cortical activation** — brain activity climbing to near-waking levels.
- **Atonia** — brainstem inhibition of motor neurons, paralysing skeletal muscle, driven mainly by glutamatergic REM-on neurons projecting to the medulla and spinal cord (Lu 2006). Eyes and diaphragm are the exceptions.

These neurons are held in check by the opposing aminergic system — noradrenaline and serotonin neurons that fire all day and go nearly silent in REM. REM is therefore a **flip-flop**: aminergic drops away, cholinergic takes over. Push either side chemically and the stage moves. This is the classic reciprocal-interaction model; the modern circuit adds the GABA/glutamate switch above, but the practical point stands. 🟢

**On the eye movements.** The *scanning hypothesis* holds that gaze direction tracks dream content — looking down into a void, eyes roll down. 🟡 Mixed evidence: in mice, REM eye movements track the animal's virtual head direction (Senzai 2022), but blind people show markedly fewer REM eye movements even when their dreams remain visual (Christensen 2019), and some movements match nothing visual. Correction to a common intuition: **the eyes are a readout, not a participant.** 🔴 Interpretation — there is no evidence the eye movements themselves contribute to memory processing. Note that hippocampal replay of the day's material to the cortex happens mainly during slow-wave (N3) sleep; REM's own contribution to consolidation is less settled (Klinzing 2019). The needle indicates the engine is running — it is not doing the work.

## THC — a presynaptic brake on the switch

CB1 receptors sit on **presynaptic terminals** — the release end — of many neuron types. CB1's job throughout the brain is to dampen neurotransmitter release.

🔴 Proposed mechanism, not shown directly in human REM circuitry: THC binds, the acetylcholine neurons still fire, but they **release less**. The switch weakens rather than breaking. Fewer PGO waves, shorter REM periods, less cortical activation. The stage is not abolished; it is thinned out. At high doses (70–210 mg/day) THC cut REM eye-movement density more than REM duration, which fits that picture (Feinberg 1975). 🟡 But a 2025 meta-analysis found that at today's lower doses REM suppression is inconsistent and often absent (Velzeboer 2025).

Simultaneously THC is sedating — older studies show faster sleep onset, and 15 mg at bedtime left measurable sleepiness the next morning, although it did not change that night's sleep stages (Nicholson 2004). The sedative route is not fully mapped (CB1 often *reduces* GABA release, so "boosting GABA" is not an accurate description). 🟡 Where both effects appear they are not in conflict: **deep-looking sleep, REM-poor.** This is the mechanistic answer to why sedation is not restoration.

**The daytime tail.** The same neurotransmitter runs attention and memory encoding from the **basal forebrain** while awake. Same chemical, different address. Next-morning memory impairment after a 15 mg evening dose is documented (Nicholson 2004). 🟢 Attributing next-day word-finding fog to residual THC at cholinergic terminals, rather than only to REM debt, is 🔴 interpretation — plausible, not shown.

## Alcohol — no receptor of its own

Alcohol has no dedicated receptor. It is a dirty drug, acting on several systems at once:

- **Enhances GABA-A** directly, the primary inhibitory channel
- **Blocks NMDA** glutamate receptors, the primary excitatory channel
- Pushes on serotonin and adenosine on the way through

REM is still suppressed — the first REM period is delayed at all doses, and total REM falls from about two drinks upward, dose-dependently (Ebrahim 2013; Gardiner 2025) ✅ — but for a different reason: broad sedation holding the whole system down, not a targeted hit on cholinergic drive. Same visible damage, entirely different route.

---

## The timing split — where they separate hardest

**THC is fat-soluble with active metabolites.** Clearance is slow, so the REM debt is not repaid the same night. Night one is REM-suppressed and flat; **night two is the rebound** — REM-heavy, shallow, fragmented, vivid dreams. One dose, two nights. The bad next day gets noticed; the night after it never gets connected. 🔴 Caveat: REM rebound with vivid dreams is well documented when *repeated* use stops (Feinberg 1975; Velzeboer 2025); a next-night rebound after a single dose has not been measured.

**Alcohol metabolises fast** — roughly one standard drink per hour, with wide individual variation — so it clears mid-night. The suppression lifts *while the person is still asleep* and the rebound happens **inside the same night**: fragmented second half with more wakefulness (Ebrahim 2013) ✅, a REM rebound 🟢, sweating, racing heart, the 3am waking.

So: alcohol front-loads the entire cost into one wrecked night and leaves. THC spreads a smaller hit across two.

**Tolerance masks both.** With frequent use, receptors downregulate (CB1 receptors in daily cannabis users are reduced and return to normal after about four weeks of abstinence: Hirvonen 2012 ✅) and suppression becomes the steady state — the contrast disappears, so nothing is noticed. After months off, receptors resensitise. The same THC on a naive system hits far harder than it did during regular use. **At restored sensitivity, occasional use is worse than regular use was.** 🔴 Interpretation. There is no isolated dose; each carries a two-night bill.

## The waking mechanisms — both cause wakings, neither the same way

**Alcohol — vasopressin suppression.** Alcohol suppresses vasopressin (antidiuretic hormone) release by blunting the brain's response to plasma concentration, so the kidneys make more dilute urine (Eisenhofer 1982) ✅. Straightforward diuresis — strongest while blood alcohol is rising and transient (in the classic study vasopressin fell in the first hour, then rose again), so the drink closest to bed costs the most. In someone already waking several times a night, a direct additive hit ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 05 Night Fluid]]).

**THC — lowered arousal threshold.** No recognised diuretic effect. The damage is indirect: arousal threshold is lowest in REM ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 01 Sleep Architecture]]), and the rebound night is REM-heavy. Bladder signals that would not break through N3 break through easily from REM ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 02 Nocturia and Broken Sleep]]).

Same outcome — more wakings — via volume in one case and via sleep depth in the other.

## CBD — the one that does not suppress REM

CBD does not bind CB1 with meaningful affinity. It is not a CB1 agonist and does not apply the presynaptic brake to acetylcholine. **It therefore does not carry THC's REM suppression or its two-night rebound.** 🟡 Consistent with the one good sleep-lab trial: 300 mg CBD left sleep architecture unchanged in healthy adults (Linares 2018) — small, single night. That is the single most useful distinction in this note.

Its actions are indirect and less well characterised: 5-HT1A interaction, modulation of anandamide availability, effects that appear anxiolytic at moderate doses. Evidence quality is weaker than the marketing implies, and dose effects are inconsistent: 15 mg was mildly alerting in one small study (Nicholson 2004), while much higher doses have been reported as sedating.

Full ratio discussion lives in [[CBD:THC Ratio for Sleep]] — not duplicated here.

## Alcohol's broader cost — beyond sleep

The sleep effect is the smaller charge on the bill.

- **Acetaldehyde** — alcohol's first metabolite before becoming acetate. Classed by IARC as a Group 1 human carcinogen when associated with alcohol consumption (Secretan 2009); it forms DNA adducts. A major mechanism behind the links to breast, liver, oesophageal and colorectal cancer (breast risk also runs through raised oestrogen). Dose-dependent; WHO states no safe threshold has been identified for cancer risk (Anderson 2023). ✅
- **Metabolic priority** — the liver processes alcohol ahead of everything else, so fat oxidation drops sharply while it clears — by about three-quarters after 24 g of alcohol, roughly two drinks (Siler 1999) ✅. Drives hepatic fat accumulation and raises triglycerides; heavy intake worsens insulin resistance (data on light intake are contested 🟡). Relevant in anyone whose HbA1c already sits in the prediabetic range or whose ApoB is elevated.
- Cortisol elevation, gut barrier disruption, blood pressure effects.

**Honest verdict** (🔴 interpretation): alcohol's overall harm profile is broader than THC's and includes an oncogenic pathway THC does not have. THC's documented costs concentrate in sleep architecture (most clearly on withdrawal) and dopamine (lower striatal dopamine synthesis in heavy users). A testosterone cost is not supported by population data (Thistle 2017). Alcohol is worse overall — with a cleaner morning, which is exactly what makes it deceptive.

## Side by side

| | THC | Alcohol | CBD |
|---|---|---|---|
| Route | CB1 presynaptic brake on acetylcholine | GABA-A enhancement plus NMDA blockade, no dedicated receptor | No meaningful CB1 binding; 5-HT1A, anandamide |
| REM suppression | Clear at high doses; inconsistent at current doses 🟡 | Yes, dose-dependent, via broad sedation | No (one trial) 🟡 |
| Rebound timing | On stopping repeated use (documented); night two after a single dose (unmeasured 🔴) | Same night, mid-sleep, fast clearance | Not applicable |
| Waking mechanism | Lowered arousal threshold in rebound REM | Vasopressin suppression, diuresis | — |
| Next-day tail | Yes — next-morning memory and sleepiness effects at 15 mg; mechanism unproven | Minimal pharmacologically; hangover is separate | Minimal |
| Systemic cost | Dopamine (heavy use); testosterone and insulin costs not supported by population data | Carcinogenic metabolite, hepatic fat, insulin resistance | Poorly characterised |

## WHAT TO HOLD ONTO

- Acetylcholine is a powerful lever on the REM switch; the switch itself is a GABA/glutamate circuit in the brainstem.
- THC dampens acetylcholine release at CB1 (the proposed REM route). Alcohol has no receptor — it floods GABA-A and blocks NMDA.
- Same visible damage, different route, different rebound timing: THC rebounds when repeated use stops (a night-two rebound after one dose is plausible but unmeasured), alcohol rebounds mid-night, same night.
- The eyes are a readout of REM activity, not a participant in memory consolidation.
- Alcohol causes wakings by increasing urine volume. THC causes wakings by making sleep shallow.
- CBD does not suppress REM in the one good trial — the one meaningful distinction from THC.
- Alcohol is the worse drug overall with the cleaner morning. That is what makes it deceptive.
