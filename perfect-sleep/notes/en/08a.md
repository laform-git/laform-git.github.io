# PERFECT SLEEP — 08A Sedative Mechanisms: THC, Alcohol and CBD

*Mechanism note. Note 08 states what these substances do; this one explains how. Read [[PERFECT SLEEP — 01 Sleep Architecture]] first — the stage definitions are assumed throughout.*

**In plain words:** dream sleep (REM) is switched on by a brain chemical called acetylcholine. THC seems to turn that switch down, so sleep looks deep but has less dream sleep, and the missing dream sleep comes back later. Alcohol knocks the whole brain down instead, then wears off in the middle of the night, breaking the second half of sleep and making more urine. CBD does not seem to hold dream sleep back. Alcohol does the most harm overall, even though the morning after feels cleaner.

**Words in this note:**
- **THC**: the part of cannabis that makes you high
- **CBD**: cannabidiol, a non-intoxicating part of cannabis
- **REM**: rapid eye movement sleep, the dreaming stage
- **GABA**: the brain's main calming chemical messenger
- **CB1**: the brain's main receptor for cannabis
- **GABA-A**: the receptor for GABA that sleeping pills and alcohol act on
- **NMDA**: a receptor for glutamate, the brain's main "go" messenger
- **DNA**: the genetic code inside each cell
- **WHO**: World Health Organization
- **HbA1c**: a blood test showing average blood sugar over about three months
- **ApoB**: apolipoprotein B; counts all the cholesterol particles that can clog arteries

## The REM switch — acetylcholine

REM (dream sleep) is not a state the brain drifts into. It is actively switched on, and acetylcholine, a chemical messenger in the brain, is one of the strongest levers on the switch. 🟢

Deep in the pons (part of the brainstem) sit two clusters of nerve cells that use acetylcholine (cholinergic cells), called **PPT and LDT** (pedunculopontine and laterodorsal tegmental). Switching them on is enough to start REM in mice (Van Dort 2015). The main on/off switch itself, though, is now thought to be a pair of cell groups in the same area that block each other: REM-on and REM-off cells that use GABA (a calming messenger), with REM-on cells that use glutamate (an exciting messenger) driving the active brain and the muscle paralysis (Lu 2006). Acetylcholine pushes that switch toward REM; it is not the only way in. 🟢 Together this network produces REM's three signs:

- **PGO waves**: bursts of activity travelling from the pons to the thalamus to the visual part of the brain. One branch moves the eye muscles, another switches on the visual system. The eye movements and the dream pictures are two outputs of the same signal.
- **An active outer brain (cortex)**: brain activity rising to near-waking levels.
- **Atonia (paralysis)**: the brainstem blocks the nerves to the muscles, paralysing the body. This is driven mainly by glutamate-using REM-on cells that reach down to the lower brainstem and spinal cord (Lu 2006). The eyes and the diaphragm (the breathing muscle) are the exceptions.

These cells are held in check by an opposing system: nerve cells using noradrenaline and serotonin, which fire all day and go nearly silent in REM. So REM is a **flip-flop**: the noradrenaline-serotonin side drops away and the acetylcholine side takes over. Push either side with a chemical and the stage moves. This is the classic "reciprocal interaction" model; the modern map adds the GABA/glutamate switch above, but the practical point stands. 🟢

**About the eye movements.** The *scanning idea* says the direction of the eyes follows what happens in the dream: looking down into a pit, the eyes roll down. 🟡 The evidence is mixed. In mice, REM eye movements follow which way the animal's head is facing in its virtual world (Senzai 2022). But blind people show far fewer REM eye movements even when their dreams are still visual (Christensen 2019), and some movements match nothing visual. A correction to a common belief: **the eyes are a gauge, not a worker.** 🔴 Interpretation: there is no evidence the eye movements themselves help process memories. Note that the brain replays the day's material from the hippocampus (a memory centre) to the cortex mainly during deep (N3) sleep; how much REM itself adds to storing memories is less settled (Klinzing 2019). The needle shows the engine is running; it is not doing the work.

## THC — a presynaptic brake on the switch

CB1 receptors (the switches THC acts on) sit on the **sending end** of many kinds of nerve cells, where they release their messages. Throughout the brain, CB1's job is to turn down how much messenger is released.

🔴 A proposed mechanism, not shown directly in the human REM circuit: THC attaches, the acetylcholine cells still fire, but they **release less**. The switch weakens rather than breaking. Fewer PGO waves, shorter REM periods, a less active brain. The stage is not wiped out; it is thinned out. At high doses (70–210 mg/day), THC cut how busy the REM eye movements were more than how long REM lasted, which fits that picture (Feinberg 1975). 🟡 But a 2025 study pooling many studies found that at today's lower doses the drop in REM is inconsistent and often absent (Velzeboer 2025).

At the same time THC is sedating. Older studies show people fall asleep faster, and 15 mg at bedtime left measurable sleepiness the next morning, although it did not change that night's sleep stages (Nicholson 2004). How the sedation works is not fully mapped (CB1 often *reduces* the release of GABA, so "boosting GABA" is not an accurate description). 🟡 Where both effects show up, they do not clash: **sleep that looks deep but is poor in REM.** This is the mechanical answer to why being sedated is not the same as being restored.

**The daytime tail.** The same messenger, acetylcholine, runs attention and the storing of new memories from the **basal forebrain** (an area at the base of the front of the brain) while we are awake. Same chemical, different address. Memory problems the next morning after a 15 mg evening dose are documented (Nicholson 2004). 🟢 Blaming next-day trouble finding words on THC still acting at acetylcholine cells, rather than only on lost REM, is 🔴 interpretation: believable, not shown.

## Alcohol — no receptor of its own

Alcohol has no switch of its own. It is a "dirty" drug, acting on several systems at once:

- **Strengthens GABA-A** directly, the brain's main calming channel
- **Blocks NMDA** receptors, the main channel for glutamate, the brain's main exciting messenger
- Pushes on serotonin and adenosine along the way

REM is still held back. The first REM period comes later at all doses, and total REM falls from about two drinks upward, more with more drinks (Ebrahim 2013; Gardiner 2025) ✅. But the reason is different: broad sedation holding the whole system down, not a targeted hit on acetylcholine. The same visible damage by an entirely different route.

---

## The timing split — where they separate hardest

**THC dissolves in fat and is broken down into products that are still active.** It clears slowly, so the lost REM is not paid back the same night. Night one has less REM and feels flat; **night two is the rebound**: heavy in REM, shallow, broken, with vivid dreams. One dose, two nights. People notice the bad next day; they never connect the night after it. 🔴 Caveat: a REM rebound with vivid dreams is well documented when *repeated* use stops (Feinberg 1975; Velzeboer 2025); a rebound the next night after a single dose has not been measured.

**Alcohol is broken down fast**, roughly one standard drink per hour, though people vary widely, so it is gone by the middle of the night. The suppression lifts *while the person is still asleep*, and the rebound happens **inside the same night**: a broken second half with more time awake (Ebrahim 2013) ✅, a REM rebound 🟢, sweating, racing heart, the 3am waking.

So alcohol puts the whole cost into one ruined night and leaves. THC spreads a smaller hit over two.

**Tolerance hides both.** With frequent use, the receptors are cut back (CB1 receptors in daily cannabis users are reduced and return to normal after about four weeks without it: Hirvonen 2012 ✅), and the suppression becomes the normal state. The contrast disappears, so nothing is noticed. After months off, the receptors become sensitive again. The same THC on a fresh system hits far harder than it did during regular use. **Once sensitivity is back, occasional use is worse than regular use was.** 🔴 Interpretation. There is no isolated dose; each one comes with a two-night bill.

## The waking mechanisms — both cause wakings, neither the same way

**Alcohol: it blocks vasopressin.** Alcohol holds back the release of vasopressin (the hormone that holds back urine) by dulling the brain's response to how concentrated the blood is, so the kidneys make more watery urine (Eisenhofer 1982) ✅. Plain extra urine. It is strongest while blood alcohol is rising and does not last (in the classic study vasopressin fell in the first hour, then rose again), so the drink closest to bed costs the most. In someone who already wakes several times a night, it adds directly to the problem ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 05 Night Fluid]]).

**THC: it makes waking easier.** It has no known effect on urine. The damage is indirect: it is easiest to wake from REM ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 01 Sleep Architecture]]), and the rebound night is heavy in REM. Bladder signals that would not get through deep N3 sleep get through easily from REM ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 02 Nocturia and Broken Sleep]]).

The same result, more wakings, through more urine in one case and through lighter sleep in the other.

## CBD — the one that does not suppress REM

CBD (cannabidiol, a part of cannabis that does not make you high) barely attaches to CB1. It does not switch CB1 on and does not put the brake on acetylcholine. **So it does not carry THC's REM suppression or its two-night rebound.** 🟡 This fits the one good sleep-lab trial: 300 mg of CBD left the sleep stages unchanged in healthy adults (Linares 2018), a small study of a single night. That is the single most useful distinction in this note.

Its effects are indirect and less well understood: it acts on the 5-HT1A serotonin switch, changes how much anandamide (the body's own cannabis-like chemical) is available, and seems to ease anxiety at moderate doses. The quality of the evidence is weaker than the marketing suggests, and the effects of the dose are inconsistent: 15 mg was mildly alerting in one small study (Nicholson 2004), while much higher doses have been reported as sedating.

The full discussion of mixing ratios is in [[CBD:THC Ratio for Sleep]], not repeated here.

## Alcohol's broader cost — beyond sleep

The effect on sleep is the smaller charge on the bill.

- **Acetaldehyde**: the first thing alcohol is broken into, before it becomes acetate. IARC (the World Health Organization's cancer agency) lists it as a Group 1 cause of cancer in humans when it comes from drinking alcohol (Secretan 2009); it damages DNA by sticking to it. It is a major reason for the links to breast, liver, oesophageal (food pipe) and bowel cancer (breast risk also comes through raised oestrogen). The risk grows with the dose; the WHO says no safe level has been found for cancer risk (Anderson 2023). ✅
- **The liver deals with alcohol first**, ahead of everything else, so fat burning drops sharply while it clears: by about three-quarters after 24 g of alcohol, roughly two drinks (Siler 1999) ✅. This builds up fat in the liver and raises triglycerides (blood fats); heavy drinking worsens insulin resistance (the body responding poorly to insulin) (the data on light drinking are disputed 🟡). It matters for anyone whose HbA1c (average blood sugar test) is already in the prediabetes range or whose ApoB (a count of artery-clogging cholesterol particles) is high.
- Raised cortisol (a stress hormone), damage to the gut lining, effects on blood pressure.

**Honest verdict** (🔴 interpretation): alcohol's overall harm is broader than THC's and includes a cancer route that THC does not have. THC's documented costs are mostly in sleep structure (most clearly on stopping) and dopamine (heavy users make less dopamine in a reward centre of the brain). A cost to testosterone is not supported by data from large populations (Thistle 2017). Alcohol is worse overall, with a cleaner morning, and that is exactly what makes it deceptive.

## Side by side

| | THC | Alcohol | CBD |
|---|---|---|---|
| Route | CB1 presynaptic brake on acetylcholine | GABA-A enhancement plus NMDA blockade, no dedicated receptor | No meaningful CB1 binding; 5-HT1A, anandamide |
| REM suppression | Clear at high doses; inconsistent at current doses 🟡 | Yes, dose-dependent, via broad sedation | No (one trial) 🟡 |
| Rebound timing | On stopping repeated use (documented); night two after a single dose (unmeasured 🔴) | Same night, mid-sleep, fast clearance | Not applicable |
| Waking mechanism | Lowered arousal threshold in rebound REM | Vasopressin suppression, diuresis | — |
| Next-day tail | Yes — next-morning memory and sleepiness effects at 15 mg; mechanism unproven | Minimal pharmacologically; hangover is separate | Minimal |
| Systemic cost | Dopamine (heavy use); testosterone and insulin costs not supported by population data | Carcinogenic metabolite, hepatic fat, insulin resistance | Poorly characterised |

(In the table: "presynaptic brake" means turning down release at the sending end of the nerve cell; "arousal threshold" means how easily you wake; "diuresis" means extra urine; "carcinogenic metabolite" means a cancer-causing breakdown product; "hepatic fat" means fat in the liver.)

## WHAT TO HOLD ONTO

- Acetylcholine is a powerful lever on the REM switch; the switch itself is a GABA/glutamate circuit in the brainstem.
- THC turns down the release of acetylcholine at CB1 (the proposed route to less REM). Alcohol has no switch of its own: it floods GABA-A and blocks NMDA.
- The same visible damage, a different route, a different rebound time: THC rebounds when repeated use stops (a second-night rebound after one dose is believable but unmeasured); alcohol rebounds in the middle of the same night.
- The eyes are a gauge of REM activity, not a worker in storing memories.
- Alcohol causes wakings by making more urine. THC causes wakings by making sleep shallow.
- CBD does not hold back REM in the one good trial: the one meaningful difference from THC.
- Alcohol is the worse drug overall, with the cleaner morning. That is what makes it deceptive.

**The point to remember: being knocked out is not the same as being restored; THC and alcohol both cost dream sleep, and alcohol costs the most overall.**
