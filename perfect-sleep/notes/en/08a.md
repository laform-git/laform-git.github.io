# PERFECT SLEEP — 08A Sedative Mechanisms: THC, Alcohol and CBD

*Mechanism note. Note 08 states what these substances do; this one explains how. Read [[PERFECT SLEEP — 01 Sleep Architecture]] first — the stage definitions are assumed throughout.*

## The REM switch — acetylcholine

REM is not a state the brain drifts into. It is actively switched on, and acetylcholine is the switch.

Deep in the pons sit two cholinergic nuclei, **PPT and LDT** (pedunculopontine and laterodorsal tegmental). When they fire, REM begins. One population produces all three of REM's signatures at once:

- **PGO waves** — pontine-geniculate-occipital bursts travelling pons → thalamus → visual cortex. One branch drives the eye muscles, another activates the visual system. The eye movements and the dream imagery are two outputs of the same signal.
- **Cortical activation** — brain activity climbing to near-waking levels.
- **Atonia** — brainstem inhibition of motor neurons, paralysing skeletal muscle. Eyes and diaphragm are the exceptions.

These neurons are held in check by the opposing aminergic system — noradrenaline and serotonin neurons that fire all day and go nearly silent in REM. REM is therefore a **flip-flop**: aminergic drops away, cholinergic takes over. Push either side chemically and the stage moves.

**On the eye movements.** The *scanning hypothesis* holds that gaze direction tracks dream content — looking down into a void, eyes roll down. Moderate support, not settled: some movements match nothing visual, and congenitally blind people still show REM eye movements. Correction to a common intuition: **the eyes are a readout, not a participant.** Consolidation is the hippocampus replaying the day's material to the cortex; the eye movements ride the same wave of activity but contribute nothing to it. The needle indicates the engine is running — it is not doing the work.

## THC — a presynaptic brake on the switch

CB1 receptors sit on the **presynaptic terminals** of cholinergic neurons — the release end. CB1's job throughout the brain is to dampen neurotransmitter release.

So THC binds, the acetylcholine neurons still fire, but they **release less**. The switch weakens rather than breaking. Fewer PGO waves, shorter REM periods, less cortical activation. The stage is not abolished; it is thinned out.

Simultaneously THC boosts GABA-driven sedation — fast onset, sleep maintained. The two effects are not in conflict: **deep-looking sleep, REM-poor.** This is the mechanistic answer to why sedation is not restoration.

**The daytime tail.** The same neurotransmitter runs attention and memory encoding from the **basal forebrain** while awake. Same chemical, different address. Next-day word-finding fog is therefore not only REM debt — it is residual THC still occupying cholinergic terminals in the waking brain.

---

## Alcohol — no receptor of its own

Alcohol has no dedicated receptor. It is a dirty drug, acting on several systems at once:

- **Enhances GABA-A** directly, the primary inhibitory channel
- **Blocks NMDA** glutamate receptors, the primary excitatory channel
- Pushes on serotonin and adenosine on the way through

REM is still suppressed in the first half of the night, but for a different reason: broad sedation holding the whole system down, not a targeted hit on cholinergic drive. Same visible damage, entirely different route.

---

## The timing split — where they separate hardest

**THC is fat-soluble with active metabolites.** Clearance is slow, so the REM debt is not repaid the same night. Night one is REM-suppressed and flat; **night two is the rebound** — REM-heavy, shallow, fragmented, vivid dreams. One dose, two nights. The bad next day gets noticed; the night after it never gets connected.

**Alcohol metabolises fast** — roughly one standard drink per hour — so it clears mid-night. The suppression lifts *while the person is still asleep* and the rebound happens **inside the same night**: REM-heavy fragmented second half, sweating, racing heart, the 3am waking.

So: alcohol front-loads the entire cost into one wrecked night and leaves. THC spreads a smaller hit across two.

**Tolerance masks both.** With frequent use, receptors downregulate and suppression becomes the steady state — the contrast disappears, so nothing is noticed. After months off, receptors resensitise. The same THC on a naive system hits far harder than it did during regular use. **At restored sensitivity, occasional use is worse than regular use was.** There is no isolated dose; each carries a two-night bill.

---

## The waking mechanisms — both cause wakings, neither the same way

**Alcohol — vasopressin suppression.** Alcohol directly suppresses vasopressin (antidiuretic hormone), so the kidneys produce more urine overnight. Straightforward diuresis. In someone already waking several times a night, a direct additive hit ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 05 Night Fluid]]).

**THC — lowered arousal threshold.** No diuretic effect. The damage is indirect: arousal threshold is lowest in REM ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 01 Sleep Architecture]]), and the rebound night is REM-heavy. Bladder signals that would not break through N3 break through easily from REM ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 02 Nocturia and Broken Sleep]]).

Same outcome — more wakings — via volume in one case and via sleep depth in the other.

---

## CBD — the one that does not suppress REM

CBD does not bind CB1 with meaningful affinity. It is not a CB1 agonist and does not apply the presynaptic brake to acetylcholine. **It therefore does not carry THC's REM suppression or its two-night rebound.** That is the single most useful distinction in this note.

Its actions are indirect and less well characterised: 5-HT1A interaction, modulation of anandamide availability, effects that appear anxiolytic at moderate doses. Evidence quality is weaker than the marketing implies, and high doses have their own alerting profile.

Full ratio discussion lives in [[CBD:THC Ratio for Sleep]] — not duplicated here.

## Alcohol's broader cost — beyond sleep

The sleep effect is the smaller charge on the bill.

- **Acetaldehyde** — alcohol's first metabolite before becoming acetate. A recognised carcinogen that binds DNA directly. Mechanism behind the links to breast, liver, oesophageal and colorectal cancer. Dose-dependent, no established safe threshold.
- **Metabolic priority** — the liver processes alcohol ahead of everything else, so fat oxidation halts while it clears. Drives hepatic fat accumulation, worsens insulin resistance, raises triglycerides. Relevant in anyone whose HbA1c already sits in the prediabetic range or whose ApoB is elevated.
- Cortisol elevation, gut barrier disruption, blood pressure effects.

**Honest verdict:** alcohol's overall harm profile is broader than THC's and includes an oncogenic pathway THC does not have. THC's damage concentrates in sleep architecture, dopamine and hormones. Alcohol is worse overall — with a cleaner morning, which is exactly what makes it deceptive.

## Side by side

| | THC | Alcohol | CBD |
|---|---|---|---|
| Route | CB1 presynaptic brake on acetylcholine | GABA-A enhancement plus NMDA blockade, no dedicated receptor | No meaningful CB1 binding; 5-HT1A, anandamide |
| REM suppression | Yes, targeted | Yes, via broad sedation | No |
| Rebound timing | Night two, slow clearance | Same night, mid-sleep, fast clearance | Not applicable |
| Waking mechanism | Lowered arousal threshold in rebound REM | Vasopressin suppression, diuresis | — |
| Next-day tail | Yes — residual CB1 occupancy, cholinergic fog | Minimal pharmacologically; hangover is separate | Minimal |
| Systemic cost | Dopamine, testosterone, insulin sensitivity | Carcinogenic metabolite, hepatic fat, insulin resistance | Poorly characterised |

## WHAT TO HOLD ONTO

- Acetylcholine is the REM switch. No acetylcholine, no REM.
- THC brakes acetylcholine release at CB1. Alcohol has no receptor — it floods GABA-A and blocks NMDA.
- Same visible damage, different route, different rebound timing: THC rebounds on night two, alcohol rebounds mid-night, same night.
- The eyes are a readout of consolidation, not a participant in it.
- Alcohol causes wakings by increasing urine volume. THC causes wakings by making sleep shallow.
- CBD does not suppress REM — the one meaningful distinction from THC.
- Alcohol is the worse drug overall with the cleaner morning. That is what makes it deceptive.
