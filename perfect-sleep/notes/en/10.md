# PERFECT SLEEP — 10 Stress and Cortisol

*The other major output of the clock, and a leading cause of the 3am alert waking.*

**In plain words:** cortisol is a hormone that follows a daily curve: high soon after waking, low around midnight. Long-term stress flattens that curve, so you wake tired and feel wired at bedtime. Lost sleep raises cortisol, and high cortisol spoils sleep, so each makes the other worse. Morning light, a quiet hour before bed, writing down the day, slow breathing and well-timed exercise help bring evening cortisol down.

**Words in this note:**
- **HPA**: the hypothalamus-pituitary-adrenal axis, the body's stress system
- **ACTH**: adrenocorticotropic hormone, the brain's signal to the adrenal glands to make cortisol
- **DHEA-S**: the stored, blood-test form of DHEA

## The normal curve

Cortisol is not a stress hormone that happens to have a rhythm. It is **a hormone that follows the body clock and also responds to stress**, and the rhythm is the bigger part of the story.

- **Lowest around midnight.** Cortisol should be at its lowest around the time you fall asleep ✅ (Buckley 2005). This quiet period matches the deepest sleep of the night, and an active stress-hormone system (the HPA axis, the brain-to-adrenal chain that releases cortisol) in the evening goes with lighter, more broken sleep (Vgontzas 2001) 🟢. Whether the low cortisol is what *allows* deep sleep is 🔴 interpretation.
- **Rises through the second half of the night**, getting the body ready to wake.
- **The cortisol awakening response**: a sharp rise of roughly 50–160% in the 30–45 minutes after waking (Pruessner 1997; Clow 2004) ✅. This is a separate event that can be measured (Stalder 2016). That it is "what makes waking feel like waking" is 🔴 interpretation.
- **Falls across the day** to the midnight low again.

The important feature is the **slope**: a steep drop from the morning high to the midnight low. A steep slope is the healthy pattern. Flatter slopes went with poorer mental and physical health across 80 studies (Adam 2017) 🟢. That is a link, and a small one, not proof that the slope matters more than the average level.

## What goes wrong

**Long-term stress flattens the curve.** It does not simply "raise cortisol." A common pattern is a weak morning rise and a high evening level. 🟢 The pattern depends on the kind of stress and when it happened: cortisol is high when stress starts and falls with time, and stress you cannot control, or trauma, gives a *high and flat* pattern: more hormone *and* a smaller swing (Miller 2007). The result is waking unrefreshed and feeling wired at bedtime. **Tired and wired is a slope problem.**

**High cortisol in the evening blocks sleep.** Cortisol makes you alert. If it has not fallen by bedtime, the pressure to sleep and the clock's signal are fighting an active wake signal. 🟢 People with long-term insomnia release more ACTH (the brain hormone that tells the adrenal glands to make cortisol) and more cortisol, most of all in the evening and the first half of the night (Vgontzas 2001).

**The 3am waking.** A waking in the middle of the night, fully alert, sometimes with a racing heart, and unable to get back to sleep, is often blamed on the stress-hormone system firing: a burst of cortisol arriving too early and too big. It feels like anxiety because the chemistry overlaps. It is often taken for a mental event when the cause is the body's timing. 🔴 Interpretation: believable and widely repeated, but not directly shown. Bursts of cortisol also *follow* wakings, so which comes first is not settled.

Two other things cause the same 3am pattern and are worth telling apart: **the body's correcting adrenaline after a sugary dinner** ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 07 Food]]) 🟡 (believable, little direct evidence) and **alcohol wearing off** ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 07 Food]]) ✅. All three feel the same from the inside.

## The bidirectional loop

Lost sleep raises cortisol. After one night of partial or total sleep loss, evening cortisol was 37–45% higher the next day and its evening fall started at least an hour later (Leproult 1997) ✅; a week of 4-hour nights gave the same pattern (Spiegel 1999). High cortisol spoils sleep 🟢. This is one of the tightest self-feeding loops in the series.

It also links straight to metabolism ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 12 Metabolic Status and Sleep]]). Cortisol makes the liver produce new sugar (gluconeogenesis), raises blood sugar, worsens insulin resistance (the body responding poorly to insulin), and encourages fat to build up around the organs (visceral fat) ✅ (clearest when it stays high for a long time). Where insulin resistance is already the root driver of someone's health path, the sleep–cortisol loop is not a side issue.

## Cortisol and night urine

A lesser-known link, and a theory. Cortisol affects how the kidneys handle water and salt, and hormones of the cortisol family are needed for the body to pass water normally, separately from vasopressin (the hormone that holds back urine) ✅. That a flattened curve with high night-time cortisol therefore adds to nocturia (waking at night to urinate), by a route separate from everything in [[PERFECT SLEEP — 05 Night Fluid]], is 🔴 unproven. No study was found that measures it directly.

## What actually lowers evening cortisol

**Morning light.** The morning cortisol rise is anchored by the same clock that light sets ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 04 The Clock]]). Bright light (800 lux) after waking raised morning cortisol by about another 35%, while the same light late in the evening had no effect (Scheer 1999) ✅. That a sharper morning peak then gives a steeper fall into the evening is 🔴 inference. **Morning light is a believable way to lower evening cortisol.**

**A real wind-down.** Cortisol needs time to fall. Sixty minutes of calm, low activity before bed does more than any supplement in [[PERFECT SLEEP — 08 Substances and Stack]]. The fall is a body process that takes time. 🔴 The sixty-minute figure and the comparison with supplements are practical judgement, not trial results.

**Getting worries out of your head.** Lying in bed going over the day keeps cortisol up. Writing the day down, or the Examen (a short evening prayer reviewing the day) ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 16 Contemplative Practice]]), moves that thinking out of the bed and out of the dark. 🟢 In a sleep-lab study, five minutes writing a specific to-do list at bedtime led to falling asleep faster than writing about tasks already done (Scullin 2018).

**Slow breathing.** A long breath out strengthens the vagus nerve (the body's calming nerve) and lowers the "fight or flight" drive, which shows up within minutes as higher heart-rate variability (Zaccaro 2018) 🟢. Around six breaths a minute is the sweet spot ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 16 Contemplative Practice]]).

**Exercise, with timing caveats** ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 13 Exercise, Sleep Pressure and Naps]]). Regular training lowers how strongly cortisol reacts in general 🟡. A hard session late in the evening raises it for a while ✅.

**Not: solving problems late in the evening, news, email, or arguments.** These push cortisol up however dim the screen is ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 17 External Environment]]).

## WHAT TO HOLD ONTO

- Cortisol follows the body clock first. The **slope** matters more than the level.
- "Tired and wired" is a flattened curve: a weak morning rise and a high evening level.
- The alert 3am waking is often blamed on an early burst of cortisol (believable, unproven). Sugary dinners and alcohol cause the same feeling by different routes.
- Cortisol may add to night urine separately from vasopressin: a theory, not a measured effect.
- Morning light sharpens the morning peak, which probably steepens the evening fall. It helps cortisol, not just the clock.
- Cortisol needs time to fall. Sixty quiet minutes before bed is something the body needs, not a luxury.
- Get the day out of your head before bed, on paper or in prayer, not in the dark.

**The point to remember: a steep cortisol curve, high in the morning and low at night, is what lets you sleep; morning light and a quiet last hour keep it steep.**
