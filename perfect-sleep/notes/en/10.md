# PERFECT SLEEP — 10 Stress and Cortisol

*The other major output of the clock, and a leading cause of the 3am alert waking.*

## The normal curve

Cortisol is not a stress hormone that happens to have a rhythm. It is **a circadian hormone that also responds to stress**, and the rhythm is the larger part of the story.

- **Nadir around midnight.** Cortisol should be at its lowest around sleep onset ✅ (Buckley 2005). This quiet period coincides with the deepest sleep of the night, and an active HPA axis in the evening goes with lighter, more broken sleep (Vgontzas 2001) 🟢 — whether the low cortisol *permits* depth is 🔴 interpretation.
- **Rises through the second half of the night**, preparing the body for waking.
- **Cortisol awakening response** — a sharp rise of roughly 50–160% in the 30–45 minutes after waking (Pruessner 1997; Clow 2004) ✅. This is a distinct, measurable event (Stalder 2016). That it is "what makes waking feel like waking" is 🔴 interpretation.
- **Declines across the day** to the midnight nadir again.

The important property is the **slope**: a steep drop from morning peak to midnight trough. A steep slope is the healthy pattern. Flatter slopes are associated with poorer mental and physical health across 80 studies (Adam 2017) 🟢 — an association, small in size, and not proof that the slope matters more than the average level.

## What goes wrong

**Chronic stress flattens the curve.** Not simply "raises cortisol" — a common pattern is a blunted morning rise and an elevated evening level. 🟢 The pattern depends on the stressor and its timing: cortisol is high at stressor onset and falls with time, and uncontrollable or traumatic stressors produce a *high and flat* profile — more hormone *and* less amplitude (Miller 2007). The result is feeling unrefreshed on waking and wired at bedtime. **Tired and wired is a slope problem.**

**Evening elevation blocks sleep onset.** Cortisol is alerting. If it has not fallen by bedtime, sleep pressure and clock signal are fighting an active wake signal. 🟢 People with chronic insomnia secrete more ACTH and cortisol, most in the evening and first half of the night (Vgontzas 2001).

**The 3am waking.** A middle-of-the-night waking — fully alert, sometimes with a racing heart, and unable to return to sleep — is frequently HPA-axis activation — a cortisol pulse arriving too early and too large. It feels like anxiety because the chemistry overlaps. It is often misread as a psychological event when the driver is physiological timing. 🔴 Interpretation: plausible and widely repeated, but not directly shown — cortisol pulses also *follow* awakenings, so the direction is not settled.

Two other things produce the same 3am signature and are worth separating: **counter-regulatory adrenaline after a sugary dinner** ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 07 Food]]) 🟡 (plausible, little direct evidence) and **alcohol metabolising out** ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 07 Food]]) ✅. All three feel identical from inside.

## The bidirectional loop

Sleep loss raises cortisol: after one night of partial or total sleep loss, evening cortisol was 37–45% higher the next day and its evening fall started at least an hour later (Leproult 1997) ✅; a week of 4-hour nights produced the same pattern (Spiegel 1999). Elevated cortisol impairs sleep 🟢. This is one of the tighter self-sustaining loops in the series.

It also connects directly to the metabolic picture ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 12 Metabolic Status and Sleep]]): cortisol drives gluconeogenesis, raises blood glucose, worsens insulin resistance and promotes visceral fat deposition ✅ (clearest at sustained high levels). Where insulin resistance is already the upstream driver of the wider health trajectory, the sleep–cortisol loop is not a side issue.

## Cortisol and night urine

A less-known link, and a hypothesis. Cortisol influences renal water and sodium handling, and glucocorticoids are needed for normal water excretion independently of vasopressin ✅. That a flattened curve with elevated night cortisol therefore adds to nocturia, by a route separate from everything in [[PERFECT SLEEP — 05 Night Fluid]], is 🔴 unverified — no study was found that measures it directly.

## What actually lowers evening cortisol

**Morning light.** The cortisol awakening response is anchored by the same clock that light sets ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 04 The Clock]]). Bright light (800 lux) after waking raised morning cortisol by about a further 35%, while the same light in the late evening had no effect (Scheer 1999) ✅. That a sharper morning peak then produces a steeper decline into the evening is 🔴 inference. **Morning light is plausibly an evening-cortisol intervention.**

**A genuine wind-down.** Cortisol needs a runway to fall. Sixty minutes of low-stimulation before bed does more than any supplement in [[PERFECT SLEEP — 08 Substances and Stack]]. The descent is a physiological process with a duration. 🔴 The sixty-minute figure and the comparison with supplements are practical judgement, not trial results.

**Offloading rumination.** Lying in bed rehearsing the day is a cortisol stimulus. Writing the day down, or the Examen ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 16 Contemplative Practice]]), moves the processing out of the bed and out of the dark. 🟢 In a sleep-lab study, five minutes writing a specific to-do list at bedtime led to faster sleep onset than writing about completed tasks (Scullin 2018).

**Slow breathing.** Extended exhalation increases vagal tone and lowers sympathetic drive, measurable within minutes as higher heart rate variability (Zaccaro 2018) 🟢. Around six breaths per minute is the resonance point ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 16 Contemplative Practice]]).

**Exercise — with timing caveats** ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 13 Exercise, Sleep Pressure and Naps]]). Regular training lowers baseline cortisol reactivity 🟡. A hard session late in the evening raises it acutely ✅.

**Not: late-evening problem-solving, news, email, or confrontational content.** These are cortisol inputs regardless of screen brightness ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 17 External Environment]]).

## WHAT TO HOLD ONTO

- Cortisol is a circadian hormone first. The **slope** matters more than the level.
- "Tired and wired" is a flattened curve: blunted morning rise, elevated evening.
- The 3am alert waking is often attributed to an early cortisol pulse (plausible, unproven). Sugary dinners and alcohol produce an identical feeling by different routes.
- Cortisol may contribute to night urine independently of vasopressin — a hypothesis, not a measured effect.
- Morning light sharpens the morning peak, which plausibly steepens the evening fall. It is a cortisol intervention, not just a clock one.
- Cortisol needs a runway. Sixty quiet minutes before bed is a physiological requirement, not a luxury.
- Get the day out of the head before bed — on paper or in prayer, not in the dark.
