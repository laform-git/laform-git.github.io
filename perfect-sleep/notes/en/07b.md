# PERFECT SLEEP — 07B Late Dinner and Alcohol Before Bed

*Companion to [[PERFECT SLEEP — 07 Food]]. That note covers what to eat. This one covers **when** — the hours between the last bite and lights-out — and what a heavy meal or a drink does to the night.*

> **The short version, in numbers**
>
> - The **same dinner at 10pm instead of 6pm** raised peak blood sugar by about **18%** and cut overnight fat burning by about **10%**.
> - Going to bed **within 3 hours of dinner**: about **7 times** the odds of acid reflux.
> - **Moderate drinking (about two drinks)** cut the restorative quality of sleep, measured by overnight heart-rate recovery, by about **a quarter**. Heavy drinking: about **40%**.
> - **Blood pressure that fails to dip at night** is linked to clearly higher heart attack and stroke risk.
> - Identical food eaten **four hours later** made people hungrier the next day and burn fewer calories.

## The body's night shift

By late evening the body is preparing to fast. Melatonin rises, insulin sensitivity is falling, core temperature should be dropping, and the kidneys are getting ready to make less urine. A large meal at that hour forces the gut, pancreas and liver to work a full shift at exactly the time the rest of the body is shutting down. Sleep becomes a digestion shift.

## Why the same meal does more damage at night

- **Insulin sensitivity falls through the day.** An identical meal eaten in the evening produces a bigger, longer blood-sugar rise than the same meal at midday. Strong evidence, replicated in many controlled studies.
- **Late dinner study (Gu, 2020):** dinner at 10pm versus 6pm, same food, same bedtime — peak glucose about 18% higher, and overnight fat burning about 10% lower. Controlled study.
- **Melatonin clashes with food.** Melatonin signals the pancreas to release less insulin. Eating while melatonin is high means eating with the brakes on. In Marta Garaulet's controlled studies, a late dinner worsened glucose tolerance, most of all in people carrying a common variant of the melatonin receptor gene (MTNR1B). Strong evidence for the effect; the gene interaction is moderate.

## Insulin at bedtime

- A heavy or high-carbohydrate dinner leaves **insulin and glucose high as sleep begins**. High glucose suppresses growth hormone, and the largest growth-hormone pulse of the day rides on the first deep sleep ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 11 Hormones and Sleep]]). Moderate evidence.
- The rise is followed by a **counter-regulatory dip** a few hours later, corrected with adrenaline and cortisol — the alert, racing-heart 3am waking described in [[PERFECT SLEEP — 07 Food]]. Larger in insulin resistance.
- **Insulin makes the kidney hold sodium**, and sodium holds water ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 12 Metabolic Status and Sleep]]). Strong evidence for the mechanism.
- **Honest limit:** direct effects of late meals on sleep stages are less consistent than the metabolic effects. Some studies show lighter, more fragmented sleep; others show little change. The metabolic case is the proven one.

## Blood pressure: the night dip

- Blood pressure should fall **10–20%** during sleep. People whose pressure does not dip ("non-dippers") have clearly higher rates of heart attack, stroke and kidney damage. Strong evidence, large observational studies.
- Things that blunt the dip: late heavy and salty meals, alcohol, poor or broken sleep, sleep apnea, and insulin-driven sodium retention. Moderate evidence for meal timing specifically; strong for salt, alcohol and apnea.

## Cravings and weight

- **Vujović, 2022 (*Cell Metabolism*):** identical meals shifted four hours later doubled the odds of feeling hungry the next day, lowered leptin (the fullness hormone) across the day, reduced calories burned, and switched on fat-storage genes in fat tissue. Controlled study.
- Late eaters in weight-loss programmes lost less weight than early eaters on similar diets. Moderate evidence.
- Short or broken sleep adds to it: ghrelin (hunger) up, leptin down, more craving for sugar the next day. Strong evidence.

## Reflux

- Going to bed within 3 hours of dinner was associated with about **7 times the odds** of reflux compared with waiting 4 hours or more (Fujiwara, 2005). Observational.
- Lying flat with a full stomach lets acid reach the oesophagus. Night reflux can fragment sleep without a full waking ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 15 GERD and Sleep]]).

## Alcohol before bed

### Vasopressin: the hormone alcohol switches off

**Vasopressin** (also called antidiuretic hormone) is made in the brain and released from the pituitary, with a rise at night. It tells the kidneys to **hold on to water**, so the body makes less, more concentrated urine and can sleep through. Strong evidence.

**Alcohol blocks vasopressin release** at the pituitary. Without it, the kidneys keep producing dilute urine all night — more volume, more trips to the bathroom, more broken sleep. In someone already waking to urinate, this stacks directly on the existing problem ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 05 Night Fluid]]). Strong evidence.

### What else alcohol does to the night

- **Sedation first, rebound later.** Falling asleep is faster, but as alcohol is metabolised the second half of the night fragments, with more awakenings. Strong evidence.
- **REM suppressed** early in the night, then crowding back late. Strong evidence. Mechanism in [[PERFECT SLEEP — 08A Sedative Mechanisms THC, Alcohol and CBD]].
- **Heart rate stays higher** and heart-rate variability lower overnight — the body recovers less. Moderate drinking cut recovery by about a quarter, heavy drinking by about 40% (Pietiä, 2018). Large observational study.
- **Airway relaxes:** more snoring, worse apnea. Strong evidence.
- There is **no dose that improves sleep**.

## How to use the window well

1. **Finish eating 3 hours before bed.** One rule, four benefits: blood sugar, reflux, night urine and core temperature.
2. **Make dinner moderate.** Move the heavier protein serving and the salt to lunch.
3. **Walk 10–15 minutes after dinner.** Light walking after a meal measurably flattens the blood-sugar rise. Strong evidence.
4. **Stay upright** for the first couple of hours.
5. **Taper fluids** in the last 2–3 hours; drink freely earlier in the day.
6. **Alcohol: none in the evening.** If at all, small and at least 3 hours before bed.
7. **Dim the lights** in the same window ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 04B Light and Temperature]]).
8. **Truly hungry later?** A small protein-based snack beats going to bed hungry.

## Example case

An adult with insulin resistance who eats a large dinner at 9pm, has a glass of wine, and goes to bed at 10:30. Blood sugar is still high at lights-out, insulin is holding sodium, the wine has switched off vasopressin, and the stomach is full while lying flat. Result: a 3am adrenaline waking, two or three bathroom trips, some reflux, and a hungrier next day. Moving dinner to 6:30, walking after it, and dropping the evening drink addresses every one of those mechanisms at once.

## WHAT TO HOLD ONTO

- At night the body is preparing to fast. A late heavy dinner turns sleep into a digestion shift.
- Same meal, later hour: higher blood sugar, less fat burned, hungrier tomorrow.
- Melatonin and food clash: high melatonin means less insulin.
- Blood pressure should dip at night. Late heavy meals and alcohol blunt the dip.
- Alcohol switches off vasopressin, so the kidneys make urine all night.
- Three hours from last bite to bed, a short walk after dinner, no evening alcohol.
