# PERFECT SLEEP — 07B Late Dinner and Alcohol Before Bed

*Companion to [[PERFECT SLEEP — 07 Food]]. That note covers what to eat. This one covers **when** — the hours between the last bite and lights-out — and what a heavy meal or a drink does to the night.*

> **The short version, in numbers**
>
> - The **same dinner at 10pm instead of 6pm** raised peak blood sugar by about **18%** and cut overnight fat burning by about **10%**.
> - Going to bed **within 3 hours of dinner**: about **7 times** the odds of acid reflux.
> - **Moderate drinking (about two drinks)** cut the restorative quality of sleep, measured by overnight heart-rate recovery, by about **a quarter**. Heavy drinking: about **40%**.
> - **Blood pressure that fails to dip at night** is linked to clearly higher heart attack and stroke risk.
> - Identical food eaten **four hours later** made people hungrier the next day and burn fewer calories.

**In plain words:** by late evening the body is getting ready to rest and fast. A big dinner then makes the gut, pancreas and liver work while the rest of the body is shutting down, so blood sugar stays higher, less fat is burned, blood pressure dips less, reflux is more likely, and you wake hungrier the next evening. Alcohol at night switches off the hormone that holds back urine. The fix: finish eating three hours before bed, walk after dinner, and skip evening alcohol.

**Words in this note:**
- **REM**: rapid eye movement sleep, the dreaming stage

## The body's night shift

By late evening the body is getting ready to go without food. Melatonin (the night-time sleep hormone) rises, the body responds less and less to insulin (the hormone that moves sugar out of the blood), body temperature should be dropping, and the kidneys are getting ready to make less urine. A large meal at that hour forces the gut, pancreas and liver to work a full shift at exactly the time the rest of the body is shutting down. Sleep becomes a digestion shift.

## Why the same meal does more damage at night

- **The body responds less to insulin as the day goes on.** The same meal eaten in the evening causes a bigger, longer rise in blood sugar than at midday. Strong evidence, repeated in many controlled studies.
- **Late dinner study (Gu, 2020):** dinner at 10pm versus 6pm, same food, same bedtime. The highest blood sugar was about 18% higher, and fat burning overnight was about 10% lower. Controlled study.
- **Melatonin and food clash.** Melatonin tells the pancreas to release less insulin. Eating while melatonin is high means eating with the brakes on. In Marta Garaulet's controlled studies, a late dinner made the body handle sugar worse, most of all in people with a common variant of the melatonin receptor gene (MTNR1B). Strong evidence for the effect; moderate for the role of the gene.

## Insulin at bedtime

- A heavy dinner, or one high in carbohydrate, leaves **insulin and blood sugar high as sleep begins**. High blood sugar holds back growth hormone, and the biggest growth-hormone release of the day comes with the first deep sleep ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 11 Hormones and Sleep]]). Moderate evidence.
- The rise is followed by a **dip** a few hours later, which the body corrects with adrenaline and cortisol (stress hormones): the alert, racing-heart 3am waking described in [[PERFECT SLEEP — 07 Food]]. It is bigger in insulin resistance (when the body responds poorly to insulin).
- **Insulin makes the kidney hold on to salt**, and salt holds water ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 12 Metabolic Status and Sleep]]). Strong evidence for the mechanism.
- **Honest limit:** the direct effects of late meals on the sleep stages are less consistent than the effects on metabolism. Some studies show lighter, more broken sleep; others show little change. The case for metabolism is the proven one.

## Blood pressure: the night dip

- Blood pressure should fall **10–20%** during sleep. People whose pressure does not dip ("non-dippers") have clearly higher rates of heart attack, stroke and kidney damage. Strong evidence, from large studies that follow people over time.
- Things that weaken the dip: late, heavy and salty meals, alcohol, poor or broken sleep, sleep apnea (breathing that stops and starts during sleep), and salt held by the kidneys because of insulin. Moderate evidence for meal timing in particular; strong for salt, alcohol and apnea.

## Cravings and weight

- **Vujović, 2022 (*Cell Metabolism*):** the same meals moved four hours later doubled the odds of feeling hungry the next day, lowered leptin (the fullness hormone) throughout the day, reduced the calories burned, and switched on fat-storing genes in fat tissue. Controlled study.
- In weight-loss programmes, late eaters lost less weight than early eaters on similar diets. Moderate evidence.
- Short or broken sleep adds to it: ghrelin (the hunger hormone) up, leptin down, more craving for sugar the next day. Strong evidence.

## The late-eating loop: no appetite in the morning, starving at night

- **Why there is no appetite at breakfast.** At night insulin works less well and digestion slows, so a late dinner is still being handled hours later. Blood sugar and insulin stay up longer, and you wake with fuel still in the system. Breakfast does not appeal. Moderate evidence.
- **Where the hunger goes.** The extra hunger shows up later in the day, not in the morning. In the Vujović study, late eating lowered leptin across 24 hours and raised ghrelin compared with leptin. Hunger and the appetite for starchy and salty food were higher, especially later in the day. Controlled study.
- **The loop.** Skip breakfast → eat little early → very hungry by evening → big late dinner → wake with no appetite → repeat. Each turn pushes the eating clock later. 🔴 Interpretation built on the findings above.
- **Breaking it:** move dinner earlier and make it lighter for a few days. Morning appetite usually comes back once the night fast is real.

*Plain version: think of the body as a factory. A 6pm dinner gets handled by the day workers, the place is tidy by bedtime, and the night crew can do repairs and burn stored fat, so you wake up hungry. A 10pm dinner arrives after the day workers have gone home. The sleepy night crew handles it slowly, sugar hangs around all night, and you wake up full. The next day the "I'm full" messenger whispers and the "I'm hungry" messenger shouts, mostly in the evening, so you eat late again.*

## Reflux

- Going to bed within 3 hours of dinner went with about **7 times the odds** of reflux (stomach acid coming up) compared with waiting 4 hours or more (Fujiwara, 2005). Observational (a study that looks for links, which cannot prove cause).
- Lying flat with a full stomach lets acid reach the oesophagus (the food pipe). Night reflux can break up sleep without fully waking you ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 15 GERD and Sleep]]).

## Alcohol before bed

### Vasopressin: the hormone alcohol switches off

**Vasopressin** (also called antidiuretic hormone) is made in the brain and released from the pituitary gland, with a rise at night. It tells the kidneys to **hold on to water**, so the body makes less, more concentrated urine and can sleep through. Strong evidence.

**Alcohol blocks the release of vasopressin** at the pituitary. Without it, the kidneys keep making watery urine all night: more of it, more trips to the bathroom, more broken sleep. In someone who already wakes to urinate, this adds straight on top of the existing problem ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 05 Night Fluid]]). Strong evidence.

### What else alcohol does to the night

- **Sedation first, rebound later.** You fall asleep faster, but as the body breaks the alcohol down, the second half of the night breaks up, with more wakings. Strong evidence.
- **REM (dream sleep) is held back** early in the night, then crowds back late. Strong evidence. How this works is in [[PERFECT SLEEP — 08A Sedative Mechanisms THC, Alcohol and CBD]].
- **The heart rate stays higher** and heart-rate variability lower overnight, which means the body recovers less. Moderate drinking cut recovery by about a quarter, heavy drinking by about 40% (Pietiä, 2018). Large observational study.
- **The airway relaxes:** more snoring, worse apnea. Strong evidence.
- There is **no amount that improves sleep**.

## How to use the window well

1. **Finish eating 3 hours before bed.** One rule, four benefits: blood sugar, reflux, night urine and body temperature.
2. **Keep dinner moderate.** Move the bigger protein portion and the salt to lunch.
3. **Walk 10–15 minutes after dinner.** A light walk after a meal clearly flattens the rise in blood sugar. Strong evidence.
4. **Stay upright** for the first couple of hours.
5. **Drink less** in the last 2–3 hours; drink freely earlier in the day.
6. **Alcohol: none in the evening.** If at all, a small amount and at least 3 hours before bed.
7. **Dim the lights** in the same window ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 04B Light and Temperature]]).
8. **Truly hungry later?** A small snack based on protein beats going to bed hungry.

## Example case

An adult with insulin resistance who eats a big dinner at 9pm, has a glass of wine, and goes to bed at 10:30. Blood sugar is still high at lights-out, insulin is holding on to salt, the wine has switched off vasopressin, and the stomach is full while lying flat. The result: a 3am adrenaline waking, two or three bathroom trips, some reflux, and a hungrier next day. Moving dinner to 6:30, walking after it, and dropping the evening drink deals with every one of those at once.

## WHAT TO HOLD ONTO

- At night the body is getting ready to fast. A late, heavy dinner turns sleep into a digestion shift.
- Same meal, later hour: higher blood sugar, less fat burned, hungrier tomorrow.
- Melatonin and food clash: high melatonin means less insulin.
- Blood pressure should dip at night. Late heavy meals and alcohol weaken the dip.
- Alcohol switches off vasopressin, so the kidneys make urine all night.
- Three hours from last bite to bed, a short walk after dinner, no alcohol in the evening.

**The point to remember: eat early and moderately, walk after dinner, and leave alcohol out of the evening so the night can be for sleep.**
