# PERFECT SLEEP — 18 Behaviour in Bed

*What happens in the bed, and what the bed comes to mean. Directly relevant for anyone waking several times a night.*

**In plain words:** after months of lying awake, the bed itself can start to mean "awake," and that habit can outlast the problem that caused it, such as an enlarged prostate. The fix is to make the bed mean sleep again: use it only for sleep, get up if you are awake for about twenty minutes, hide the clock, and keep the same wake time. On bathroom trips, use dim red light, no phone, no clock. Trying hard to sleep backfires. If broken sleep continues, CBT-I is the right next step, not sleeping pills.

**Words in this note:**
- **AASM**: American Academy of Sleep Medicine
- **ACG**: American College of Gastroenterology
- **CBT-I**: cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia

## Conditioned insomnia — how a bladder problem becomes a sleep problem

The most important idea in this note, and the one that explains why nocturia (waking at night to urinate) can outlive its cause.

The bed is a **learned cue**. Lying awake in it again and again, frustrated, checking the clock, counting the hours left, trains the brain to link the bed with being awake. Over months that link becomes automatic: getting into bed causes a small jolt of alertness instead of settling you.

🟢 This is truly learned, not imagined. This "conditioned arousal" is one of the "factors that keep it going" in the standard model of long-term insomnia (Spielman 1987). It is why **treating the original cause does not always fix the sleep.** Someone can have their prostate problem fixed and still sleep badly, because the link outlived the mechanism that built it.

**The risk with long-standing nocturia.** Months or years of several wakings a night is a very thorough training schedule. As a mechanical cause clears up, this is the factor most likely to be left behind, holding the problem in place (🔴 a reasoned prediction, not a measured one). It is worth dealing with early, before it becomes the only cause left.

## Stimulus control — the fix

The treatment undoes the learning by making the bed mean sleep again. The rules are simple; sticking to them is the hard part. 🟢 The AASM (American Academy of Sleep Medicine) conditionally recommends stimulus control as a treatment on its own for long-term insomnia (Edinger 2021).

1. **The bed is for sleep (and sex) only, and only when sleepy.** No work, no phone, no television, no lying there awake.
2. **The 20-minute rule.** Awake for more than roughly twenty minutes? Get up. Sit somewhere else, in dim light, doing something dull. Go back when you feel sleepy, not when the clock says to (AASM 2025).
3. **Do not watch the clock.** Turn it away. Watching the clock turns a waking into a maths problem, and maths wakes you up. 🟢 In experiments, people told to watch a clock while trying to fall asleep worried more, took longer to fall asleep and thought it took longer than it did (Tang 2007).
4. **The same wake time whatever the night was like** ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 04 The Clock]]). This is the anchor that makes the rest work.
5. **No naps while rebuilding the link** ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 13 Exercise, Sleep Pressure and Naps]]).

**Do not count the twenty minutes.** Counting means watching the clock, which breaks rule 3. It is a rough guess: "this is taking a while and I am getting frustrated" is the real signal.

## Applying this to nocturia specifically

The standard rules need adjusting, because these wakings have a physical cause and a real reason to leave the bed. 🟡 Behavioural treatment for insomnia also works less well when there is nocturia. In a randomised trial of a short behavioural treatment in older adults, the sleep gains were smaller in those who woke to urinate (Tyagi 2014a), although the same treatment reduced the number of night-time bathroom trips (Tyagi 2014b).

**The goal is to stop the bathroom trip from turning into a real waking.**

- **As little light as possible**: a red light of 5–10 lumens ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 17 External Environment]]).
- **No phone.** Checking it brings light, content and alertness all at once, and is probably the biggest cost you can control in a broken night (🔴 a judgement, not a measured ranking).
- **No clock.** Knowing it is 3:40 turns a two-minute event into twenty minutes of sums.
- **Do not go over the problem.** Lying back down thinking "third time tonight, tomorrow is ruined" wakes you up and trains the wrong link once more.
- **Drift back to sleep without effort.** Slow breathing with a long breath out, or a short repeated prayer ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 16 Contemplative Practice]]). Both are likely to work here exactly because there is nothing to achieve and no falling asleep to check on (🔴 reasoned from the mechanism; not tested specifically after night wakings).
- **If still awake after roughly twenty minutes, get up.** The same rule applies; staying in bed frustrated is what trains the wrong link.

**The target is how long it takes to get back to sleep, not the count** ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 02 Nocturia and Broken Sleep]]). Three wakings with two-minute returns is a manageable night. Three wakings with twenty-five-minute returns is a ruined one. The count is the same, and only one of those numbers can be controlled.

## Sleep effort — the paradox

**Trying to sleep stops sleep.** 🟢 This "sleep effort" is a well-established part of how insomnia keeps going (Espie 2006). Falling asleep needs you to stop checking, and checking whether sleep is coming is the opposite. That is why the harder someone tries on a bad night, the worse it gets.

The same trap catches the fixes. A relaxation practice done in bed *in order to* fall asleep turns into a test and stops working ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 16 Contemplative Practice]]). It also catches sleep trackers: checking a sleep score in the morning and then aiming at it the next night turns sleep into a task with a grade. The pattern is common enough to have a name, **orthosomnia** (an unhealthy focus on perfect sleep data) (Baron 2017) ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 19 Measuring It]]).

## Time in bed versus time asleep

A surprising point. When sleep is poor, the instinct is to spend **more** time in bed to catch up. This usually makes it worse: it spreads sleep pressure over more hours ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 13 Exercise, Sleep Pressure and Naps]]) and adds more time lying awake in bed, which strengthens the wrong link.

The medical version of this, sleep restriction therapy, deliberately **shortens** time in bed to make sleep more solid, then lengthens it bit by bit. 🟢 The AASM conditionally recommends it as a treatment on its own (Edinger 2021). It works well but is demanding, is not advised for people with conditions such as bipolar disorder or seizures (AASM 2025), and should be done with a clinician.

The rule you can use without formal therapy: **do not stay longer in bed to make up for a bad night.** Keep the same wake time.

## Position

- **Left side**: 🟢 reduces reflux at night (stomach acid coming up) ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 15 GERD and Sleep]]). In a trial with a fake-device comparison group, a wearable device that discouraged sleeping on the right side nearly doubled the share whose symptoms improved (44% vs 24%) (Schuitenmaker 2022). 🔴 For the brain's nightly cleaning ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 03 Glymphatic Clearance]]), the evidence is a rat study in which lying on the side in general, not the left side in particular, beat lying on the back or front (Lee 2015). For keeping the airway open ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 14 Sleep-Disordered Breathing]]), either side beats the back. One strong reason for the left in particular, and two for side sleeping in general.
- **Right side**: makes reflux worse in particular.
- **On the back**: worse for the airway and gives no slope against reflux.
- **Head of the bed raised six to eight inches**: for reflux (🟢 the ACG conditionally recommends raising the head of the bed for night-time reflux symptoms; Katz 2022); blocks or risers under the legs, not extra pillows ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 15 GERD and Sleep]]).

## Breathing

**Breathing through the nose** is better: it filters and moistens the air and adds nitric oxide, which is made in the sinuses and breathed in with each breath, where it helps the lungs take up oxygen (Lundberg 2008). 🟡 Breathing through the mouth dries the airway, makes snoring worse, and goes with more broken sleep.

A blocked nose is worth treating if present, since it forces mouth breathing whatever you intend. 🟡 Taping the mouth shut is popular and poorly supported: a systematic review found only 10 small studies, most showing no benefit, and warned of a risk of harm, including suffocation when the nose is blocked (Rhee 2025). It should not be used by anyone with untreated apnea or a blocked nose.

## Where this becomes a clinical matter

**Cognitive behavioural therapy for insomnia (CBT-I)** is the first treatment for long-term insomnia. ✅ It is recommended as the first treatment by the American College of Physicians (Qaseem 2016) and strongly recommended by the AASM (Edinger 2021), ahead of medicine. In trials comparing them directly, its benefits last longer than those of sleeping pills (Mitchell 2012), with no rebound. It is basically the contents of this note plus sleep restriction and a thinking part that works on worries and unhelpful beliefs about sleep, given step by step over several weeks.

If broken sleep continues after the mechanical cause is fixed, **CBT-I is the right next step, not a sleeping pill** ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 09 Sleep Medications and Aids]]). Worth knowing before the question comes up, along with the caveat that it tends to help less while nocturia is still active (Tyagi 2014a).

## WHAT TO HOLD ONTO

- The bed is a learned cue. A year of lying awake teaches it to mean being awake, and that outlives the cause that built it.
- This is the factor most likely to keep the problem going after a prostate problem is fixed. Deal with it early.
- Awake for more than about twenty minutes: get up. Guess the time; do not check the clock.
- On a bathroom trip: no phone, no clock, as little red light as possible, no going over the problem.
- The time it takes to get back to sleep is the number you can control. The count mostly is not.
- Trying to sleep stops sleep. The same trap catches relaxation practices and tracker scores.
- Do not stay longer in bed after a bad night. Keep the same wake time.
- Left side, head of the bed raised, nose not mouth.
- If broken sleep outlives the prostate problem, the answer is CBT-I, not a sleeping pill.

**The point to remember: teach your bed to mean sleep again, and keep every night waking dark, short and calm.**
