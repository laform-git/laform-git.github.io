# PERFECT SLEEP — 18 Behaviour in Bed

*What happens in the bed, and what the bed comes to mean. Directly relevant for anyone waking several times a night.*

## Conditioned insomnia — how a bladder problem becomes a sleep problem

The most important idea in this note, and the one that explains why nocturia can outlive its cause.

The bed is a **learned cue**. Repeatedly lying awake in it — frustrated, checking the clock, calculating remaining hours — trains an association between the bed and wakefulness. Over months that association becomes automatic: getting into bed produces a small arousal response rather than a settling one.

This is genuinely learned, not imagined, and it is why **treating the original cause does not always fix the sleep.** Someone can have their prostate resolved and continue to sleep badly, because the association outlived the mechanism that built it.

**The risk for long-standing nocturia.** Months or years of multiple wakings a night is a very thorough conditioning schedule. As a mechanical cause resolves, this is the factor most likely to be left behind holding the problem in place. It is worth addressing early, before it becomes the sole remaining cause.

## Stimulus control — the fix

The treatment reverses the learning by making the bed mean sleep again. The rules are simple and the compliance is the hard part.

1. **Bed is for sleep only.** No work, no phone, no television, no lying there awake.
2. **The 20-minute rule.** Awake beyond roughly twenty minutes — get up. Sit somewhere else, dim light, something dull. Return when sleepy, not when the clock says to.
3. **Do not watch the clock.** Turn it away. Clock-watching converts a waking into an arithmetic problem, and arithmetic is alerting.
4. **Fixed wake time regardless of the night** ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 04 The Clock]]). This is the anchor that makes the rest work.
5. **No naps while re-establishing the association** ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 13 Exercise, Sleep Pressure and Naps]]).

**Do not count the twenty minutes.** Counting requires clock-watching, which breaks rule 3. It is an estimate — "this is taking a while and I am getting frustrated" is the actual trigger.

## Applying this to nocturia specifically

The standard rules need adaptation, because these wakings have a physical cause and a legitimate reason to leave the bed.

**The goal is to keep the bathroom trip from becoming a waking.**

- **Minimal light** — a red 5–10 lumen fixture ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 17 External Environment]]).
- **No phone.** Checking it introduces light, content and arousal in one action, and is probably the single biggest controllable cost in a fragmented night.
- **No clock.** Knowing it is 3:40 converts a two-minute event into twenty minutes of calculation.
- **Do not rehearse the problem.** Lying back down thinking "third time tonight, tomorrow is ruined" is a cortisol stimulus and a conditioning trial.
- **Return to sleep passively.** Slow breathing with a long exhalation, or a short repetitive prayer ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 16 Contemplative Practice]]) — both work here precisely because there is nothing to achieve and no onset to monitor.
- **If still awake after roughly twenty minutes, get up.** The same rule applies; staying in bed frustrated is what does the conditioning.

**The target is return-to-sleep latency, not the count** ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 02 Nocturia and Broken Sleep]]). Three wakings with two-minute returns is a manageable night. Three wakings with twenty-five-minute returns is a destroyed one. The count is identical, and only one of those numbers is controllable.

## Sleep effort — the paradox

**Trying to sleep prevents sleep.** Sleep onset requires a reduction in monitoring, and monitoring whether sleep is arriving is the opposite. This is why the harder someone tries on a bad night, the worse it gets.

The same trap applies to interventions. A relaxation practice performed in bed *in order to* fall asleep becomes performance-monitored and stops working ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 16 Contemplative Practice]]). It also applies to trackers: checking a sleep score in the morning and then aiming at it the next night converts sleep into a task with a grade — a pattern well enough recognised to have a name, **orthosomnia** ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 19 Measuring It]]).

## Time in bed versus time asleep

A counterintuitive point. When sleep is poor, the instinct is to spend **more** time in bed to catch up. This usually makes it worse: it dilutes sleep pressure across more hours ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 13 Exercise, Sleep Pressure and Naps]]) and adds more awake-in-bed time, which strengthens the wrong association.

The clinical version of this — sleep restriction therapy — deliberately **shortens** time in bed to consolidate sleep, then extends it gradually. It works well but is demanding and belongs with a clinician.

The usable principle without formal therapy: **do not extend time in bed to compensate for a bad night.** Hold the fixed wake time.

## Position

- **Left side** — reduces reflux ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 15 GERD and Sleep]]), favoured for glymphatic clearance ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 03 Glymphatic Clearance]]), better for airway patency ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 14 Sleep-Disordered Breathing]]). Three reasons, one habit.
- **Right side** — worsens reflux specifically.
- **Supine** — worsens airway and offers no reflux gradient.
- **Head of bed raised six to eight inches** — for reflux; blocks or risers under the legs, not extra pillows ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 15 GERD and Sleep]]).

## Breathing

**Nasal breathing** is preferable: it filters, humidifies, and produces nitric oxide which aids gas exchange. Mouth breathing dries the airway, worsens snoring, and is associated with more fragmented sleep.

Nasal obstruction is worth treating if present, since it forces mouth breathing regardless of intention. Mouth taping is popular and under-evidenced; it should not be used by anyone with untreated apnea.

## Where this becomes a clinical matter

**Cognitive behavioural therapy for insomnia (CBT-I)** is the first-line treatment for chronic insomnia — ahead of medication, with better durability and no rebound. It is essentially the contents of this note plus sleep restriction, delivered systematically over several weeks.

If fragmentation persists after the mechanical cause resolves, **CBT-I is the correct next step, not a sleep medication** ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 09 Sleep Medications and Aids]]). Worth knowing before the question arises.

## WHAT TO HOLD ONTO

- The bed is a learned cue. A year of lying awake teaches it to mean wakefulness — and that outlives the cause that built it.
- This is the factor most likely to keep the problem going after a prostate issue resolves. Address it early.
- Awake beyond about twenty minutes: get up. Estimate it — do not check the clock.
- On a bathroom trip: no phone, no clock, minimal red light, no rehearsing the problem.
- Return-to-sleep latency is the controllable number. The count mostly is not.
- Trying to sleep prevents sleep. The same trap catches relaxation practices and tracker scores.
- Do not extend time in bed after a bad night. Hold the fixed wake time.
- Left side, head of bed raised, nose not mouth.
- If fragmentation outlives the prostate, the answer is CBT-I, not a hypnotic.
