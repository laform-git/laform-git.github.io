# PERFECT SLEEP — 18 Behaviour in Bed

*What happens in the bed, and what the bed comes to mean. Directly relevant for anyone waking several times a night.*

## Conditioned insomnia — how a bladder problem becomes a sleep problem

The most important idea in this note, and the one that explains why nocturia can outlive its cause.

The bed is a **learned cue**. Repeatedly lying awake in it — frustrated, checking the clock, calculating remaining hours — trains an association between the bed and wakefulness. Over months that association becomes automatic: getting into bed produces a small arousal response rather than a settling one.

🟢 This is genuinely learned, not imagined — conditioned arousal is one of the "perpetuating factors" in the standard model of chronic insomnia (Spielman 1987) — and it is why **treating the original cause does not always fix the sleep.** Someone can have their prostate resolved and continue to sleep badly, because the association outlived the mechanism that built it.

**The risk for long-standing nocturia.** Months or years of multiple wakings a night is a very thorough conditioning schedule. As a mechanical cause resolves, this is the factor most likely to be left behind holding the problem in place (🔴 a reasoned prediction, not a measured one). It is worth addressing early, before it becomes the sole remaining cause.

## Stimulus control — the fix

The treatment reverses the learning by making the bed mean sleep again. The rules are simple and the compliance is the hard part. 🟢 The AASM conditionally recommends stimulus control as a stand-alone therapy for chronic insomnia (Edinger 2021).

1. **Bed is for sleep (and sex) only, and only when sleepy.** No work, no phone, no television, no lying there awake.
2. **The 20-minute rule.** Awake beyond roughly twenty minutes — get up. Sit somewhere else, dim light, something dull. Return when sleepy, not when the clock says to (AASM 2025).
3. **Do not watch the clock.** Turn it away. Clock-watching converts a waking into an arithmetic problem, and arithmetic is alerting. 🟢 In experiments, people told to watch a clock while trying to fall asleep worried more, took longer to fall asleep and overestimated how long it took (Tang 2007).
4. **Fixed wake time regardless of the night** ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 04 The Clock]]). This is the anchor that makes the rest work.
5. **No naps while re-establishing the association** ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 13 Exercise, Sleep Pressure and Naps]]).

**Do not count the twenty minutes.** Counting requires clock-watching, which breaks rule 3. It is an estimate — "this is taking a while and I am getting frustrated" is the actual trigger.

## Applying this to nocturia specifically

The standard rules need adaptation, because these wakings have a physical cause and a legitimate reason to leave the bed. 🟡 Behavioural insomnia treatment also works less well when nocturia is present: in a randomised trial of brief behavioural treatment in older adults, the sleep gains were smaller in those who woke to void (Tyagi 2014a) — although the same treatment reduced the number of night-time voids (Tyagi 2014b).

**The goal is to keep the bathroom trip from becoming a waking.**

- **Minimal light** — a red 5–10 lumen fixture ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 17 External Environment]]).
- **No phone.** Checking it introduces light, content and arousal in one action, and is probably the single biggest controllable cost in a fragmented night (🔴 a judgement, not a measured ranking).
- **No clock.** Knowing it is 3:40 converts a two-minute event into twenty minutes of calculation.
- **Do not rehearse the problem.** Lying back down thinking "third time tonight, tomorrow is ruined" is an arousal stimulus and a conditioning trial.
- **Return to sleep passively.** Slow breathing with a long exhalation, or a short repetitive prayer ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 16 Contemplative Practice]]) — both are likely to work here precisely because there is nothing to achieve and no onset to monitor (🔴 reasoned from the mechanism; not tested specifically after night wakings).
- **If still awake after roughly twenty minutes, get up.** The same rule applies; staying in bed frustrated is what does the conditioning.

**The target is return-to-sleep latency, not the count** ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 02 Nocturia and Broken Sleep]]). Three wakings with two-minute returns is a manageable night. Three wakings with twenty-five-minute returns is a destroyed one. The count is identical, and only one of those numbers is controllable.

## Sleep effort — the paradox

**Trying to sleep prevents sleep.** 🟢 This "sleep effort" is a well-established part of how insomnia is maintained (Espie 2006). Sleep onset requires a reduction in monitoring, and monitoring whether sleep is arriving is the opposite. This is why the harder someone tries on a bad night, the worse it gets.

The same trap applies to interventions. A relaxation practice performed in bed *in order to* fall asleep becomes performance-monitored and stops working ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 16 Contemplative Practice]]). It also applies to trackers: checking a sleep score in the morning and then aiming at it the next night converts sleep into a task with a grade — a pattern well enough recognised to have a name, **orthosomnia** (Baron 2017) ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 19 Measuring It]]).

## Time in bed versus time asleep

A counterintuitive point. When sleep is poor, the instinct is to spend **more** time in bed to catch up. This usually makes it worse: it dilutes sleep pressure across more hours ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 13 Exercise, Sleep Pressure and Naps]]) and adds more awake-in-bed time, which strengthens the wrong association.

The clinical version of this — sleep restriction therapy — deliberately **shortens** time in bed to consolidate sleep, then extends it gradually. 🟢 The AASM conditionally recommends it as a single therapy (Edinger 2021). It works well but is demanding, is not advised for people with conditions such as bipolar disorder or seizures (AASM 2025), and belongs with a clinician.

The usable principle without formal therapy: **do not extend time in bed to compensate for a bad night.** Hold the fixed wake time.

## Position

- **Left side** — 🟢 reduces night-time reflux ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 15 GERD and Sleep]]): in a sham-controlled trial, a wearable that discouraged right-side sleeping nearly doubled the rate of symptom improvement (44% vs 24%) (Schuitenmaker 2022). 🔴 For glymphatic clearance ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 03 Glymphatic Clearance]]) the evidence is a rat study in which side-lying in general, not the left side specifically, beat lying on the back or front (Lee 2015). For airway patency ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 14 Sleep-Disordered Breathing]]), either side beats the back. One strong reason for the left specifically, and two for side-sleeping in general.
- **Right side** — worsens reflux specifically.
- **Supine** — worsens airway and offers no reflux gradient.
- **Head of bed raised six to eight inches** — for reflux (🟢 the ACG conditionally recommends head-of-bed elevation for night-time reflux symptoms; Katz 2022); blocks or risers under the legs, not extra pillows ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 15 GERD and Sleep]]).

## Breathing

**Nasal breathing** is preferable: it filters, humidifies, and adds nitric oxide — made in the sinuses and inhaled with each breath, where it improves oxygen uptake in the lungs (Lundberg 2008). 🟡 Mouth breathing dries the airway, worsens snoring, and is associated with more fragmented sleep.

Nasal obstruction is worth treating if present, since it forces mouth breathing regardless of intention. 🟡 Mouth taping is popular and under-evidenced: a systematic review found only 10 small studies, most showing no benefit, and flagged a risk of harm, including asphyxiation when the nose is blocked (Rhee 2025). It should not be used by anyone with untreated apnea or nasal obstruction.

## Where this becomes a clinical matter

**Cognitive behavioural therapy for insomnia (CBT-I)** is the first-line treatment for chronic insomnia — ✅ recommended as the initial treatment by the American College of Physicians (Qaseem 2016) and strongly recommended by the AASM (Edinger 2021), ahead of medication. In head-to-head trials its benefits outlast those of sleeping pills (Mitchell 2012), with no rebound. It is essentially the contents of this note plus sleep restriction and a cognitive component that works on worries and unhelpful beliefs about sleep, delivered systematically over several weeks.

If fragmentation persists after the mechanical cause resolves, **CBT-I is the correct next step, not a sleep medication** ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 09 Sleep Medications and Aids]]). Worth knowing before the question arises — along with the caveat that it tends to help less while nocturia is still active (Tyagi 2014a).

## WHAT TO HOLD ONTO

- The bed is a learned cue. A year of lying awake teaches it to mean wakefulness — and that outlives the cause that built it.
- This is the factor most likely to keep the problem going after a prostate issue resolves. Address it early.
- Awake beyond about twenty minutes: get up. Estimate it — do not check the clock.
- On a bathroom trip: no phone, no clock, minimal red light, no rehearsing the problem.
- Return-to-sleep latency is the controllable number. The count mostly is not.
- Trying to sleep prevents sleep. The same trap catches relaxation practices and tracker scores.
- Do not extend time in bed after a bad night. Hold the fixed wake time.
- Left side, head of bed raised, nose not mouth.
- If fragmentation outlives the prostate, the answer is CBT-I, not a hypnotic.
