# PERFECT SLEEP — 11 Hormones and Sleep

*Hormones as sleep factors only. Lab values, dosing and interpretation fall outside this series.*

## Testosterone is made during sleep

The detail that reframes this whole area: **testosterone release is sleep-dependent, not merely sleep-associated.** The major rise occurs during sleep and needs about three hours of normally structured sleep, peaking in the early morning (Wittert 2014) ✅. Corrected: the rise is tied to the first hours of sleep and to the appearance of the first REM episode, not to slow-wave sleep (Luboshitzky 2001).

The consequence is direct. **Fragmented sleep is not a context in which testosterone happens to be low. It is a cause of it.** 🟡 In healthy men, experimental sleep fragmentation delayed and blunted the nocturnal rise, mainly in those who lost their REM periods (Luboshitzky 2001).

The research is less unambiguous than often claimed. In one well-known study, restricting 10 healthy young men to five hours a night for one week lowered daytime testosterone by 10–15% (Leproult 2011) 🟢. But a meta-analysis of 18 studies found that total sleep deprivation (24 hours or more) clearly lowers testosterone, while short-term partial restriction had no significant effect (Su 2021) 🟡. That years of fragmented nights add up to a far larger cumulative effect is 🔴 extrapolation, not measured.

And it runs both ways. Low testosterone is itself associated with poorer sleep quality, which replacement doses can improve (Wittert 2014) 🟡. Another self-sustaining loop.

**Practical implication:** a testosterone value drawn during a period of fragmented sleep is partly reporting the sleep, not only the gonadal axis. This matters for interpreting any lab draw taken during such a period.

## Growth hormone

Released in pulses, tightly coupled to slow-wave sleep — the largest pulse of the day occurs in the first N3 episode of the night ✅. Independently of age, the amount of GH secreted tracks the amount of slow-wave sleep (Van Cauter 2000).

Since N3 is front-loaded ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 01 Sleep Architecture]]), **the first uninterrupted stretch carries the growth hormone pulse**. A 1:00am waking can cut into it 🟡 (plausible from timing; not measured directly). This is a second independent reason, alongside glymphatic clearance ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 03 Glymphatic Clearance]]), to protect the first sleep period specifically.

## The full hormonal cascade of short sleep

Sleep restriction produces a fairly consistent pattern — though not every link is equally solid:

- **Ghrelin up** — the hunger hormone rises.
- **Leptin down** — the satiety signal falls. 🟡 Shown in a small, tightly controlled study (ghrelin +28%, leptin −18%: Spiegel 2004), but a meta-analysis of 41 randomised trials found no strong evidence of a change in average leptin or ghrelin (Zhu 2019).
- Net effect: increased hunger and intake ✅ — about 250–385 extra kcal a day under partial sleep restriction (Zhu 2019; Al Khatib 2017). Appetite for calorie-dense, carbohydrate-rich food rose by 33–45% in the small study (Spiegel 2004), but measured extra intake came mainly from fat, not carbohydrate (Al Khatib 2017).
- **Insulin sensitivity falls** ✅ (Zhu 2019) ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 12 Metabolic Status and Sleep]]).
- **Cortisol curve flattens** ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 10 Stress and Cortisol]]).
- **Testosterone falls** 🟡 (clear with total sleep loss; inconsistent with partial restriction).

This is the chain in concrete terms: **poor sleep → low energy → low testosterone → food craving → no exercise → worse metabolic status → worse sleep.** It is not several separate problems. It is one problem expressing itself in several places — and it runs in reverse when the nights are restored. Testosterone, appetite regulation, insulin sensitivity and energy all improve without being targeted individually. 🔴 The chain is an interpretation linking separate findings. The reversal has partial support: extending sleep by about 1.2 hours a night cut energy intake by about 270 kcal a day in adults with overweight (Tasali 2022) 🟢.

## Testosterone therapy and sleep

Exogenous testosterone has a genuinely two-sided relationship with sleep, and both sides should be known.

- **It can worsen sleep-disordered breathing.** Testosterone therapy is associated with worsening or unmasking of obstructive sleep apnea in some men, particularly at higher doses — high-dose injections in older men shortened sleep by about an hour and added about seven breathing events per hour (Liu 2003) 🟢; at replacement doses the effect appears small and often transient (Wittert 2014). The Endocrine Society lists untreated severe sleep apnea as a reason not to start therapy (Bhasin 2018) ✅ ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 14 Sleep-Disordered Breathing]]). Relevant even where apnea is not currently present.
- **Night sweats** can fragment sleep. 🟡 Corrected: hot flushes are listed but uncommon (about 1%) on testosterone gel labelling; flushing and sweats are more typical of *low or falling* testosterone, for example late in a dosing interval ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 06 Heat and Timing]]).
- **Dosing consistency matters for interpretation.** A gel applied inconsistently produces swinging values that can look like a biological swing but are a dosing-gap artifact; standardising the daily morning dose resolves it.

## DHEA

Commonly taken in the morning for low DHEA-S. Sleep-relevant points only:

- It is a precursor hormone converting to both androgens and estrogens, so effects are indirect and variable.
- Some reports of insomnia or vivid dreams, generally at higher doses 🟡 (sparse evidence).
- Morning dosing is appropriate — it follows the natural DHEA rhythm, which peaks early. An evening dose would be the wrong timing 🔴 (logical, not tested).
- Evidence for DHEA improving sleep directly is weak. When taken, it is for the marker, not for sleep.

## Thyroid

Worth having on the map even when nothing points to it. Both directions disturb sleep: overactive thyroid produces insomnia and night sweats; underactive produces excessive sleepiness, weight gain and fatigue that mimics poor sleep. Fatigue that does not resolve when sleep improves is a reason to check thyroid function.

## Age — stated plainly

This belongs in the series so the target is realistic rather than a twenty-year-old's sleep architecture.

- **Slow-wave sleep declines substantially with age** ✅. In men the steepest fall is from early adulthood to midlife — from about 19% of the night at ages 16–25 to about 3% at 36–50 — after which it levels off while time awake rises by about 28 minutes per decade (Van Cauter 2000). Corrected: earlier versions said the decline continues steadily. Total sleep time falls far less than deep sleep does (Ohayon 2004).
- **The overnight vasopressin surge flattens with age** 🟢 — most healthy older adults in one study lacked the normal night-time rise (Asplund 1991) — an independent contributor to nocturia that has nothing to do with the prostate ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 05 Night Fluid]]).
- **Circadian amplitude decreases**, and phase tends to advance earlier 🟢.
- **Arousal threshold falls** — older sleep is more easily disturbed by the same stimulus ✅; auditory awakening thresholds fall from early adulthood onward, already by the 40s in deep and N2 sleep (Zepelin 1984).

What this does *not* mean: that poor sleep is simply age and must be accepted. Age sets the ceiling. It does not explain a sharp deterioration over months.

## WHAT TO HOLD ONTO

- Testosterone is produced during sleep, especially in the first hours. Broken nights blunt the rise; one week of restriction cut it 10–15% in one small study of young men, though partial restriction is inconsistent across studies.
- The largest growth hormone pulse rides on the first N3 episode. Another reason to protect the first uninterrupted stretch.
- Short sleep raises hunger and adds roughly 250–385 kcal a day of intake. The craving is physiological, not a failure of will — though the leptin/ghrelin part is less consistent than often claimed.
- One problem, four expressions — and it reverses in the same direction it broke.
- Testosterone therapy can worsen sleep-disordered breathing and cause night sweats. Both are sleep issues, not just endocrine ones.
- Age lowers deep sleep and flattens the vasopressin surge. It sets the ceiling; it does not explain a sudden change.
- A testosterone value drawn during fragmented sleep is partly measuring the sleep.
