# PERFECT SLEEP — 11 Hormones and Sleep

*Hormones as sleep factors only. Lab values, dosing and interpretation fall outside this series.*

## Testosterone is made during sleep

The detail that reframes this whole area: **testosterone release is sleep-dependent, not merely sleep-associated.** The major rise occurs during sleep, tied particularly to the first few hours and to slow-wave sleep, peaking in the early morning.

The consequence is direct. **Fragmented sleep is not a context in which testosterone happens to be low. It is a cause of it.**

The research is unambiguous about magnitude: restricting healthy young men to five hours a night for one week produced a 10–15% fall in daytime testosterone. One week. Young men. Someone with years of fragmented nights is operating under a far larger cumulative effect.

And it runs both ways. Low testosterone is itself associated with poorer sleep quality and reduced slow-wave sleep. Another self-sustaining loop.

**Practical implication:** a testosterone value drawn during a period of fragmented sleep is partly reporting the sleep, not only the gonadal axis. This matters for interpreting any lab draw taken during such a period.

## Growth hormone

Released in pulses, tightly coupled to slow-wave sleep — the largest pulse of the day occurs in the first N3 episode of the night.

Since N3 is front-loaded ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 01 Sleep Architecture]]), **the first uninterrupted stretch carries the growth hormone pulse**. A 1:00am waking can cut into it. This is a second independent reason, alongside glymphatic clearance ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 03 Glymphatic Clearance]]), to protect the first sleep period specifically.

## The full hormonal cascade of short sleep

Sleep restriction produces a consistent and well-replicated pattern:

- **Ghrelin up** — the hunger hormone rises.
- **Leptin down** — the satiety signal falls.
- Net effect: increased appetite, with a specific preference for calorie-dense, carbohydrate-rich food.
- **Insulin sensitivity falls** ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 12 Metabolic Status and Sleep]]).
- **Cortisol curve flattens** ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 10 Stress and Cortisol]]).
- **Testosterone falls.**

This is the chain in concrete terms: **poor sleep → low energy → low testosterone → food craving → no exercise → worse metabolic status → worse sleep.** It is not several separate problems. It is one problem expressing itself in several places — and it runs in reverse when the nights are restored. Testosterone, appetite regulation, insulin sensitivity and energy all improve without being targeted individually.

## Testosterone therapy and sleep

Exogenous testosterone has a genuinely two-sided relationship with sleep, and both sides should be known.

- **It can worsen sleep-disordered breathing.** Testosterone therapy is associated with worsening or unmasking of obstructive sleep apnea in some men, particularly at higher doses ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 14 Sleep-Disordered Breathing]]). Relevant even where apnea is not currently present.
- **Night sweats** are a recognised effect and can fragment sleep ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 06 Heat and Timing]]).
- **Dosing consistency matters for interpretation.** A gel applied inconsistently produces swinging values that can look like a biological swing but are a dosing-gap artifact; standardising the daily morning dose resolves it.

## DHEA

Commonly taken in the morning for low DHEA-S. Sleep-relevant points only:

- It is a precursor hormone converting to both androgens and estrogens, so effects are indirect and variable.
- Some reports of insomnia or vivid dreams, generally at higher doses.
- Morning dosing is appropriate — it follows the natural DHEA rhythm, which peaks early. An evening dose would be the wrong timing.
- Evidence for DHEA improving sleep directly is weak. When taken, it is for the marker, not for sleep.

## Thyroid

Worth having on the map even when nothing points to it. Both directions disturb sleep: overactive thyroid produces insomnia and night sweats; underactive produces excessive sleepiness, weight gain and fatigue that mimics poor sleep. Fatigue that does not resolve when sleep improves is a reason to check thyroid function.

## Age — stated plainly

This belongs in the series so the target is realistic rather than a twenty-year-old's sleep architecture.

- **Slow-wave sleep declines substantially with age**, beginning in the twenties and continuing steadily. Total sleep time falls far less than deep sleep does.
- **The overnight vasopressin surge flattens with age** — an independent contributor to nocturia that has nothing to do with the prostate ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 05 Night Fluid]]).
- **Circadian amplitude decreases**, and phase tends to advance earlier.
- **Arousal threshold falls** — older sleep is more easily disturbed by the same stimulus.

What this does *not* mean: that poor sleep is simply age and must be accepted. Age sets the ceiling. It does not explain a sharp deterioration over months.

## WHAT TO HOLD ONTO

- Testosterone is produced during sleep, especially in the first hours. Broken nights lower it directly — 10–15% in one week of restriction in healthy young men.
- The largest growth hormone pulse rides on the first N3 episode. Another reason to protect the first uninterrupted stretch.
- Short sleep raises ghrelin, lowers leptin, and drives craving for dense carbohydrate. The craving is hormonal, not a failure of will.
- One problem, four expressions — and it reverses in the same direction it broke.
- Testosterone therapy can worsen sleep-disordered breathing and cause night sweats. Both are sleep issues, not just endocrine ones.
- Age lowers deep sleep and flattens the vasopressin surge. It sets the ceiling; it does not explain a sudden change.
- A testosterone value drawn during fragmented sleep is partly measuring the sleep.
