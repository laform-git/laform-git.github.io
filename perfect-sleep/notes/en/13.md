# PERFECT SLEEP — 13 Exercise, Sleep Pressure and Naps

*Process S — the homeostatic half of the two-process model — and the movement that feeds it.*

## Sleep pressure: what it actually is

Adenosine is a byproduct of cellular energy use. It accumulates in the brain every hour awake and binds receptors that promote sleepiness. During sleep it is cleared. That accumulation and clearance is **Process S** ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 01 Sleep Architecture]]). 🟢 Adenosine is the best-studied chemical signal of this pressure: in animals it rises in the brain with prolonged waking and falls during recovery sleep (Porkka-Heiskanen 1997).

This is what caffeine acts on — it blocks the adenosine receptor without removing the adenosine. The pressure is still building behind the block. The popular idea that this produces an abrupt "crash" when caffeine wears off is plausible but not well studied. 🔴

Two consequences that are not obvious:

- **Pressure is built mainly by time awake, not by physical activity.** Exercise adds only a small amount of deep sleep (see below), so a physically exhausting day builds little more sleep pressure than a sedentary one. 🟡 Physical tiredness and sleep pressure are different things, which is why someone can be exhausted and still not sleep.
- **Anything that discharges pressure early costs sleep at bedtime.** That is the whole of the nap and lie-in problem below.

## Naps

Not forbidden, but they have a dose and a timing.

**Length.** Under about 20–30 minutes usually stays in N1 and N2 and limits sleep inertia — though a review found that short naps do not always avoid deep sleep or grogginess; it depends on prior sleep loss and time of day (Hilditch 2017). 🟡 Past that, sleep enters N3, and waking out of N3 produces the heavy disoriented state that can last an hour ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 04 The Clock]]). If a longer nap is taken, a full cycle of roughly 90 minutes is better than 45. 🔴 (common advice; little direct evidence)

**Timing.** Early afternoon, roughly 1–3pm, coinciding with the natural circadian dip — the most favourable time in nap research (Lovato 2010). 🟢 A nap after about 4pm discharges too much of the pressure needed for bedtime. 🟡

**The specific risk with fragmented sleep.** In a fragmented-sleep pattern, daytime napping is very tempting and directly reinforces the problem: it reduces the pressure that would otherwise help carry sleep through the night, and it shallows the following night, which lowers the arousal threshold and lets bladder signals break through more easily ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 02 Nocturia and Broken Sleep]]). **When nights are broken, naps are more costly than usual.** 🔴 (reasoning from the two-process model; not tested directly in nocturia)

**Lying in** does the same thing with more force. It discharges pressure *and* shifts the clock later, undoing the morning light work. Weekend lie-ins are a common cause of a failed wake-time shift. 🔴

## Exercise — what it actually does to sleep

**It increases slow-wave sleep slightly.** In meta-analyses the effect of a single session is small (Kredlow 2015); evening sessions added about 1.3 percentage points of slow-wave sleep (Stutz 2019). In people with insomnia, regular exercise improves self-rated sleep quality, though not measured sleep efficiency (Banno 2018). 🟢 Modest, but real, and it acts on precisely the stage that matters most for fragmented sleep ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 01 Sleep Architecture]], [[PERFECT SLEEP — 03 Glymphatic Clearance]]).

**It modestly shortens the time to fall asleep and improves subjective sleep quality** — small-to-medium and moderate effects respectively for regular exercise (Kredlow 2015). 🟢

**Regular training lowers baseline stress reactivity**, which flattens fewer cortisol curves ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 10 Stress and Cortisol]]). 🟡

**It improves insulin sensitivity**, which acts on three of the loops in [[PERFECT SLEEP — 12 Metabolic Status and Sleep]] simultaneously.

**And where nocturia is involved: it clears leg fluid.** Muscle contraction pumps the deep veins, returning pooled fluid to circulation during daylight rather than at 2am ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 05 Night Fluid]]). This may be the single most directly relevant benefit of daytime movement in this entire series. 🔴 The calf-pump mechanism is established physiology, but trials of exercise specifically for nocturia are scarce; the International Continence Society consensus says only that lifestyle measures "may be useful" (Everaert 2019).

## Morning versus evening — the honest answer

The old advice was that evening exercise ruins sleep. The evidence does not support that as stated.

**Evening exercise is largely fine** provided it finishes roughly 60–90 minutes before bed. A meta-analysis of 23 studies found no overall negative effect of evening exercise, and slightly more deep sleep (Stutz 2019). ✅ What may disrupt sleep is **vigorous** exercise ending within about an hour of bedtime (later sleep onset, shorter sleep) 🟢 — core temperature is still elevated when it should be falling ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 04 The Clock]]), and sympathetic arousal has not settled.

**Morning exercise has advantages that are not about sleep physiology at all:**

- Done outdoors, it **doubles as the light anchor** ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 04 The Clock]]). One action, two systems.
- It clears leg fluid at the start of the sedentary day rather than the end.
- Adherence is generally better — fewer things have gone wrong by 7am. 🔴 (plausible; not well studied)

**Afternoon has one genuine physiological advantage:** strength and power peak in the late afternoon, alongside the core temperature peak. Performance is better then.

**Practical resolution for a desk-bound adult with nocturia:** morning outdoor movement for the light and fluid benefits, afternoon for anything performance-oriented, and nothing vigorous inside the final hour.

## The desk problem

Sedentary work is the largest untouched lever in this series. It acts through:

- Leg fluid pooling → overnight excretion ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 05 Night Fluid]])
- Reduced insulin sensitivity ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 12 Metabolic Status and Sleep]])
- Lower total adenosine-independent fatigue, but also lower fitness and higher stress reactivity

**Movement snacks** — short breaks every 45–60 minutes — address the fluid and glycemic pieces in a way one concentrated session does not, because the *duration of continuous sitting* matters, not only the daily total. 🟢 In a randomised crossover trial, 2-minute walks every 20 minutes cut post-meal glucose and insulin by roughly a quarter compared with unbroken sitting (Dunstan 2012); every 45–60 minutes is a practical compromise, not the tested interval.

**Calf-specific work matters disproportionately** for the fluid mechanism: calf raises at the desk, standing, walking. Late-afternoon leg elevation for 20–30 minutes is the passive equivalent and is the highest-yield single item if only one is realistic. 🔴 (a judgement from the mechanism, not a measured result)

## WHAT TO HOLD ONTO

- Sleep pressure is built mainly by hours awake, not by exertion. Physical tiredness is not the same thing.
- Naps under 30 minutes, before 3pm. When nights are already broken, naps cost more than usual.
- Weekend lie-ins are a common cause of a failed wake-time shift — they discharge pressure and move the clock.
- Exercise modestly increases deep sleep and improves how well people feel they slept, including in insomnia.
- Evening exercise is fine if it ends 60–90 minutes before bed. Only vigorous, late sessions hurt.
- Morning outdoor movement does three jobs at once: light anchor, leg-fluid clearance, adherence.
- Continuous sitting matters, not only the daily total. Break it every 45–60 minutes, and use the calves.
