# PERFECT SLEEP — 13 Exercise, Sleep Pressure and Naps

*Process S — the homeostatic half of the two-process model — and the movement that feeds it.*

**In plain words:** the drive to sleep builds with every hour you are awake, not with how hard you work your body. Naps and sleeping in drain that drive, so keep naps short and early, especially when nights are already broken. Exercise helps sleep a little and helps blood sugar a lot; evening exercise is fine if it ends an hour or more before bed. For desk workers, short movement breaks and calf work also clear fluid from the legs before night.

## Sleep pressure: what it actually is

Adenosine is a by-product of the cells using energy. It builds up in the brain with every hour awake and attaches to switches (receptors) that make us sleepy. During sleep it is cleared. That build-up and clearing is **Process S** ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 01 Sleep Architecture]]). 🟢 Adenosine is the best-studied chemical sign of this pressure: in animals it rises in the brain with long periods awake and falls during catch-up sleep (Porkka-Heiskanen 1997).

This is what caffeine acts on. It blocks the adenosine switch without removing the adenosine. The pressure keeps building behind the block. The popular idea that this causes a sudden "crash" when caffeine wears off is believable but not well studied. 🔴

Two results that are not obvious:

- **Sleep pressure is built mainly by time awake, not by physical activity.** Exercise adds only a little deep sleep (see below), so a physically exhausting day builds little more sleep pressure than a day sitting down. 🟡 Physical tiredness and sleep pressure are different things, which is why someone can be exhausted and still not sleep.
- **Anything that drains pressure early costs sleep at bedtime.** That is the whole of the nap and sleeping-in problem below.

## Naps

Not forbidden, but they have a dose and a time.

**Length.** A nap under about 20–30 minutes usually stays in the light stages (N1 and N2) and limits grogginess after waking (sleep inertia). However, a review found that short naps do not always avoid deep sleep or grogginess; it depends on how much sleep you have lost and the time of day (Hilditch 2017). 🟡 Beyond that, sleep goes into deep N3, and waking out of N3 gives the heavy, confused state that can last an hour ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 04 The Clock]]). If you take a longer nap, a full cycle of roughly 90 minutes is better than 45. 🔴 (common advice; little direct evidence)

**Timing.** Early afternoon, roughly 1–3pm, matching the natural dip in the body clock: the best time in nap research (Lovato 2010). 🟢 A nap after about 4pm drains too much of the pressure needed at bedtime. 🟡

**The particular risk when sleep is broken.** When nights are broken, daytime naps are very tempting and they feed the problem directly. They lower the pressure that would otherwise help carry sleep through the night, and they make the next night shallower. That makes waking easier and lets bladder signals break through more easily ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 02 Nocturia and Broken Sleep]]). **When nights are broken, naps cost more than usual.** 🔴 (reasoning from the two-process model; not tested directly in nocturia)

**Sleeping in** does the same thing, more strongly. It drains pressure *and* moves the clock later, undoing the work of the morning light. Sleeping in at weekends is a common reason an earlier wake time fails. 🔴

## Exercise — what it actually does to sleep

**It increases deep slow-wave sleep slightly.** In studies pooling many studies, the effect of a single session is small (Kredlow 2015); evening sessions added about 1.3 percentage points of slow-wave sleep (Stutz 2019). In people with insomnia, regular exercise improves how well they feel they slept, though not the measured share of time in bed spent asleep (Banno 2018). 🟢 Modest but real, and it works on exactly the stage that matters most for broken sleep ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 01 Sleep Architecture]], [[PERFECT SLEEP — 03 Glymphatic Clearance]]).

**It shortens the time to fall asleep a little and improves how well people feel they slept**: small-to-medium and moderate effects for regular exercise (Kredlow 2015). 🟢

**Regular training makes the body react less strongly to stress**, which means fewer flattened cortisol curves ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 10 Stress and Cortisol]]). 🟡

**It improves the body's response to insulin**, which acts on three of the loops in [[PERFECT SLEEP — 12 Metabolic Status and Sleep]] at the same time.

**And where nocturia (waking at night to urinate) is involved: it clears fluid from the legs.** Working muscles pump the deep veins, returning pooled fluid to the blood during the day instead of at 2am ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 05 Night Fluid]]). This may be the single most directly useful benefit of daytime movement in this whole series. 🔴 The calf-pump mechanism is established body science, but trials of exercise specifically for nocturia are scarce; the International Continence Society's expert agreement says only that lifestyle measures "may be useful" (Everaert 2019).

## Morning versus evening — the honest answer

The old advice was that evening exercise ruins sleep. The evidence does not support that as stated.

**Evening exercise is mostly fine** as long as it finishes roughly 60–90 minutes before bed. A study pooling 23 studies found no overall harm from evening exercise, and slightly more deep sleep (Stutz 2019). ✅ What may disturb sleep is **hard** exercise ending within about an hour of bedtime (falling asleep later, shorter sleep) 🟢. Body temperature is still high when it should be falling ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 04 The Clock]]), and the "fight or flight" arousal has not settled.

**Morning exercise has advantages that have nothing to do with sleep science:**

- Done outdoors, it **also gives the clock its morning light** ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 04 The Clock]]). One action, two systems.
- It clears leg fluid at the start of the sitting day instead of the end.
- People usually stick with it better: fewer things have gone wrong by 7am. 🔴 (believable; not well studied)

**The afternoon has one real advantage for the body:** strength and power peak in the late afternoon, along with the body temperature peak. Performance is better then.

**A practical answer for a desk-bound adult with nocturia:** morning outdoor movement for the light and fluid benefits, the afternoon for anything aimed at performance, and nothing hard in the last hour before bed.

## The desk problem

Sitting at work all day is the biggest unused lever in this series. It acts through:

- Fluid pooling in the legs → passed overnight ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 05 Night Fluid]])
- A weaker response to insulin ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 12 Metabolic Status and Sleep]])
- Less tiredness of the kind that has nothing to do with adenosine, but also lower fitness and a stronger reaction to stress

**Movement snacks**, short breaks every 45–60 minutes, deal with the fluid and blood sugar parts in a way that one long session does not, because *how long you sit without a break* matters, not only the daily total. 🟢 In a randomised trial where each person tried both, 2-minute walks every 20 minutes cut blood sugar and insulin after meals by roughly a quarter compared with sitting without a break (Dunstan 2012); every 45–60 minutes is a practical compromise, not the interval tested.

**Calf work matters more than its size suggests** for the fluid mechanism: calf raises at the desk, standing, walking. Raising the legs in the late afternoon for 20–30 minutes is the no-effort version, and is the most rewarding single step if only one is realistic. 🔴 (a judgement from the mechanism, not a measured result)

## WHAT TO HOLD ONTO

- Sleep pressure is built mainly by hours awake, not by effort. Physical tiredness is not the same thing.
- Naps under 30 minutes, before 3pm. When nights are already broken, naps cost more than usual.
- Sleeping in at weekends is a common reason an earlier wake time fails: it drains pressure and moves the clock.
- Exercise slightly increases deep sleep and improves how well people feel they slept, including in insomnia.
- Evening exercise is fine if it ends 60–90 minutes before bed. Only hard, late sessions hurt.
- Moving outdoors in the morning does three jobs at once: light for the clock, clearing leg fluid, and a habit that sticks.
- Unbroken sitting matters, not only the daily total. Break it every 45–60 minutes, and use the calves.

**The point to remember: stay awake and active all day, keep naps short and early, and break up your sitting so the night has nothing to undo.**
