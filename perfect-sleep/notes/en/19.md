# PERFECT SLEEP — 19 Measuring It

*How to know whether any of this worked — and how to avoid measurement becoming its own problem.*

**In plain words:** the single most useful measurement is a three-day bladder diary: write down the time and amount of every trip to the toilet, day and night. It shows whether the body makes too much urine at night or the bladder holds too little, and those need different fixes. Also note each morning roughly how long it took to get back to sleep after each waking. Sleep trackers are good for trends over weeks, not for single nights or sleep stages, and checking scores too closely can itself spoil sleep.

## The one measurement that would change the plan

**The bladder diary.** Everything in [[PERFECT SLEEP — 05 Night Fluid]] rests on an assumption that often goes untested.

For at least three days and nights, write down **the time and the amount** of every trip to urinate over the full 24 hours, day and night, plus the time you went to sleep and the time you got up. ✅ The European Association of Urology strongly recommends a bladder diary for nocturia (waking at night to urinate), kept for at least three days; the days do not have to be in a row, as long as they are typical (EAU 2026). A cheap measuring jug is all the equipment needed.

What it gives you is the **nocturnal polyuria index**: the amount of urine made at night divided by the total amount over 24 hours. The night amount counts every trip during the main sleep period **plus the first trip after getting up**, since that urine was made during sleep (Hashim 2019).

- Above roughly **a third** in an older adult (over about 65; above 20% in younger adults, somewhere in between in middle age) → **nocturnal polyuria.** Too much urine is being made at night. 🟡 These cut-offs are the most widely used, but the International Continence Society notes that they were never properly proven, so treat them as guides rather than hard lines (Hashim 2019; Fine 2017). This is a hormone and fluid problem, and everything in [[PERFECT SLEEP — 05 Night Fluid]] applies: the morning light, the leg fluid, the timing of salt and protein.
- A normal night share but still waking → **a capacity problem.** Urine left behind in the bladder, or a thickened, irritable bladder wall after years of pushing against a blockage. Mechanical, and it is for the urologist. The diary shows this too: if the night's urine is more than the biggest single trip of the day (a "nocturia index" above 1), the bladder simply cannot hold the night's output (Hashim 2019).
- Two other patterns the diary can show: **global polyuria**, too much urine around the clock, more than 40 mL per kg of body weight in 24 hours, which points to how much you drink or conditions such as diabetes (Hashim 2019); and a **mixed** picture (Fine 2017). Nocturnal polyuria itself has several possible medical causes, including heart failure, diabetes, obstructive sleep apnea (breathing that stops and starts in sleep) and swollen legs, which are worth raising with a doctor (Hashim 2019).

**These point to completely different fixes, and without measuring, the answer is only a guess.** Amounts per trip that are *not small* point toward polyuria, but that is a guess dressed up as a conclusion. Three days and a jug settle it.

**This is the most valuable measurement in the whole series.** It is also the easiest.

## The number that matters more than the count

**Return-to-sleep latency** ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 02 Nocturia and Broken Sleep]]): how long it takes to fall back asleep after each waking.

Three wakings with two-minute returns is a manageable night. Three wakings with twenty-five-minute returns is a ruined one. The count is the same, and only the time to get back to sleep can be controlled. 🟡 It responds to light, phone use, clock-watching and anxious going-over of problems, all of which are choices. (Clock-watching at least has direct evidence: it increases worry before sleep and makes falling asleep take longer; Tang 2007.)

**How to record it without watching the clock**, which would defeat the purpose ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 18 Behaviour in Bed]]): estimate it roughly in the morning. "Straight back," "a while," or "ages" is detailed enough. Precision is not the point; the trend is.

## The first uninterrupted sleep period

The stretch from falling asleep to the first waking. It is used in nocturia research because 🟢 a short first stretch goes with more trouble functioning in the day and poorer sleep quality (Bliwise 2019). Whether it predicts how you function the next day better than the total number of trips is believable but unproven.

The reason is the structure of the night: deep sleep comes early ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 01 Sleep Architecture]]), so that first stretch carries the deep N3 sleep, the growth hormone burst ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 11 Hormones and Sleep]]) and the brain's nightly cleaning ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 03 Glymphatic Clearance]]). **If only one waking could be removed, the first is worth the most.**

Easy to record and worth tracking alongside the count.

## A simple sleep log

Five lines in the morning, taking under a minute. More useful than any device for this purpose, because it captures what the device cannot.

- Bedtime and wake time
- Number of wakings, and roughly when
- Time to get back to sleep for each, estimated
- How rested you feel on waking, 1–10
- Anything unusual the day before: sauna timing, late meal, THC, a hard day

The value is entirely in the **patterns across weeks**, not in any single night. That is what turns "I think the sauna change helped" into evidence.

## What wearables can and cannot do

**Fairly reliable:**

- Detecting sleep. 🟢 In a comparison of seven consumer devices against a full lab sleep study (polysomnography), all correctly spotted at least 93% of the time asleep. **Detecting time awake is much weaker:** they caught only 18–54% of the time spent awake, and did worse on broken nights (Chinoy 2021). Headline agreement figures of around 90% look good mainly because most of the night is sleep, and these devices usually overstate total sleep in people who wake a lot.
- Total sleep time, and trends over weeks
- 🟡 Trends in resting heart rate and heart-rate variability: useful signs of recovery and stress when read as your own trends rather than as absolute numbers

**Not reliable:**

- Sorting sleep into stages. 🟢 In a test of 11 devices against a lab sleep study, agreement on four-stage scoring ranged from poor to moderate (macro F1, a measure of accuracy, from 0.26 to 0.69), with wide differences between devices and between nights (Lee 2023; Chinoy 2021). **A "deep sleep" number for a given night is not a measurement.**
- Detecting brief mini-wakings, which is exactly what broken sleep is made of

**About smartwatches in particular:**

- Newer models track **wrist** temperature, which is skin temperature, not inner (core) temperature, and show it as a difference from your usual level. That is useful for spotting illness or long-term trends. It **cannot** find tonight's core temperature low point, so it cannot be used to time an alarm to the curve ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 04 The Clock]]).
- On many watches the built-in alarm only rings at a fixed time. Some newer models (for example certain Garmin watches) and some apps offer a wake window, usually 30 minutes, that aims for lighter sleep. But they aim at the *stage*, using the least reliable measurement, not at the temperature curve. Worth trying as a nudge; not worth treating as a real mechanism.

**The better route to an easy wake** is the reliable one: shift the body clock with steady light and a fixed wake time, and a plain alarm lands on the rising part of the curve by itself ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 04 The Clock]]).

## Orthosomnia — the failure mode of measuring

A known pattern, first described by sleep doctors in 2017 (Baron 2017): worry about sleep **caused by** tracking it. Someone sees a poor score, becomes worried about their sleep, and the worry makes their sleep worse. The measurement creates the problem it reports.

This links straight to sleep effort ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 18 Behaviour in Bed]]): tracking turns sleep into a task with a grade, and trying to sleep stops sleep.

**Practical rules:**

- Look at trends once a week, not scores every day.
- If checking the score in the morning changes your mood for the day, stop checking it.
- How rested you feel is a real measurement, and in some ways a better one. How the sleeper feels is the actual result; the number only stands in for it.

## Validated questionnaires

If you ever want a structured starting point:

- **Insomnia Severity Index**: seven questions, well tested, good for tracking change over months. ✅ A score of 10 or more fits medical insomnia in the general population, and a drop of about 8 points matches a moderate improvement after treatment (Morin 2011)
- **Epworth Sleepiness Scale**: measures daytime sleepiness; a high score despite enough time in bed points toward a cause of broken sleep such as apnea. 🟡 A normal score does not rule apnea out, so it is a pointer, not a test ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 14 Sleep-Disordered Breathing]])
- **Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index**: broader and longer

## What to measure, in priority order

1. **Bladder diary, at least 3 days.** Settles too much urine versus too little room. Highest value, lowest effort, and the one most often skipped.
2. **Time to get back to sleep**, estimated, every day.
3. **First unbroken sleep period.**
4. **Number of wakings**: usually already counted informally.
5. **How rested you feel**, 1–10.
6. Everything a device reports: weekly trends only.

## WHAT TO HOLD ONTO

- Three days and nights with a measuring jug settles too much urine versus too little room. Nothing else in this series would change the plan as much for so little effort.
- The time it takes to get back to sleep is the number to track. The count mostly cannot be controlled; the time can.
- A short first unbroken stretch goes with worse functioning the next day. Track it alongside the count.
- Estimate the time to get back to sleep in the morning. Never watch the clock at night.
- Trackers are good for asleep-versus-awake and weekly trends. Stage numbers for a single night are not measurements.
- Wrist temperature cannot find the core low point, so no watch can time an alarm to the curve. Shift the body clock instead.
- If the score changes your mood for the day, stop looking at the score. How rested the sleeper feels is the real result.

**The point to remember: a three-day bladder diary and a one-minute morning log tell you more than any gadget, so measure first and then fix.**
