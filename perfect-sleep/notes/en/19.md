# PERFECT SLEEP — 19 Measuring It

*How to know whether any of this worked — and how to avoid measurement becoming its own problem.*

## The one measurement that would change the plan

**The bladder diary.** Everything in [[PERFECT SLEEP — 05 Night Fluid]] rests on an assumption that often goes untested.

For two or three nights, record **the time and the volume** of every void across a full 24 hours, day and night. A cheap measuring jug is the entire equipment list.

What it produces is the **nocturnal polyuria index**: the fraction of total 24-hour urine produced during sleep.

- Above roughly **a third** in an older adult → **nocturnal polyuria.** Too much urine is being made at night. This is a hormone and volume problem, and everything in [[PERFECT SLEEP — 05 Night Fluid]] applies — the light work, the leg fluid, the salt and protein timing.
- Normal night fraction but still waking → **a capacity problem.** Residual volume, or a thickened irritable bladder wall after years of straining against obstruction. Mechanical, and it belongs to urology.

**These point at completely different fixes, and without measurement the answer is only inferred.** Void volumes that are *not small* lean toward polyuria, but that is a guess dressed as a conclusion. Two nights and a jug settles it.

**This is the highest-value measurement in the entire series.** It is also the easiest.

## The number that matters more than the count

**Return-to-sleep latency** ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 02 Nocturia and Broken Sleep]]). How long it takes to fall back asleep after each waking.

Three wakings with two-minute returns is a manageable night. Three wakings with twenty-five-minute returns is a destroyed one. The count is identical and only the latency is controllable — it responds to light exposure, phone use, clock-watching and anxious rehearsal, all of which are choices.

**How to capture it without clock-watching**, which would defeat the purpose ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 18 Behaviour in Bed]]): estimate it in the morning, roughly. "Straight back," "a while," or "ages" is adequate resolution. Precision is not the point; the trend is.

## The first uninterrupted sleep period

The stretch from falling asleep to the first waking. Used in clinical nocturia research because it predicts next-day function better than the total void count.

The reason is structural: deep sleep is front-loaded ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 01 Sleep Architecture]]), so that first stretch carries the N3, the growth hormone pulse ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 11 Hormones and Sleep]]) and the glymphatic clearance ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 03 Glymphatic Clearance]]). **If only one waking could be removed, the first is worth the most.**

Easy to record and worth tracking alongside the count.

## A simple sleep log

Five lines in the morning, taking under a minute. More useful than any device for this purpose because it captures what the device cannot.

- Bedtime and wake time
- Number of wakings, and roughly when
- Return-to-sleep latency for each — estimated
- How rested on waking, 1–10
- Anything unusual the day before: sauna timing, late meal, THC, a hard day

The value is entirely in the **correlations across weeks**, not in any single night. That is what turns "I think the sauna change helped" into evidence.

## What wearables can and cannot do

**Reasonably reliable:**

- Sleep versus wake detection — often around 90% agreement with laboratory polysomnography
- Total sleep time, trends over weeks
- Resting heart rate and heart rate variability trends — genuinely useful markers of recovery and stress load

**Not reliable:**

- Stage classification. Commonly cited around 70% for four-stage scoring against polysomnography, with wide variation between devices and between nights. **A number for "deep sleep" on a given night is not a measurement.**
- Detecting brief arousals — which is exactly what fragmentation consists of

**On smartwatches specifically:**

- Newer models track **wrist** temperature, which is skin, not core, and is reported as a deviation from personal baseline. Useful for spotting illness or long-run trends. It **cannot** locate tonight's core temperature minimum, so it cannot be used to time an alarm to the curve ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 04 The Clock]]).
- The built-in alarm is fixed-time only. Third-party apps offer a wake window that targets lighter sleep — but they target *stage*, using the least reliable measurement, not temperature phase. Worth trying as a nudge; not worth treating as a mechanism.

**The better route to an easy wake** is the reliable one: shift the phase with consistent light and a fixed wake time, and a plain alarm lands on the rising limb by itself ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 04 The Clock]]).

## Orthosomnia — the failure mode of measuring

A recognised pattern: anxiety about sleep **caused by** sleep tracking. Someone sees a poor score, becomes concerned about sleep quality, and the resulting worry degrades sleep further. The measurement creates the problem it reports.

This connects directly to sleep effort ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 18 Behaviour in Bed]]) — tracking converts sleep into a task with a grade, and trying to sleep prevents sleep.

**Practical rules:**

- Look at trends weekly, not scores daily.
- If checking the score in the morning changes the mood of the day, stop checking it.
- Subjective rested-ness is a legitimate measurement and in some ways a better one. How the sleeper feels is the actual outcome; the number is a proxy for it.

## Validated questionnaires

If a structured baseline is ever wanted:

- **Insomnia Severity Index** — seven questions, well validated, good for tracking change over months
- **Epworth Sleepiness Scale** — daytime sleepiness; a high score with adequate time in bed points toward a fragmentation cause such as apnea ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 14 Sleep-Disordered Breathing]])
- **Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index** — broader, longer

## What to measure, in priority order

1. **Bladder diary, 2–3 nights.** Settles polyuria versus capacity. Highest value, lowest effort, and the one most often skipped.
2. **Return-to-sleep latency**, estimated, daily.
3. **First uninterrupted sleep period.**
4. **Waking count** — usually already tracked informally.
5. **Subjective rested-ness**, 1–10.
6. Everything a device reports — weekly trends only.

## WHAT TO HOLD ONTO

- Two or three nights with a measuring jug settles polyuria versus capacity. Nothing else in this series would change the plan as much for as little effort.
- Return-to-sleep latency is the number to track. The count mostly is not controllable; the latency is.
- The first uninterrupted stretch predicts next-day function better than the void count.
- Estimate latency in the morning. Never watch the clock at night.
- Trackers are good for sleep-versus-wake and weekly trends. Stage numbers on a single night are not measurements.
- Wrist temperature cannot find the core minimum, so no watch can time an alarm to the curve. Shift the phase instead.
- If the score changes the mood of the day, stop looking at the score. How rested the sleeper feels is the real outcome.
