# PERFECT SLEEP — 17 External Environment

*The physical conditions of the room — things set up once and then left alone. Distinct from [[PERFECT SLEEP — 04 The Clock]], which covers light and temperature as circadian physiology. Here they are equipment settings.*

**In plain words:** the usual advice for a bedroom is "dark, cool, quiet," and it is right, but each word hides the thing that matters most. For light, it is where the light comes from: ceiling lights count more than low lamps, and even light during sleep can upset blood sugar. For temperature, it is whether your body can release heat. For noise, it is sound that comes and goes. Stale air with too much carbon dioxide also harms sleep, and only fresh air fixes it.

**Words in this note:**
- **REM**: rapid eye movement sleep, the dreaming stage
- **WHO**: World Health Organization
- **CPAP**: a mask machine that keeps the airway open with air pressure during sleep

## Dark, cool, quiet — and what that phrase gets wrong

The standard advice for a bedroom is three words, and all three are right as far as they go. The problem is that each one points at the obvious thing and misses the one that really decides the result.

- **Dark**: right, but the question is not just how much light. It is **where the light comes from**. Light from above counts more than light from below, because the lower part of the retina (the light-sensitive layer at the back of the eye), which sees the upper part of what is in front of you, drives the body clock more strongly. And darkness during sleep is not only about the clock: in a one-night lab study, ~100 lux of light overnight (lux is a measure of brightness) measurably worsened the body's response to insulin the next morning (Mason 2022).
- **Cool**: right, but the number is a habit, not a rule. What matters is whether the body can **release heat**, which depends on bedding and humidity as much as on air temperature. Cold hands and feet at bedtime mean the release is blocked, and that is a worse sign than a warm room.
- **Quiet**: right, but the question is **steadiness, not loudness**. A dripping tap at 30 dB (decibels, a measure of loudness) can bother people more than steady motorway noise at 50 dB. Sound that comes and goes unpredictably is what breaks up sleep, because each new sound can trigger a mini-waking, while steady sound gives the brain nothing new to react to.

The rest of this note is those three corrections in detail, plus air quality, which the phrase leaves out completely.

## Light in the room

**The height rule, from [[PERFECT SLEEP — 04 The Clock]].** 🟢 The lower part of the retina, which receives light coming from above, adds more to the clock's response to light than the upper part. In a small lab study, light reaching only the lower retina lowered melatonin (the night-time sleep hormone), while the same light on the upper retina did no better than darkness (Glickman 2003). Whether that is because the light-sensing cells are more crowded there, or more sensitive, is not settled. So light from above counts much more.

What this means for the room:

- **Ceiling lights off in the evening.** A ceiling light is the worst light in the house at 9pm.
- **Lamps at table or knee height.** Much safer, for the same reason.
- 🟡 Brightness and angle matter more than how warm or cool the light looks. What the clock responds to is "melanopic" light, mainly the blue-green part of light, scaled by brightness. So a screen with a warm tint still acts on the clock if it is bright. Cutting that part does help people fall asleep faster and lowers melatonin less (Schöllhorn 2023), so night-shift modes help a little, but only modestly unless the brightness also comes down.

**Light during sleep is not harmless.** 🟢 A finding worth knowing: in a lab study of healthy young adults, one night in a moderately lit room, around 100 lux (roughly a dim ceiling light), raised the heart rate overnight, lowered heart-rate variability (a sign of recovery), and made the body respond measurably worse to insulin the next morning, compared with a dim (<3 lux) room (Mason 2022). It was a single night in a small group of healthy adults. **Light during sleep affects metabolism, not only sleep** ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 12 Metabolic Status and Sleep]]). Aim for real darkness: blackout curtains or blinds, and cover or remove standby lights.

**The bathroom trip.** The single most valuable lighting choice in the house. 🟢 Bright light at 2am falls in the window when light delays the clock most strongly, the hours before the body's temperature low point (Khalsa 2003; [[PERFECT SLEEP — 04 The Clock]]), though a few minutes in a bathroom is a far smaller dose than the hours of light used in those experiments. 🟡 Bright light also wakes you up, which probably makes getting back to sleep take longer, and that is the number that matters most ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 02 Nocturia and Broken Sleep]]).

**Screens.** Two separate effects, usually mixed up:

- *Effect on the clock*: 🟢 real but modest at normal phone brightness held below eye level. In the best-known lab study, people who read a bright e-reader for four hours before bed took longer to fall asleep, had lower evening melatonin and a later body clock than when reading a printed book (Chang 2015). That is a much bigger dose than a few minutes on a dimmed phone.
- *Effect on alertness*: 🔴 probably the bigger one in everyday life, though this is reasoned rather than measured. News, email, work, anything with suspense or the endless novelty of scrolling keeps the mind busy and raises alertness, however dim the screen. **A gripping paper novel does this too.**

The rule is not "screens off." It is **dim and dull**: low brightness, device below eye level, boring content.

## Temperature in the room

**The usual advice:** 65–68°F / 18–20°C.

**Why it is a habit and not a rule.** A thermostat reads the air at one spot, usually on a central wall. A bedroom can be several degrees different depending on vents, sunshine and whether the door is open. What really matters is whether the body can release heat, which depends on bedding, nightwear and humidity as much as on air temperature. 🟢 With normal bedding, a hot room means more time awake and less deep slow-wave sleep and REM (dream sleep), and humid heat adds to the load (Okamoto-Mizuno 2012). A heavy duvet at 68°F can be warmer than a sheet at 72°F.

**The real test is not the number:**

- Waking warm or kicking off the covers → too warm, or the wrong bedding
- Cold hands and feet at bedtime → heat release is blocked (warm hands and feet predict falling asleep faster; Kräuchi 1999), which is the **worse** of the two problems ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 04 The Clock]])

**A detail from [[PERFECT SLEEP — 01 Sleep Architecture]] that applies here:** the body mostly stops controlling its temperature during REM. Since REM comes mostly late in the night, a wrong room temperature hits the **second half** of the night hardest (🟡 reasoned from the body science rather than directly measured). A room that felt fine at bedtime can be the reason for 4am wakings.

## Air quality

**Carbon dioxide** (CO₂) is the one most people never measure, and the effect is real.

- Outdoor air: ~425–430 ppm (parts per million) (2025–2026, and rising)
- 🟢 A closed bedroom with one or two sleepers often goes above 1,500 ppm by morning; field studies with windows and doors shut have recorded overnight averages of about 1,150 to 2,600 ppm (Mishra 2018; Strøm-Tejsen 2016)
- 🟡 Above roughly **1,000 ppm**, studies show measurably worse sleep and worse thinking the next day. The evidence comes from small field studies. Lowering bedroom CO₂ from about 2,400–2,600 to 660–835 ppm improved sleep measured by a wrist tracker, next-day concentration and a logical-thinking test (Strøm-Tejsen 2016), and secretly reducing the airflow gave less deep sleep and more wakings on wrist trackers (Fan 2023). 1,000 ppm is a practical sign of poor airflow rather than a proven body limit, and CO₂ may partly stand in for other indoor pollutants that build up along with it.
- 🟡 At 2,000–2,600 ppm, the levels in the closed rooms of those studies, the effects were clear

The fix is fresh air, not filtering. **An air purifier does nothing for CO₂.** It needs air exchange: a window slightly open, a door open, or a ventilation system.

**Carbon monoxide** is a completely different thing: a gas made by burning fuel, dangerous at any lasting level, and not dealt with by filtering or by a CO₂ monitor. It needs its own detector. Worth checking one is fitted and in date if there are gas appliances.

**VOCs** (chemical fumes given off by things in the home) matter mainly from new furniture, paint, flooring and mattresses. Filtering helps; fresh air and time help more.

**Humidity** around 40–60%. Too dry irritates the airway and makes snoring and a blocked nose worse; too damp stops sweat from evaporating, which blocks heat release (Okamoto-Mizuno 2012).

## Sound

**Noise breaks up sleep without waking you.** As with apnea and reflux, mini-wakings happen without being remembered. 🟢 Someone can sleep through traffic and still lose sleep structure to it all night: noise events trigger brief mini-wakings and jumps in heart rate that the sleeper does not remember (Basner 2014). The WHO's 2009 night-noise guideline sets 40 dB as the night-time average outside the home, and its 2018 guidelines set 40–45 dB depending on the source (aircraft 40, rail 44, road 45) (WHO 2009; WHO 2018).

**The key point: noise that comes and goes unpredictably wakes the brain far more than steady noise.** 🟢 Sleep disturbance follows the number and loudness of separate noise events more than the average level (Basner 2014). The brain gets used to steady sound well, and to irregular sound only partly: the heart-rate jumps to noise events continue even in people long used to them. This explains a lot:

- A steady fan is easier to sleep through than a door banging now and then
- Snoring with apnea (loud, silent, then suddenly loud again) is far more disturbing than its loudness alone suggests ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 14 Sleep-Disordered Breathing]])
- A dripping tap at 30 dB can bother people more than steady motorway noise at 50 dB (an illustration, not a measured result)

**That is why white noise can help.** It does not cover sounds by being louder. It **raises the background** so that come-and-go sounds stand out less. In a small sleep-lab study, what triggered mini-wakings was the jump from background to peak, not the peak itself, and adding white noise to recorded hospital ICU noise brought the mini-wakings back to normal (Stanchina 2005). 🟡 But the overall evidence is weak: a systematic review rated the evidence that steady noise improves sleep as very low quality, with some studies finding it disturbs sleep, and loud sound all night raises concerns for hearing (Riedy 2021). A quiet, steady fan is a sensible, low-risk thing to try; keep it as low as does the job.

**Snoring in particular.** In a shared bed this is often the main sound problem, so it belongs here as well as in [[PERFECT SLEEP — 14 Sleep-Disordered Breathing]]. Snoring with apnea is not a steady drone. It is loud, then silent while the airway is blocked, then suddenly loud again as it reopens. That pattern is the worst case for the point about getting used to sound: the silences reset the brain's adjustment, and each restart lands as something new. It breaks up sleep far more than its loudness would suggest, and it does so through mini-wakings that usually do not reach full awareness, the same silent mechanism as apnea and reflux. Ordinary snoring without apnea is steadier and easier to get used to; snoring with apnea is not. 🟢 The cost to the partner can be measured. In a small study that recorded both spouses' sleep at the same time, the wives of men with apnea had about 21 mini-wakings an hour. When the husband was put on CPAP (a machine that keeps the airway open) halfway through the night, this fell to 12, and the share of their time in bed spent asleep rose from 74% to 87%, even though they were long used to the snoring (Beninati 1999). 🔴 The loud–silent–loud pattern as the reason, and the contrast with ordinary snoring, are reasoned from the point about getting used to sound rather than measured.

**A partner's movement** passes through the mattress. 🔴 A mattress that does not pass on movement is worth thinking about, though it has not been tested in trials, when sharing a bed with someone who has apnea-related mini-wakings ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 14 Sleep-Disordered Breathing]]).

## Bed and bedding

Less backed by evidence than the rest of this note, and mostly personal. One point has trial support; the other is reasoned:

- 🟢 **Medium-firm generally does better than very firm** for sleep quality and back pain. In a randomised double-blind trial of 313 adults with long-term low-back pain, medium-firm mattresses beat firm ones on pain and disability (Kovacs 2003), and a systematic review reached the same conclusion for comfort and sleep quality (Caggiari 2021).
- 🔴 **Breathable materials matter more than firmness** for anyone who wakes warm (reasoned from the heat-release point above).

## WHAT TO HOLD ONTO

- **Dark, cool, quiet** is right, but each word points at the wrong thing: dark is about the *direction* of light, cool is about *releasing heat*, quiet is about *steadiness* rather than loudness. And the phrase leaves out air completely.
- Ceiling lights off, lamps low. Height matters more than how warm the light looks.
- Light during sleep affects metabolism: in a lab study, one night at ~100 lux worsened the body's response to insulin the next morning. Aim for real darkness.
- A red bathroom light of 5–10 lumens, fitted low, is the best single light for night trips.
- "Dim and dull" beats "no screens." What you are watching is usually a bigger problem than the light.
- Temperature: the number is a habit. Waking warm or having cold feet are the real signs, and since REM comes late, the room hits the second half of the night hardest.
- CO₂ above about 1,000 ppm is a sign of poor airflow and is linked to worse sleep in small studies. Fresh air fixes it; a purifier does not.
- Noise that comes and goes wakes the brain far more than steady noise, which is why steady white noise may help, though the evidence for it as a sleep aid is very weak.
- Sound is the bedroom factor most often left unchecked.

**The point to remember: keep the bedroom truly dark, let your body shed heat, open a window for fresh air, and smooth out sudden noises.**
