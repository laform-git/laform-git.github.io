# PERFECT SLEEP — 17 External Environment

*The physical conditions of the room — things set up once and then left alone. Distinct from [[PERFECT SLEEP — 04 The Clock]], which covers light and temperature as circadian physiology. Here they are equipment settings.*

## Dark, cool, quiet — and what that phrase gets wrong

The standard advice for a bedroom is three words, and all three are correct as far as they go. The problem is that each one points at the obvious variable and misses the one that actually decides the outcome.

- **Dark** — right, but the variable is not just how much light. It is **where the light comes from**. Overhead light counts more than light from below, because the lower retina, which sees the upper field of view, drives the clock more strongly. And darkness during sleep is not only about the clock: in a one-night lab study, ~100 lux overnight measurably worsened next-morning insulin sensitivity (Mason 2022).
- **Cool** — right, but the number is a convention. What matters is whether the body can **dump heat**, which depends on bedding and humidity as much as air temperature. Cold hands and feet at bedtime mean the dump is blocked, and that is a worse sign than a warm room.
- **Quiet** — right, but the variable is **steadiness, not volume**. A dripping tap at 30 dB can bother people more than steady motorway noise at 50 dB. Intermittent, unpredictable sound is what fragments sleep, because each new sound event can trigger an arousal, while steady sound offers no new events to react to.

The rest of this note is those three corrections in detail, plus air quality, which the phrase leaves out entirely.

## Light in the room

**The height rule, from [[PERFECT SLEEP — 04 The Clock]].** 🟢 The lower (inferior) retina, which receives light coming from above, contributes more to the clock's light response than the upper retina: in a small lab study, light reaching only the lower retina suppressed melatonin, while the same light on the upper retina did no better than darkness (Glickman 2003). Whether that is because the light-sensing cells are denser there or more sensitive is not settled. Light from above therefore counts disproportionately.

Practical translation for the room:

- **Overheads off in the evening.** A ceiling light is the worst fixture in the house at 9pm.
- **Lamps at table or knee height.** Disproportionately safe for the same reason.
- 🟡 Intensity and angle matter more than colour temperature. What the clock responds to is "melanopic" light — mainly the blue-green part of the spectrum, scaled by brightness — so a warm-tinted screen still acts on the clock if it is bright. Cutting that component does shorten sleep latency and reduce melatonin suppression (Schöllhorn 2023), so night-shift modes help a little, but only modestly unless brightness also comes down.

**Light during sleep is not harmless.** 🟢 A finding worth knowing: in a laboratory study of healthy young adults, one night in a moderately lit room — around 100 lux, roughly a dim overhead — raised overnight heart rate, lowered heart-rate variability and produced measurably worse insulin sensitivity the next morning compared with a dim (<3 lux) room (Mason 2022). It was a single night in a small group of healthy adults. **Light during sleep is a metabolic variable, not only a sleep one** ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 12 Metabolic Status and Sleep]]). Aim for genuine darkness: blackout treatment, and cover or remove standby LEDs.

**The bathroom trip.** The single highest-value lighting decision in the house. 🟢 Bright light at 2am falls in the window where light delays the clock most strongly — the hours before the body-temperature low point (Khalsa 2003; [[PERFECT SLEEP — 04 The Clock]]) — though a few minutes in a bathroom is a far smaller dose than the hours of light used in those experiments. 🟡 Bright light also alerts, which plausibly lengthens return-to-sleep latency — the number that matters most ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 02 Nocturia and Broken Sleep]]).

**Screens.** Two separate effects, usually conflated:

- *Clock effect* — 🟢 real but modest at typical phone brightness held below eye level. In the best-known lab study, people reading a bright e-reader for four hours before bed took longer to fall asleep, had lower evening melatonin and a later clock than when reading a printed book (Chang 2015) — a much larger dose than a few minutes of a dimmed phone.
- *Arousal effect* — 🔴 probably the larger one in everyday use, though this is reasoned rather than measured. News, email, work, anything with narrative tension or scrolling novelty keeps the mind engaged and raises arousal regardless of screen brightness. **A gripping paper novel does this too.**

The rule is not "screens off." It is **dim and dull**: low brightness, device below eye level, boring content.

## Temperature in the room

**The convention:** 65–68°F / 18–20°C.

**Why it is a convention and not a law.** A thermostat reads air at one spot, usually a central wall. A bedroom can sit several degrees off that reading depending on vents, sun exposure and whether the door is open. What actually matters is whether the body can dump heat, which depends on bedding, sleepwear and humidity as much as on air temperature. 🟢 With normal bedding, a hot room increases wakefulness and reduces slow-wave and REM sleep, and humid heat adds to the load (Okamoto-Mizuno 2012). A heavy duvet at 68°F can be warmer than a sheet at 72°F.

**The real test is not the number:**

- Waking warm or kicking covers off → too warm, or the bedding is wrong
- Cold hands and feet at bedtime → the heat dump is blocked (warm hands and feet predict faster sleep onset; Kräuchi 1999), which is the **worse** of the two problems ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 04 The Clock]])

**A detail from [[PERFECT SLEEP — 01 Sleep Architecture]] that applies here:** thermoregulation is largely suspended during REM. Since REM is back-loaded, an unsuitable room temperature hits the **second half** of the night hardest (🟡 reasoned from the physiology rather than directly measured). A room that felt fine at bedtime can be the reason for 4am wakings.

## Air quality

**Carbon dioxide** is the one most people never measure, and the effect is real.

- Outdoor air: ~425–430 ppm (2025–2026, and rising)
- 🟢 A closed bedroom with one or two sleepers commonly exceeds 1,500 ppm by morning; field studies with windows and doors shut have recorded overnight averages of about 1,150 to 2,600 ppm (Mishra 2018; Strøm-Tejsen 2016)
- 🟡 Above roughly **1,000 ppm**, studies show measurably worse sleep quality and next-day cognitive performance. The evidence comes from small field studies: lowering bedroom CO₂ from about 2,400–2,600 to 660–835 ppm improved actigraphic sleep, next-day concentration and a logical-thinking test (Strøm-Tejsen 2016), and covertly reducing ventilation gave less deep sleep and more awakenings on wrist trackers (Fan 2023). 1,000 ppm is a practical marker of poor ventilation rather than a proven biological threshold, and CO₂ may partly stand in for other indoor pollutants that build up with it.
- 🟡 At 2,000–2,600 ppm, the levels in the closed-room conditions of those studies, the effects were clear

The fix is ventilation, not filtration — **an air purifier does nothing for CO₂.** It needs air exchange: a window ajar, a door open, or mechanical ventilation.

**Carbon monoxide** is an entirely different thing — a combustion product, dangerous at any sustained level, and not addressed by filtration or by a CO₂ monitor. It requires a dedicated detector. Worth confirming one is present and current if there are gas appliances.

**VOCs** matter mainly from new furniture, paint, flooring and mattresses. Filtration helps; ventilation and time help more.

**Humidity** around 40–60%. Too dry irritates the airway and worsens snoring and nasal congestion; too damp impairs the evaporative heat dump (Okamoto-Mizuno 2012).

## Sound

**Noise fragments sleep below the threshold of waking.** As with apnea and reflux, arousals occur without conscious memory. 🟢 Someone can sleep through traffic and still lose architecture to it all night: noise events trigger brief arousals and heart-rate surges that the sleeper does not remember (Basner 2014). The WHO's 2009 night-noise guideline sets 40 dB as the night-time average outside the home, and its 2018 guidelines set 40–45 dB depending on the source (aircraft 40, rail 44, road 45) (WHO 2009; WHO 2018).

**The key property: intermittent, unpredictable noise is far more arousing than continuous noise.** 🟢 Sleep disturbance tracks the number and loudness of individual noise events more than the average level (Basner 2014). The brain adapts well to steady sound and only partly to irregular sound: heart-rate responses to noise events persist even in people long used to them. This explains a lot:

- A steady fan is easier to sleep through than an occasional door
- Apneic snoring — loud, silent, sudden loud resumption — is disproportionately disruptive relative to its decibel level ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 14 Sleep-Disordered Breathing]])
- A dripping tap at 30 dB can bother people more than steady motorway noise at 50 dB (an illustration, not a measured result)

**Which is why white noise can help.** It does not mask by being louder. It **raises the floor** so intermittent sounds are less distinct against the background: in a small sleep-lab study, what triggered arousals was the jump from background to peak, not the peak itself, and adding white noise to recorded ICU noise brought arousals back to baseline (Stanchina 2005). 🟡 But the overall evidence is weak: a systematic review rated the evidence that continuous noise improves sleep as very low quality, with some studies finding it disrupts sleep, and loud all-night sound raises hearing concerns (Riedy 2021). A quiet, steady fan is a reasonable low-risk experiment; keep it as low as does the job.

**Snoring, specifically.** In a shared bed this is often the main sound problem, so it belongs here as well as in [[PERFECT SLEEP — 14 Sleep-Disordered Breathing]]. Apneic snoring is not a steady drone — it runs loud, then silent during the obstructive event, then a sudden loud resumption as the airway reopens. That pattern is the worst case for the habituation point above: the silences reset the brain's adaptation and each resumption lands as a fresh stimulus. It fragments sleep far out of proportion to its decibel level, and it does so through arousals that usually do not reach conscious waking — the same silent mechanism as apnea and reflux. Ordinary non-apneic snoring is steadier and easier to habituate to; apneic snoring is not. 🟢 The cost to the partner is measurable: in a small study with simultaneous sleep studies of both spouses, the wives of men with apnea had about 21 arousals an hour; when the husband was put on CPAP halfway through the night, this fell to 12 and their sleep efficiency rose from 74% to 87%, even though they were long used to the snoring (Beninati 1999). 🔴 The loud–silent–loud pattern as the reason, and the contrast with ordinary snoring, are reasoned from the habituation point rather than measured.

**Partner movement** transmits through the mattress. 🔴 Motion isolation is worth weighing, though it has not been tested in trials, when sharing a bed with someone who has apnea-related arousals ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 14 Sleep-Disordered Breathing]]).

## Bed and bedding

Less evidence-driven than the rest of this note, and mostly individual. One point has trial support; the other is reasoned:

- 🟢 **Medium-firm generally outperforms very firm** for sleep quality and back pain: in a randomised double-blind trial of 313 adults with chronic low-back pain, medium-firm mattresses beat firm ones on pain and disability (Kovacs 2003), and a systematic review reached the same conclusion for comfort and sleep quality (Caggiari 2021).
- 🔴 **Breathable materials matter more than firmness** for anyone waking warm (reasoned from the heat-dump point above).

## WHAT TO HOLD ONTO

- **Dark, cool, quiet** is right, but each word points at the wrong variable: dark is about the *direction* of light, cool is about the *heat dump*, quiet is about *steadiness* rather than volume. And the phrase leaves out air entirely.
- Overheads off, lamps low. Height beats colour temperature.
- Light during sleep is a metabolic variable — in a lab study, one night at ~100 lux worsened next-morning insulin sensitivity. Aim for real darkness.
- A red 5–10 lumen bathroom light, mounted low, is the best single fixture for night trips.
- "Dim and dull" beats "no screens." The content is usually the bigger problem than the light.
- Temperature: the number is a convention. Waking warm or having cold feet are the real readouts, and REM being back-loaded means the room hits the second half of the night hardest.
- CO₂ above about 1,000 ppm marks poor ventilation and is linked to worse sleep in small studies. Ventilation fixes it; a purifier does not.
- Intermittent noise is far more arousing than steady noise — which is why continuous white noise may help, though the evidence for it as a sleep aid is very weak.
- Sound is the environmental factor most often left unassessed.
