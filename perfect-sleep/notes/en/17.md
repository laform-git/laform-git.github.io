# PERFECT SLEEP — 17 External Environment

*The physical conditions of the room — things set up once and then left alone. Distinct from [[PERFECT SLEEP — 04 The Clock]], which covers light and temperature as circadian physiology. Here they are equipment settings.*

## Dark, cool, quiet — and what that phrase gets wrong

The standard advice for a bedroom is three words, and all three are correct as far as they go. The problem is that each one points at the obvious variable and misses the one that actually decides the outcome.

- **Dark** — right, but the variable is not just how much light. It is **where the light comes from**. Overhead light counts far more than light from below, because the sensing cells sit in the lower retina. And darkness during sleep is not only about the clock: ~100 lux overnight measurably worsened next-morning insulin sensitivity.
- **Cool** — right, but the number is a convention. What matters is whether the body can **dump heat**, which depends on bedding and humidity as much as air temperature. Cold hands and feet at bedtime mean the dump is blocked, and that is a worse sign than a warm room.
- **Quiet** — right, but the variable is **steadiness, not volume**. A dripping tap defeats people at 30 dB while a motorway at 50 dB does not. Intermittent, unpredictable sound is what fragments sleep, because the brain habituates to steady sound and cannot habituate to irregular sound.

The rest of this note is those three corrections in detail, plus air quality, which the phrase leaves out entirely.

## Light in the room

**The height rule, from [[PERFECT SLEEP — 04 The Clock]].** The retinal cells that report brightness to the clock are densest in the lower retina, facing upward. Light from above therefore counts disproportionately.

Practical translation for the room:

- **Overheads off in the evening.** A ceiling light is the worst fixture in the house at 9pm.
- **Lamps at table or knee height.** Disproportionately safe for the same reason.
- Intensity and angle matter more than colour temperature. Night-shift modes address the weakest of the three variables and are largely marketing.

**Light during sleep is not harmless.** A finding worth knowing: sleeping in a moderately lit room — around 100 lux, roughly a dim overhead — raised overnight heart rate and produced measurably worse insulin sensitivity the next morning compared with a dark room. **Light during sleep is a metabolic variable, not only a sleep one** ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 12 Metabolic Status and Sleep]]). Aim for genuine darkness: blackout treatment, and cover or remove standby LEDs.

**The bathroom trip.** The single highest-value lighting decision in the house. Bright light at 2am has the largest clock-delaying effect of any time of day ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 04 The Clock]]), and it lengthens return-to-sleep latency — the number that matters most ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 02 Nocturia and Broken Sleep]]).

**Screens.** Two separate effects, usually conflated:

- *Clock effect* — real but modest at typical phone brightness held below eye level.
- *Arousal effect* — usually the larger one. News, email, work, anything with narrative tension or scrolling novelty raises cortisol and sympathetic activity regardless of screen brightness. **A gripping paper novel does this too.**

The rule is not "screens off." It is **dim and dull**: low brightness, device below eye level, boring content.

## Temperature in the room

**The convention:** 65–68°F / 18–20°C.

**Why it is a convention and not a law.** A thermostat reads air at one spot, usually a central wall. A bedroom can sit several degrees off that reading depending on vents, sun exposure and whether the door is open. What actually matters is whether the body can dump heat, which depends on bedding, sleepwear and humidity as much as on air temperature. A heavy duvet at 68°F can be warmer than a sheet at 72°F.

**The real test is not the number:**

- Waking warm or kicking covers off → too warm, or the bedding is wrong
- Cold hands and feet at bedtime → the heat dump is blocked, which is the **worse** of the two problems ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 04 The Clock]])

**A detail from [[PERFECT SLEEP — 01 Sleep Architecture]] that applies here:** thermoregulation is largely suspended during REM. Since REM is back-loaded, an unsuitable room temperature hits the **second half** of the night hardest. A room that felt fine at bedtime can be the reason for 4am wakings.

## Air quality

**Carbon dioxide** is the one most people never measure, and the effect is real.

- Outdoor air: ~420 ppm
- A closed bedroom with one or two sleepers routinely exceeds 1,500 ppm by morning
- Above roughly **1,000 ppm**, studies show measurably worse sleep quality and next-day cognitive performance
- Above 2,000 ppm the effect becomes substantial

The fix is ventilation, not filtration — **an air purifier does nothing for CO₂.** It needs air exchange: a window ajar, a door open, or mechanical ventilation.

**Carbon monoxide** is an entirely different thing — a combustion product, dangerous at any sustained level, and not addressed by filtration or by a CO₂ monitor. It requires a dedicated detector. Worth confirming one is present and current if there are gas appliances.

**VOCs** matter mainly from new furniture, paint, flooring and mattresses. Filtration helps; ventilation and time help more.

**Humidity** around 40–60%. Too dry irritates the airway and worsens snoring and nasal congestion; too damp impairs the evaporative heat dump.

## Sound

**Noise fragments sleep below the threshold of waking.** As with apnea and reflux, arousals occur without conscious memory. Someone can sleep through traffic and still lose architecture to it all night. The WHO night-noise guideline sits around 40 dB for this reason.

**The key property: intermittent, unpredictable noise is far more arousing than continuous noise.** The brain habituates to steady sound and cannot habituate to irregular sound. This explains a lot:

- A steady fan is easier to sleep through than an occasional door
- Apneic snoring — loud, silent, sudden loud resumption — is disproportionately disruptive relative to its decibel level ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 14 Sleep-Disordered Breathing]])
- A dripping tap defeats people at 30 dB while a motorway at 50 dB does not

**Which is exactly why white noise works.** It does not mask by being louder. It **raises the floor** so intermittent sounds are less distinct against the background. That is the mechanism, and it is why a continuous fan is a legitimate intervention rather than a comfort item.

**Snoring, specifically.** In a shared bed this is often the main sound problem, so it belongs here as well as in [[PERFECT SLEEP — 14 Sleep-Disordered Breathing]]. Apneic snoring is not a steady drone — it runs loud, then silent during the obstructive event, then a sudden loud resumption as the airway reopens. That pattern is the worst case for the habituation point above: the silences reset the brain's adaptation and each resumption lands as a fresh stimulus. It fragments sleep far out of proportion to its decibel level, and it does so through arousals that usually do not reach conscious waking — the same silent mechanism as apnea and reflux. Ordinary non-apneic snoring is steadier and easier to habituate to; apneic snoring is not.

**Partner movement** transmits through the mattress. Motion isolation is a real specification, not a marketing one, when sharing a bed with someone who has apnea-related arousals ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 14 Sleep-Disordered Breathing]]).

## Bed and bedding

Less evidence-driven than the rest of this note, and mostly individual. Two things do have support:

- **Medium-firm generally outperforms very firm** for sleep quality and back pain.
- **Breathable materials matter more than firmness** for anyone waking warm.

## WHAT TO HOLD ONTO

- **Dark, cool, quiet** is right, but each word points at the wrong variable: dark is about the *direction* of light, cool is about the *heat dump*, quiet is about *steadiness* rather than volume. And the phrase leaves out air entirely.
- Overheads off, lamps low. Height beats colour temperature.
- Light during sleep is a metabolic variable — ~100 lux worsened next-morning insulin sensitivity. Aim for real darkness.
- A red 5–10 lumen bathroom light, mounted low, is the best single fixture for night trips.
- "Dim and dull" beats "no screens." The content is usually the bigger problem than the light.
- Temperature: the number is a convention. Waking warm or having cold feet are the real readouts, and REM being back-loaded means the room hits the second half of the night hardest.
- CO₂ above 1,000 ppm degrades sleep. Ventilation fixes it; a purifier does not.
- Intermittent noise is far more arousing than steady noise — which is exactly why continuous white noise works.
- Sound is the environmental factor most often left unassessed.
