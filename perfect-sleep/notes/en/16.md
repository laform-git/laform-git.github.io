# PERFECT SLEEP — 16 Contemplative Practice

*Meditation, yoga nidra and Christian prayer — covered here only for their effect on arousal and sleep. Prayer as prayer lies outside the scope of this series.*

**In plain words:** light, food and temperature cannot help if your mind will not switch off. Slow breathing, especially with a long breath out at about six breaths a minute, calms the nervous system within minutes. Meditation, yoga nidra and prayer all use this, and the Rosary naturally slows breathing to that rate. Prayer is a relationship, not a relaxation trick, and the evening Examen is especially suited to putting the day to rest. Do these before bed, sitting up, not in bed trying to force sleep.

**Words in this note:**
- **CBT-I**: cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia
- **AASM**: American Academy of Sleep Medicine

## The problem these address

Everything else in this series works on something physical: light, fluid, temperature, food. This note deals with the one thing that defeats all of them: **being tired and unable to switch off.**

From [[PERFECT SLEEP — 10 Stress and Cortisol]], falling asleep needs cortisol (a stress hormone) and the "fight or flight" arousal to have come down. If the mind is still racing, they have not. No amount of well-timed light or the right bedroom temperature can beat a nervous system that is switched on.

## The shared mechanism

These practices are very different in meaning and purpose, but in the body they meet in the same place. All three involve **slow breathing, steady attention on one thing, and stepping back from problem-solving.** That combination:

- 🟢 Strengthens the vagus nerve (the body's calming nerve) and lowers the "fight or flight" drive, measurable within minutes (Zaccaro 2018)
- 🟢 Lowers heart rate and blood pressure (Pascoe 2017)
- 🟡 Lowers cortisol. In a study pooling meditation trials against active comparison groups, the drop was clearest for practices that focus attention on one thing (Pascoe 2017)
- Breaks the cycle of going over and over things (rumination), which is what most trouble falling asleep is really made of

**A long breath out is probably one of the working parts of the breathing.** ✅ The heart rate rises a little as you breathe in and falls as you breathe out (respiratory sinus arrhythmia). 🟡 Making the breath out longer than the breath in tips the system toward the calming side. In a small lab study, a long breath out during slow breathing increased the calming signal in heart-rate variability and felt more relaxing (Van Diest 2014). In a one-month randomised study from Huberman's lab, "cyclic sighing" (breathing that stresses a long breath out) improved mood more than mindfulness meditation (Balban 2023). Both studies are small; this is promising, not settled.

## Six breaths per minute — the resonance point

The most interesting body finding in this note, and a specific one.

🟢 At roughly six breaths a minute (about 0.1 Hz; the exact rate varies a little between people), the heart and blood vessels go into **resonance**. The swings in heart rate caused by breathing line up with the rhythm of the baroreflex (the body's automatic blood-pressure control), so heart-rate variability reaches its highest and the baroreflex is strongly engaged (Lehrer 2000). Breathing at that rate has a bigger calming effect than breathing faster or slower.

The striking part is how many old practices land there without ever being designed to. A study published in the *BMJ* in 2001 compared saying the Rosary (the Hail Mary, in Latin) with a yoga mantra and found that **both slowed breathing to almost exactly six a minute**. The sensitivity of the baroreflex rose clearly, from 9.5 to 11.5 ms/mm Hg (Bernardi 2001). 🟢 The body science is solid, but the study was of 23 healthy adults in a lab, and it measured heart and blood-pressure rhythms, not sleep.

The form produced the body effect. Nobody was trying to fine-tune it.

## Yoga nidra

A guided practice of lying still while attention is led step by step through the body, staying on purpose at the edge of sleep without falling in.

The key finding: 🟡 in one small study recording brain waves (EEG), beginners doing it were scored as awake the whole time, yet showed **more slow (delta) waves in one central area of the brain** while staying aware. Researchers call this "local sleep" (Datta 2022). It is interesting, but it is not deep N3 sleep: by standard sleep scoring the state was wakefulness.

🟡 The evidence that it helps people fall asleep faster and sleep better is promising but weak: a handful of small randomised trials, most with a moderate-to-high risk of bias (Dutta 2026), and a 2026 study pooling the trials whose combined effects did not reach statistical significance (Singh 2026). It is one of the more promising practices for someone whose problem is specifically being unable to wind down, and it needs nothing but a recording and twenty minutes.

## Meditation

🟢 The best evidence is for mindfulness programmes. In older adults with sleep complaints, a six-week mindfulness course did better than lessons in sleep habits on sleep quality in a randomised trial (Black 2015). A study pooling 18 trials found moderate-strength evidence that it helps compared with other activities of the same length, but no advantage over proven insomnia treatments such as CBT-I (a talking-and-habits treatment for insomnia) (Rusch 2019).

It works through the calming described above, plus a specific effect on **going over and over things**. The training is partly in noticing a thought and not following it, which is exactly the skill that lying awake needs.

What it is not: a quick fix. The effect comes from practice over weeks, not from one session at bedtime.

## Christian prayer — distinguished

**The basic difference.** Most practices in this note are ways of managing yourself: the focus is your own attention, and the goal is a state of mind. Christian prayer is addressed to a person. It is a relationship, not a method applied to yourself.

This difference is practical, not decoration. 🔴 **If prayer is turned into a relaxation routine, it stops being prayer, and usually stops working as either.** The body benefit comes as a by-product of the form, not as its purpose, and once the purpose is replaced the form tends to fall apart too.

**Where the mechanism truly overlaps.** The Rosary and the Jesus Prayer are repeated spoken prayers, and repeated spoken prayer paces breathing toward the resonance rate described above. This is the clearest piece of mechanism evidence in the whole note (Bernardi 2001), though that study measured breathing and blood-pressure reflexes, not sleep.

**Forms that suit the night especially well:**

- **Compline**: the Church's night prayer, the last prayer of the day in the Liturgy of the Hours. Short, fixed, and made for exactly this hour. Being fixed is useful in itself: nothing to decide at the end of a tired day.
- **The Examen**: a guided review of the day, offered up and handed over. It deals with going over and over things **directly and by design**, which is the real reason people lie awake replaying the day. The day is looked at on purpose and closed, instead of replaying by itself in the dark. Of everything in this note, the Examen fits the specific problem most closely. 🔴 This is reasoning from the mechanism; no study has tested the Examen on sleep.
- **The Jesus Prayer**: short, repeated, paced with the breath. The form with the clearest effect on the body.
- **The Rosary**: the same breath-pacing effect over a longer stretch.

**The honest picture of the evidence.** 🟡 Studies that look at groups of people generally link religious practice with better sleep (while religious doubt and crises of faith go with worse sleep). But they look at one moment in time and are heavily clouded by other factors (Basis 2026): people who pray regularly also tend to drink less, keep more regular hours and have more support from others. The breathing and blood-pressure research is the only part with a clean, separate mechanism. **Stated so the note does not claim too much.**

**Why this may be the longest-lasting help for someone who already prays.** Every other item in this series needs a new habit to be started and kept up. Where prayer is already part of daily life, **a practice that already exists does not need to be forced into a habit**, and in a list of things to try, that is a big practical advantage whatever else is true.

## The trap: do not use these as sleep tools in bed

The most important practical point in the note.

**Trying to fall asleep wakes you up.** 🟢 This is a well-established explanation of how insomnia keeps going (Espie 2006). Any practice done in bed with sleep as the goal turns into a test: the sleeper starts checking whether it is working, and checking is itself alerting. That is how relaxation techniques become one more thing to fail at.

The practices work best done **before** bed, sitting up, in a dim room, with sleep as a side effect rather than the goal. Compline said in a chair at 9:15 does more than a body scan tried at 11:40 out of frustration. 🟢 Slow, paced breathing for 20 minutes before bed helped people with insomnia fall asleep faster and spend more of their time in bed asleep in a small trial (Tsai 2015), and the AASM (American Academy of Sleep Medicine) conditionally recommends relaxation therapy for long-term insomnia (Edinger 2021).

The exception is getting back to sleep after a waking ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 02 Nocturia and Broken Sleep]]). There, a short repeated prayer or slow breathing is likely to help, precisely because at that point there is no falling asleep to check on and nothing to achieve. 🔴 This follows from the mechanism; it has not been tested specifically after night wakings.

## WHAT TO HOLD ONTO

- These deal with the one problem physical measures cannot touch: being tired and unable to switch off.
- Slow breathing with a long breath out shifts the nervous system within minutes. Around six breaths a minute is the resonance point.
- The Rosary and a yoga mantra both land at six a minute on their own. The form produces the body effect.
- Prayer is a relationship, not a technique. Turn it into a method and it stops working as either.
- The Examen fits the real problem most closely: it closes the day on purpose instead of letting it replay in the dark.
- Compline is short, fixed, and needs no decision at the end of a tired day.
- Do these before bed, sitting up, not in bed aiming at sleep. Trying to fall asleep wakes you up.
- The one exception: after a night waking, repeated prayer or slow breathing helps, because there is nothing to achieve.

**The point to remember: slow your breathing and close the day before you lie down, and let sleep come as a gift rather than a goal.**
