# PERFECT SLEEP — 09 Sleep Medications and Aids

*Reference note. Not medical advice — prescription decisions belong with the physician.*

**In plain words:** a sleeping pill can knock you out and still make your sleep worse, because many of them cut the deep and dream stages that do the real repair. Common sleeping pills and over-the-counter sleep aids lose their effect quickly, can be hard to stop, and some make it harder to empty the bladder. A few newer or low-dose options are gentler on sleep. Melatonin is a timing signal, best taken in a small dose in the early evening, not a knock-out pill.

**Words in this note:**
- **CBT-I**: cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia
- **AASM**: American Academy of Sleep Medicine
- **GABA-A**: the receptor for GABA that sleeping pills and alcohol act on
- **REM**: rapid eye movement sleep, the dreaming stage
- **FDA**: U.S. Food and Drug Administration
- **BPH**: benign prostatic hyperplasia, a non-cancerous enlarged prostate
- **THC**: the part of cannabis that makes you high

## The governing idea: sedation is not sleep

This is the single most important thing in this note. A drug can reliably make you unconscious while **making the sleep that follows worse**. Time asleep goes up, real rest goes down, and it feels like a drug that works.

That gap between "I fell asleep" and "I woke up restored" is why sleeping pills have a poor long-term record even though they clearly work in the short term. 🟡 Interpretation, but it matches the guidelines. The American College of Physicians makes CBT-I (cognitive behavioural therapy for insomnia, a talking-and-habits treatment) the first treatment for long-term insomnia for all adults (strong recommendation) and limits drugs to short-term use, decided together with the patient (Qaseem 2016). The AASM (American Academy of Sleep Medicine) rates the evidence for every single sleeping pill as WEAK (Sateia 2017). ✅

## Benzodiazepines

Temazepam, lorazepam, clonazepam and similar. They act on GABA-A receptors (the brain's main calming switch), the same pathway that a supplement mix with doubled-up sources overloads ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 08 Substances and Stack]]), but with far more force.

- They increase total sleep time and help you fall asleep faster.
- **They increase N2 (light sleep) while cutting N3 (deep sleep) and, to a lesser degree, REM (dream sleep)** 🟢 (Roehrs 2010): the two stages that do the restoring work ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 01 Sleep Architecture]], [[PERFECT SLEEP — 03 Glymphatic Clearance]]).
- The body gets used to them within weeks. The FDA label warns that physical dependence can develop after several days to weeks of regular use, and since 2020 all benzodiazepines carry a boxed warning (the FDA's strongest warning) for misuse, addiction, dependence and withdrawal reactions (FDA 2020). ✅
- Stopping can cause **rebound insomnia**, sometimes worse than the original problem for a few nights. (Stopping suddenly can also cause dangerous withdrawal, including seizures: reduce slowly, never stop suddenly.) This is what makes them hard to stop and easy to mistake for a permanent need.
- Long-term use in older adults raises concerns about thinking and falls, and goes with next-day impairment. The AGS Beers Criteria (a list of medicines older adults should avoid) list benzodiazepines as drugs to avoid in adults over 65 (AGS 2023). ✅

## Z-drugs

Zolpidem, eszopiclone, zaleplon. They act at the same receptor, mainly at the types of GABA-A that contain the α1 part.

- Somewhat less cut to REM than benzodiazepines, and shorter-acting.
- A smaller effect on deep sleep than benzodiazepines: the zolpidem label reports deep sleep kept at sleeping doses (FDA Ambien label). 🟢 Corrected: earlier versions said Z-drugs reduce deep sleep.
- Known for **complex sleep behaviours**: sleepwalking, driving, eating while asleep, with no memory of it afterwards. Rare, but serious enough to carry an FDA boxed warning since 2019; anyone who has had such an episode should never take any of the three again (FDA 2019). ✅
- The body still gets used to them, and rebound still happens.

## Antihistamines

Diphenhydramine and doxylamine: the active ingredient in most sleep products sold without a prescription.

- They sedate by blocking H1 (a histamine switch in the brain).
- **The body gets used to them within days.** In a controlled trial, the sleepiness from diphenhydramine was completely gone by day 3–4 (Richardson 2002) ✅, often faster than with prescription sleeping pills.
- They carry a large **anticholinergic load** (they block acetylcholine, a messenger used by the brain and the body). That goes with next-day grogginess, dry mouth, constipation, and, with long-term use in older adults, concerns about thinking and memory (more total anticholinergic use over the years was linked to a higher risk of dementia in a large study: Gray 2015 🟢). The AASM suggests *not* using diphenhydramine for insomnia (Sateia 2017), and the Beers Criteria advise avoiding older-generation antihistamines after 65 (AGS 2023). ✅
- Anticholinergics also **make it harder to empty the bladder**. That makes them a poor choice for anyone with BPH (an enlarged prostate) or urine already left in the bladder, reason enough to avoid the whole group in that case. ✅ The labels themselves say to ask a doctor first if there is trouble urinating because of an enlarged prostate, and the Beers Criteria advise avoiding strongly anticholinergic drugs in men with BPH symptoms.

## The better prescription options
*"Better" here means gentler on the structure of sleep. That is an interpretation (🔴) based on how the drugs work and on sleep-stage data, not on trials of real-life outcomes. The guidelines rank them differently: the AASM 2017 suggests (WEAK) suvorexant and low-dose doxepin for staying asleep, and suggests **against** trazodone for insomnia (Sateia 2017).*

**Trazodone**, at the low doses used for sleep, works mostly by blocking 5-HT2A (a serotonin switch) and H1, not GABA-A. It keeps, and in some studies increases, deep slow-wave sleep. (50 mg for a week reduced night wakings and raised slow-wave sleep on day 7, but caused small problems with memory and balance the next day: Roth 2011) 🟢. Less tolerance and no rebound insomnia like the benzodiazepines are believable but poorly studied 🟡. Although it is widely used, it is not FDA-approved for insomnia, and the AASM suggests against it for insomnia (WEAK) because the benefits in trials were small (Sateia 2017). Side effects include morning grogginess, dizziness on standing up and, rarely, priapism (a painful erection that will not go away, a warning on the label), worth raising with a urologist when there is prostate disease.

**Low-dose doxepin** (3–6mg) works mainly by blocking H1 at that dose, without the broad anticholinergic load of diphenhydramine. It is FDA-approved specifically for *staying* asleep rather than falling asleep, which is the relevant problem in a broken night; the AASM suggests it for that use (WEAK) (Sateia 2017). ✅ Caution: the label still says not to use it with severe trouble passing urine, and advises starting at 3 mg in anyone prone to that or over 65 (FDA Silenor label).

**Orexin receptor blockers** (suvorexant, lemborexant, daridorexant) are the newest group. They block the brain's orexin system, which keeps us awake, instead of forcing sedation, and they mostly keep the structure of sleep intact 🟢. The AASM suggests suvorexant for staying asleep (WEAK); lemborexant and daridorexant were approved after that guideline. Right now they are the option kindest to sleep structure, though expensive and still building long-term data.

## Melatonin — almost everyone uses it wrong

**It is a timing signal, not a sedative.** Melatonin tells the body it is night. Its direct sleep-inducing effect is small: in a study pooling many studies it helped people fall asleep about 7 minutes faster and added about 8 minutes of sleep (Ferracioli-Oda 2013) ✅. Expecting it to knock you out leads to taking more and more when it does not.

Three consequences:

1. **Dose.** What the body makes on its own matches roughly 0.3–0.5mg: 0.3 mg brings back normal night-time blood levels (Zhdanova 2001) ✅. Shop products commonly sell 5 or 10mg, ten to thirty times the natural amount. Higher doses do not move the clock more: 0.5 mg and 3 mg give similar shifts when each is taken at the best time (Burgess 2010) ✅. In older adults, 3 mg kept blood levels high into the next morning and lowered body temperature (Zhdanova 2001). The idea that high doses make the receptors less sensitive is a theory, not established 🔴.
2. **Timing is the whole mechanism.** Taken a few hours *before* the body's own melatonin rise, it moves the clock earlier (the biggest shifts with 0.5 mg came when it was taken in the afternoon, 2–4 hours before the evening rise: Burgess 2010 ✅), which is useful for moving to an earlier wake time. Taken at bedtime, it does very little to the clock.
3. **Where it really works:** jet lag (Cochrane review: Herxheimer 2002) ✅, a sleep schedule that runs very late (the AASM suggests timed melatonin: Auger 2015) ✅, shift work (smaller, less consistent benefit 🟡). Where it mostly does not: ordinary insomnia in someone already sleeping at the right hour. The AASM suggests against melatonin for long-term insomnia (WEAK) (Sateia 2017). ✅

To help move to an earlier wake time, the sensible use is **0.5mg in the early evening**: not at bedtime, and not 5mg.

## Prescription drugs that affect sleep as a side effect

Worth knowing, because people do not think of them as sleep drugs.

- **Beta blockers** (blood-pressure and heart medicines) lower the night-time production of melatonin through β1 receptors (propranolol and atenolol in a controlled study; carvedilol did not) (Stoschitzky 1999) ✅. This is a known and often overlooked cause of insomnia. In people with high blood pressure taking beta blockers, melatonin supplements improved sleep (Scheer 2012). 🟢
- **SSRIs and SNRIs** (common antidepressants) cut REM and can cause or worsen limb jerks during sleep and restless legs (Wichniak 2017). 🟢
- **Corticosteroids** (steroid medicines like prednisone) are alerting and break up sleep. Any observation about sleep made during a course of steroids is clouded by them.
- **Alpha blockers** used for BPH usually do not affect sleep; their effect on nocturia (waking to urinate) is real but modest 🟡.
- **Diuretics** ("water pills") taken late in the day will obviously add to night urine, so timing matters.

## WHAT TO HOLD ONTO

- Sedation is not sleep. Time asleep can go up while real rest goes down.
- Benzodiazepines and Z-drugs cut the two stages that do the work, the body gets used to them, and stopping causes a rebound.
- Over-the-counter antihistamines make it harder to empty the bladder: avoid them completely with BPH.
- Trazodone, low-dose doxepin and the orexin blockers are the options kinder to sleep structure, if anything is ever needed.
- Melatonin is a clock signal, not a sedative. 0.5mg in the early evening; not 10mg at bedtime.
- Beta blockers lower melatonin. Steroids break up sleep. Check any new prescription against this list.

**The point to remember: a pill that puts you to sleep is not the same as a good night; fix the habits first and treat drugs as a short-term last resort.**
