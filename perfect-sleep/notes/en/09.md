# PERFECT SLEEP — 09 Sleep Medications and Aids

*Reference note. Not medical advice — prescription decisions belong with the physician.*

## The governing idea: sedation is not sleep

This is the single most important thing in this note. A compound can reliably produce unconsciousness while **degrading the sleep that follows**. Time-asleep goes up, restoration goes down, and the subjective experience is of a drug that works.

That gap between "I fell asleep" and "I was restored" is why sleep medication has a poor long-term record despite obvious short-term effect.

## Benzodiazepines

Temazepam, lorazepam, clonazepam and similar. They act on GABA-A receptors — the same pathway a supplement stack with duplicated sources overloads ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 08 Substances and Stack]]), at far greater force.

- Increase total sleep time and reduce onset latency.
- **Increase N2 while suppressing both N3 and REM** — the two stages that do the restorative work ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 01 Sleep Architecture]], [[PERFECT SLEEP — 03 Glymphatic Clearance]]).
- Tolerance develops within weeks.
- Discontinuation produces **rebound insomnia**, often worse than the original problem, which is what makes them hard to stop and easy to mistake for a permanent need.
- Long-term use in older adults carries cognitive and fall-risk concerns, and is associated with next-day impairment.

## Z-drugs

Zolpidem, eszopiclone, zaleplon. Act at a subtype of the same receptor.

- Somewhat less REM suppression than benzodiazepines, and shorter-acting.
- Still reduce deep sleep.
- Notable for **complex sleep behaviours** — sleepwalking, sleep-driving, sleep-eating, with amnesia for the episode. Uncommon but serious enough to carry a boxed warning.
- Tolerance and rebound still apply.

## Antihistamines

Diphenhydramine and doxylamine — the active ingredient in most over-the-counter sleep products.

- Sedating via H1 blockade.
- **Tolerance develops within days**, often faster than for prescription hypnotics.
- Carry a significant **anticholinergic burden**, associated with next-day grogginess, dry mouth, constipation, and — in long-term use in older adults — cognitive concerns.
- Anticholinergics also **impair bladder emptying**, which makes them a poor choice in anyone with BPH or existing residual volume — reason enough to avoid the entire category in that case.

## The better prescription options

**Trazodone**, at the low doses used for sleep, works largely by 5-HT2A and H1 antagonism rather than GABA-A. It preserves and in some studies increases slow-wave sleep. Less tolerance, no rebound insomnia of the benzodiazepine kind. Side effects include morning grogginess and, rarely, priapism — worth flagging with a urologist when prostate disease is present.

**Low-dose doxepin** (3–6mg) is selectively antihistaminergic at that dose without the broad anticholinergic load of diphenhydramine. Specifically useful for sleep *maintenance* — staying asleep rather than falling asleep — which is the relevant problem in a fragmented night.

**Orexin receptor antagonists** (suvorexant, lemborexant, daridorexant) are the newest class. They block the wake-promoting orexin system rather than forcing sedation, and largely preserve architecture. Currently the most architecturally respectful option, though expensive and still accumulating long-term data.

## Melatonin — almost everyone uses it wrong

**It is a timing signal, not a sedative.** Melatonin tells the body it is night. It does not push anyone into sleep, and expecting sedation from it leads to escalating the dose when it does not work.

Three consequences:

1. **Dose.** Physiological release corresponds to roughly 0.3–0.5mg. Commercial products commonly sell 5 or 10mg — ten to thirty times physiological. Higher doses do not work better and can produce next-day grogginess and receptor desensitisation.
2. **Timing is the entire mechanism.** Taken a few hours *before* the natural rise, it advances the clock — useful for shifting an earlier wake time. Taken at bedtime, it does very little to phase.
3. **Where it genuinely works:** jet lag, shift work, delayed sleep phase. Where it mostly does not: ordinary insomnia in someone already sleeping at the right hour.

To support shifting to an earlier wake time, the logical use is **0.5mg in the early evening** — not at bedtime, not at 5mg.

## Prescription drugs that affect sleep as a side effect

Worth knowing because they are not thought of as sleep drugs.

- **Beta blockers** suppress melatonin production — a well-documented and often unrecognised cause of insomnia.
- **SSRIs and SNRIs** suppress REM and can cause or worsen periodic limb movements.
- **Corticosteroids** are alerting and fragment sleep. Any sleep observation made during a steroid course is confounded.
- **Alpha blockers** used for BPH are usually sleep-neutral or helpful by reducing nocturia.
- **Diuretics** taken late in the day will obviously add to night urine — timing matters.

## WHAT TO HOLD ONTO

- Sedation is not sleep. Time asleep can rise while restoration falls.
- Benzodiazepines and Z-drugs suppress the two stages that do the work, build tolerance, and rebound on withdrawal.
- Over-the-counter antihistamines impair bladder emptying — avoid entirely with BPH.
- Trazodone, low-dose doxepin and the orexin antagonists are the architecturally kinder options if anything is ever needed.
- Melatonin is a clock signal, not a sedative. 0.5mg in the early evening; not 10mg at bedtime.
- Beta blockers suppress melatonin. Steroids fragment sleep. Check any new prescription against this list.
