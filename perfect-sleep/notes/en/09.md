# PERFECT SLEEP — 09 Sleep Medications and Aids

*Reference note. Not medical advice — prescription decisions belong with the physician.*

## The governing idea: sedation is not sleep

This is the single most important thing in this note. A compound can reliably produce unconsciousness while **degrading the sleep that follows**. Time-asleep goes up, restoration goes down, and the subjective experience is of a drug that works.

That gap between "I fell asleep" and "I was restored" is why sleep medication has a poor long-term record despite obvious short-term effect. 🟡 Interpretation, but it matches the guidelines: the American College of Physicians makes CBT-I the first-line treatment for chronic insomnia for all adults (strong recommendation) and limits drugs to short-term use by shared decision (Qaseem 2016), and the AASM rates the evidence for every individual hypnotic as WEAK (Sateia 2017). ✅

## Benzodiazepines

Temazepam, lorazepam, clonazepam and similar. They act on GABA-A receptors — the same pathway a supplement stack with duplicated sources overloads ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 08 Substances and Stack]]), at far greater force.

- Increase total sleep time and reduce onset latency.
- **Increase N2 while suppressing N3 and, to a lesser degree, REM** 🟢 (Roehrs 2010) — the two stages that do the restorative work ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 01 Sleep Architecture]], [[PERFECT SLEEP — 03 Glymphatic Clearance]]).
- Tolerance develops within weeks; FDA labelling warns that physical dependence can develop after several days to weeks of regular use, and since 2020 all benzodiazepines carry a boxed warning for abuse, addiction, dependence and withdrawal reactions (FDA 2020). ✅
- Discontinuation can produce **rebound insomnia**, sometimes worse than the original problem for a few nights (abrupt stopping can also cause dangerous withdrawal, including seizures — taper, never stop suddenly), which is what makes them hard to stop and easy to mistake for a permanent need.
- Long-term use in older adults carries cognitive and fall-risk concerns, and is associated with next-day impairment. The AGS Beers Criteria list benzodiazepines as drugs to avoid in adults over 65 (AGS 2023). ✅

## Z-drugs

Zolpidem, eszopiclone, zaleplon. Act at the same receptor, preferentially at GABA-A subtypes containing the α1 subunit.

- Somewhat less REM suppression than benzodiazepines, and shorter-acting.
- Smaller effect on deep sleep than benzodiazepines: the zolpidem label reports deep sleep preserved at hypnotic doses (FDA Ambien label). 🟢 Corrected — earlier versions said Z-drugs reduce deep sleep.
- Notable for **complex sleep behaviours** — sleepwalking, sleep-driving, sleep-eating, with amnesia for the episode. Rare but serious enough to carry an FDA boxed warning since 2019; anyone who has had such an episode should not take any of the three again (FDA 2019). ✅
- Tolerance and rebound still apply.

## Antihistamines

Diphenhydramine and doxylamine — the active ingredient in most over-the-counter sleep products.

- Sedating via H1 blockade.
- **Tolerance develops within days** — tolerance to diphenhydramine's sedation was complete by day 3–4 in a controlled trial (Richardson 2002) ✅ — often faster than for prescription hypnotics.
- Carry a significant **anticholinergic burden**, associated with next-day grogginess, dry mouth, constipation, and — in long-term use in older adults — cognitive concerns (higher cumulative anticholinergic use was linked to higher dementia risk in a large cohort: Gray 2015 🟢). The AASM suggests *not* using diphenhydramine for insomnia (Sateia 2017), and the Beers Criteria advise avoiding first-generation antihistamines after 65 (AGS 2023). ✅
- Anticholinergics also **impair bladder emptying**, which makes them a poor choice in anyone with BPH or existing residual volume — reason enough to avoid the entire category in that case. ✅ The OTC labels themselves say to ask a doctor first if there is trouble urinating due to an enlarged prostate, and the Beers Criteria advise avoiding strongly anticholinergic drugs in men with BPH symptoms.

## The better prescription options
*"Better" here means kinder to sleep architecture — an interpretation (🔴) based on mechanism and stage data, not on outcome trials. Guidelines rank differently: the AASM 2017 suggests (WEAK) suvorexant and low-dose doxepin for sleep maintenance, and suggests **against** trazodone for insomnia (Sateia 2017).*

**Trazodone**, at the low doses used for sleep, works largely by 5-HT2A and H1 antagonism rather than GABA-A. It preserves and in some studies increases slow-wave sleep (50 mg for a week reduced night wakings and raised slow-wave sleep on day 7, but caused small next-day memory and balance impairments: Roth 2011) 🟢. Less tolerance and no rebound insomnia of the benzodiazepine kind are plausible but poorly studied 🟡. Despite wide use it is not FDA-approved for insomnia, and the AASM suggests against it for insomnia (WEAK) because trial benefits were small (Sateia 2017). Side effects include morning grogginess, orthostatic dizziness and, rarely, priapism (a labelled warning) — worth flagging with a urologist when prostate disease is present.

**Low-dose doxepin** (3–6mg) acts mainly as an H1 antihistamine at that dose, without the broad anticholinergic load of diphenhydramine. FDA-approved specifically for sleep *maintenance* — staying asleep rather than falling asleep — which is the relevant problem in a fragmented night; the AASM suggests it for that use (WEAK) (Sateia 2017). ✅ Caution: the label still contraindicates it in severe urinary retention and advises starting at 3 mg in anyone prone to retention or over 65 (FDA Silenor label).

**Orexin receptor antagonists** (suvorexant, lemborexant, daridorexant) are the newest class. They block the wake-promoting orexin system rather than forcing sedation, and largely preserve architecture 🟢. The AASM suggests suvorexant for sleep maintenance (WEAK); lemborexant and daridorexant were approved after that guideline. Currently the most architecturally respectful option, though expensive and still accumulating long-term data.

## Melatonin — almost everyone uses it wrong

**It is a timing signal, not a sedative.** Melatonin tells the body it is night. Its direct sleep-inducing effect is small — in a meta-analysis it shortened sleep onset by about 7 minutes and added about 8 minutes of sleep (Ferracioli-Oda 2013) ✅ — and expecting sedation from it leads to escalating the dose when it does not work.

Three consequences:

1. **Dose.** Physiological release corresponds to roughly 0.3–0.5mg: 0.3 mg restores normal night-time blood levels (Zhdanova 2001) ✅. Commercial products commonly sell 5 or 10mg — ten to thirty times physiological. Higher doses do not shift the clock more: 0.5 mg and 3 mg give similar-sized shifts when each is timed optimally (Burgess 2010) ✅. In older adults, 3 mg kept blood levels raised into the next morning and lowered body temperature (Zhdanova 2001). Receptor desensitisation at high doses is a hypothesis, not established 🔴.
2. **Timing is the entire mechanism.** Taken a few hours *before* the natural rise — the largest advances with 0.5 mg came when it was taken in the afternoon, 2–4 hours before the evening melatonin rise (Burgess 2010) ✅ — it advances the clock — useful for shifting an earlier wake time. Taken at bedtime, it does very little to phase.
3. **Where it genuinely works:** jet lag (Cochrane review: Herxheimer 2002) ✅, delayed sleep phase (AASM suggests timed melatonin: Auger 2015) ✅, shift work (smaller, less consistent benefit 🟡). Where it mostly does not: ordinary insomnia in someone already sleeping at the right hour — the AASM suggests against melatonin for chronic insomnia (WEAK) (Sateia 2017). ✅

To support shifting to an earlier wake time, the logical use is **0.5mg in the early evening** — not at bedtime, not at 5mg.

## Prescription drugs that affect sleep as a side effect

Worth knowing because they are not thought of as sleep drugs.

- **Beta blockers** suppress night-time melatonin production via β1 receptors (propranolol and atenolol in a controlled study; carvedilol did not) (Stoschitzky 1999) ✅ — a recognised and often overlooked cause of insomnia. In hypertensive patients on beta blockers, melatonin supplementation improved sleep (Scheer 2012). 🟢
- **SSRIs and SNRIs** suppress REM and can cause or worsen periodic limb movements and restless legs (Wichniak 2017). 🟢
- **Corticosteroids** are alerting and fragment sleep. Any sleep observation made during a steroid course is confounded.
- **Alpha blockers** used for BPH are usually sleep-neutral; their effect on nocturia is real but modest 🟡.
- **Diuretics** taken late in the day will obviously add to night urine — timing matters.

## WHAT TO HOLD ONTO

- Sedation is not sleep. Time asleep can rise while restoration falls.
- Benzodiazepines and Z-drugs suppress the two stages that do the work, build tolerance, and rebound on withdrawal.
- Over-the-counter antihistamines impair bladder emptying — avoid entirely with BPH.
- Trazodone, low-dose doxepin and the orexin antagonists are the architecturally kinder options if anything is ever needed.
- Melatonin is a clock signal, not a sedative. 0.5mg in the early evening; not 10mg at bedtime.
- Beta blockers suppress melatonin. Steroids fragment sleep. Check any new prescription against this list.
