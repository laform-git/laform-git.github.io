# PERFECT SLEEP — 04B Light and Temperature

*Companion to [[PERFECT SLEEP — 04 The Clock]]. The body reads two signals to decide whether it is day or night: light and temperature. Day is bright and warm. Night is dark and cool. Most sleep problems in this area come from sending the wrong signal at the wrong time.*

**In plain words:** the body clock reads two signals to know it is night: darkness and a cooler body. Bright light in the morning and dim, warm light in the evening keep the clock strong. At night, even a bathroom light can wake the brain up, so a dim red light low to the floor is best. A cool bedroom, warm hands and feet, and a warm shower an hour or two before bed help sleep come.

**Words in this note:**
- **REM**: rapid eye movement sleep, the dreaming stage

## Part 1 — Light

### How light reaches the clock

Besides rods and cones (the cells we see with), the eye has a third kind of light sensor: a small group of cells in the retina that contain a pigment called **melanopsin**. They do not help us see. They measure **brightness** and report it straight to the master clock in the hypothalamus (the suprachiasmatic nucleus). Strong evidence.

- They are most sensitive to **blue** light (around 480 nm, a measure of light's colour) and least sensitive to **red** (above about 600 nm).
- They respond slowly and add up the light they get over minutes, so the clock cares about **how bright, for how long, and when**.

### Scale: outdoors and indoors are different worlds

| Setting | Approximate light |
| --- | --- |
| Direct sun | up to 100,000 lux |
| Overcast day outdoors | 1,000–10,000 lux |
| Typical office or living room | 100–500 lux |
| Dim evening lamp | 10–30 lux |
| Full moon | under 1 lux |

(Lux is a measure of how bright light is.) A bright room is still dim to the clock. Staying indoors all day leaves the clock with a weak daytime signal, which makes the night signal weaker too.

### Morning light: setting the clock

- Bright light in the first hours after waking **moves the clock earlier**, makes the morning rise of cortisol (a hormone that helps you get going) sharper, and sets the timing of that evening's melatonin (the night-time sleep hormone). Strong evidence.
- Being outdoors for 5–10 minutes on a clear morning, longer when it is cloudy, gives the clock more signal than hours indoors. Moderate evidence for the exact times; strong for the principle.
- Bright days also make the clock **less sensitive** to evening light. Moderate evidence.

### Evening light: delaying the night

- Ordinary room light (under 200 lux) in the hours before bed lowered melatonin in almost everyone tested and shortened the melatonin night by about 90 minutes. Strong evidence.
- Reading on a tablet that gives off light before bed, compared with a printed book, delayed melatonin, made people less sleepy, made them take longer to fall asleep, and reduced REM sleep (dream sleep). Controlled study.
- **People differ hugely:** how sensitive people are to evening light varies more than fiftyfold. Some people are barely affected; others lose melatonin in light most would call dim. Strong evidence.
- An expert agreement (2022) recommends no more than about **10 melanopic lux** (roughly one dim, warm lamp) in the last three hours before bed, and darkness during sleep.

### Light during sleep

- In a 2022 Northwestern University study, sleeping one night with the overhead room light on (about 100 lux, eyes closed) raised the **heart rate at night** and made the body **respond worse to insulin the next morning**, compared with sleeping in dim light. Controlled laboratory study.
- In older adults, any measurable light during the night went with higher rates of obesity, diabetes and high blood pressure. Observational (a study that looks for links, which cannot prove cause).
- Eyelids block only part of the light. A dark room, or an eye mask, closes the gap.

### The 3am bathroom light

**Turn on a bright bathroom light at 3am and the brain has just been told it is morning.** Within seconds the brain's alerting system fires. Within minutes melatonin, the hormone that keeps the body in its night state, starts shutting down. The eyes switch into daytime mode, so the dark bedroom on the way back feels like a cave. You go back to bed more awake than when you left, and the next stretch of sleep pays the price. Three trips a night with the light on, and a full night has been traded for broken pieces.

The science underneath:

- Light has a **direct waking effect** on the brain, separate from melatonin. It shows up within minutes as faster reaction times and more wake-type brain activity. Strong evidence.
- Melatonin starts dropping within minutes at night-time light levels. After a short exposure it recovers, so the **biggest cost is the longer time to fall back asleep**, not the hormone itself. Moderate evidence.
- Bright light **ruins the eyes' adjustment to the dark.** It takes many minutes for the eyes to adjust to darkness again, which keeps the brain busier finding the way back. Strong evidence.

### Dim red: the right light for the night

**Two things decide how much a night light disturbs sleep: how bright it is and its colour.**

- **Low brightness matters most.** A bright red light is worse than a dim warm one.
- **Red is the colour the clock notices least,** because the melanopsin cells are nearly blind to it. Amber is next best.
- **Red also keeps your night vision.** The rods, the eye cells used for seeing in the dark, do not respond to red, so dim red light lets the eyes stay adjusted to the dark. You can still find your way, and settling back is faster.
- Put the two together, **dim plus red**, and the effect on the clock is very small. Not zero, but very small.
- Put it **low**: night lights at floor level or with motion sensors, not ceiling lights. Light from below reaches the lower part of the retina less, and less light reaches the clock cells.

## Part 2 — Temperature

Temperature is the second night signal. Just as darkness triggers melatonin, **a falling inner body temperature triggers sleep.**

### The core temperature drop

- The body's inner (core) temperature follows a daily curve. It is highest in the early evening, then falls by about **0.5–1 °C** through the night to a low point roughly two hours before natural waking. Strong evidence.
- Sleep is most likely to start while the curve is **falling**. A body that cannot get rid of heat falls asleep more slowly and sleeps more lightly. Strong evidence.
- The body gets rid of heat through the **hands and feet**. Warm hands and feet (open blood vessels in the skin) were the best sign of falling asleep quickly in a classic study. Strong evidence. Cold feet slow the start of sleep; warm socks can help.

### The bedroom

- Heat during sleep causes more waking and less deep sleep and REM. Strong evidence.
- A common target is a **cool room, about 60–67 °F (16–19 °C)**, with bedding that lets the body keep a comfortable warm pocket around it. In some studies older adults sleep best a little warmer; bedding and personal comfort decide the final number. Moderate evidence.
- **The body mostly stops controlling its temperature during REM** ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 01 Sleep Architecture]]). It cannot protect itself against a room that is too hot or too cold, and REM fills most of the second half of the night. So a bad room temperature hits the early-morning hours hardest.

### Using heat to get cool

- A **warm shower or bath (about 40–42 °C) 1–2 hours before bed** cut the time to fall asleep by about 10 minutes in a study pooling many studies. The warm water opens blood vessels in the skin; afterwards the body loses heat fast, and that drop is what triggers sleep. Strong evidence for the timing window.
- The same idea is behind sauna timing in [[PERFECT SLEEP — 06 Heat and Timing]]: heat early, cool down after, bed on the way down.
- **Too close to bed backfires.** Hard exercise, a hot bath or a sauna in the last 30–60 minutes can leave body temperature high at lights-out.
- A **late heavy meal** adds heat for several hours while it is digested, at exactly the time body temperature should be falling ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 07B Late Dinner and Alcohol Before Bed]]). Moderate evidence.

## The rule

|  | Day | Evening | Night |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Light | Bright, outdoors if possible | Dim and warm, screens down | Dark; dim red and low if needed |
| Temperature | Active, warm | Warm shower 1–2 h before bed, then cool down | Cool room, warm hands and feet |

## Example case

An adult who wakes several times a night to urinate. Each trip under a bright ceiling light in the bathroom costs more than the trip itself: alertness jumps, the eyes lose their adjustment to the dark, and getting back to sleep takes far longer than the walk. A warm, overheated bedroom makes the early-morning REM hours lighter and the wakings easier to set off. The fixes cost almost nothing: a dim red night light at floor level along the route, a cooler room, a warm shower an hour or two before bed, and morning light outdoors to make the whole rhythm stronger.

## WHAT TO HOLD ONTO

- Night means dark and cool. Day means bright and warm. Send the right signal at the right time.
- Morning light outdoors sets the clock; evening room light pushes it later.
- How sensitive people are to evening light varies more than fiftyfold.
- One night with the room light on raises heart rate and worsens the body's response to insulin the next morning.
- A bright light at 3am costs the next stretch of sleep. Dim red, low down, is the right night light.
- Sleep begins as body temperature falls. Cool room, warm hands and feet, warm shower 1–2 hours before bed.

**The point to remember: make your days bright and your nights dark and cool, and use only a dim red light low down if you must get up.**
