# PERFECT SLEEP — 04B Light and Temperature

*Companion to [[PERFECT SLEEP — 04 The Clock]]. The body reads two signals to decide whether it is day or night: light and temperature. Day is bright and warm. Night is dark and cool. Most sleep problems in this area come from sending the wrong signal at the wrong time.*

## Part 1 — Light

### How light reaches the clock

The eye has a third kind of light sensor besides rods and cones: a small set of retinal cells containing the pigment **melanopsin**. They do not help with seeing. They measure **brightness** and report it straight to the master clock in the hypothalamus (the suprachiasmatic nucleus). Strong evidence.

- They are most sensitive to **blue** light (around 480 nm) and least sensitive to **red** (above about 600 nm).
- They respond slowly and add up exposure over minutes, so the clock cares about **how bright, for how long, and when**.

### Scale: outdoors and indoors are different worlds

| Setting | Approximate light |
| --- | --- |
| Direct sun | up to 100,000 lux |
| Overcast day outdoors | 1,000–10,000 lux |
| Typical office or living room | 100–500 lux |
| Dim evening lamp | 10–30 lux |
| Full moon | under 1 lux |

A bright room is still dim to the clock. Being indoors all day leaves the clock with a weak daytime signal, which makes the night signal weaker too.

### Morning light: setting the clock

- Bright light in the first hours after waking **moves the clock earlier**, sharpens the morning cortisol rise, and sets the timing of that evening's melatonin release. Strong evidence.
- Outdoors for 5–10 minutes on a clear morning, longer when overcast, delivers more clock signal than hours indoors. Moderate evidence for the exact durations; strong for the principle.
- Bright days also make the clock **less sensitive** to evening light. Moderate evidence.

### Evening light: delaying the night

- Ordinary room light (under 200 lux) in the hours before bed suppressed melatonin in almost all subjects and shortened the melatonin night by about 90 minutes. Strong evidence.
- Reading on a light-emitting tablet before bed, compared with a printed book, delayed melatonin, made people less sleepy, lengthened the time to fall asleep and reduced REM sleep. Controlled study.
- **People differ enormously:** sensitivity to evening light varies more than fiftyfold between individuals. Some people are barely affected; others lose melatonin at levels of light most would call dim. Strong evidence.
- An expert consensus (2022) recommends no more than about **10 melanopic lux** (roughly a dim, warm lamp) in the last three hours before bed, and darkness during sleep.

### Light during sleep

- In a 2022 Northwestern University study, sleeping one night with overhead room light on (about 100 lux, closed eyes) raised **night-time heart rate** and worsened **next-morning insulin sensitivity**, compared with sleeping in dim light. Controlled laboratory study.
- In older adults, any measurable light during the night was associated with higher rates of obesity, diabetes and high blood pressure. Observational.
- Eyelids block only part of the light. A dark room, or an eye mask, closes the gap.

### The 3am bathroom light

**Flip on a bright bathroom light at 3am and the brain has just been told it is morning.** Within seconds the alerting system fires. Within minutes melatonin, the hormone holding the body in its night state, starts shutting down. The eyes snap into daytime mode, so the dark bedroom on the way back feels like a cave. The return to bed happens more awake than the departure, and the next stretch of sleep is the price. Three trips a night with the light on, and a full night has been traded for broken pieces.

The science underneath:

- Light has a **direct alerting effect** on the brain, separate from melatonin — measurable as faster reaction times and more wake-type brain activity within minutes. Strong evidence.
- Melatonin suppression starts within minutes at night-time light levels; after a short exposure it recovers, so the **biggest cost is the longer time to fall back asleep**, not the hormone itself. Moderate evidence.
- Bright light **ruins dark adaptation** — it takes many minutes for the eyes to readjust to darkness, which keeps the brain more engaged in finding the way back. Strong evidence.

### Dim red: the right light for the night

**Two things decide how much a night light disturbs sleep: brightness and colour.**

- **Low intensity matters most.** A bright red light is worse than a dim warm one.
- **Red is the colour the clock notices least,** because the melanopsin cells are nearly blind to long wavelengths. Amber is the next best.
- **Red also preserves night vision.** The rods used for seeing in the dark are insensitive to red, so dim red light lets the eyes stay dark-adapted — the room stays navigable and settling back is faster.
- Put the two together — **dim plus red** — and the effect on the clock is minimal. Not zero, but minimal.
- Place it **low**: floor-level or motion-sensor night lights, not overhead fixtures. Light from below reaches the lower retina less, and less light reaches the clock cells.

## Part 2 — Temperature

Temperature is the second night signal. Just as darkness triggers melatonin, **a falling core temperature triggers sleep.**

### The core temperature drop

- Core body temperature follows a daily curve: highest in the early evening, then falling by about **0.5–1 °C** through the night to a minimum roughly two hours before natural waking. Strong evidence.
- Sleep is most likely to begin on the **falling** part of the curve. A body that cannot shed heat falls asleep more slowly and sleeps more lightly. Strong evidence.
- The body dumps heat through the **hands and feet**. Warm extremities (open blood vessels in the skin) were the best predictor of falling asleep quickly in a classic study. Strong evidence. Cold feet slow sleep onset; warm socks can help.

### The bedroom

- Heat exposure during sleep increases wakefulness and reduces deep sleep and REM. Strong evidence.
- A common target is a **cool room, about 60–67 °F (16–19 °C)**, with bedding that lets the body hold a comfortable microclimate. Older adults sleep best in a slightly warmer range in some studies — bedding and individual comfort decide the final number. Moderate evidence.
- **Thermoregulation is largely switched off during REM** ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 01 Sleep Architecture]]). The body cannot defend itself against a room that is too hot or too cold, and REM dominates the second half of the night — so a bad room temperature hits the early-morning hours hardest.

### Using heat to get cool

- A **warm shower or bath (about 40–42 °C) 1–2 hours before bed** shortened the time to fall asleep by about 10 minutes in a meta-analysis. The warm water opens blood vessels in the skin; afterwards the body sheds heat fast, and that rebound drop is what triggers sleep. Strong evidence for the timing window.
- The same logic sits behind sauna timing in [[PERFECT SLEEP — 06 Heat and Timing]]: heat early, cooling after, bed on the way down.
- **Too close to bed backfires** — vigorous exercise, a hot bath or a sauna in the last 30–60 minutes can leave core temperature high at lights-out.
- A **late heavy meal** adds metabolic heat for several hours as it is digested, at exactly the time core temperature should be falling ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 07B Late Dinner and Alcohol Before Bed]]). Moderate evidence.

## The rule

|  | Day | Evening | Night |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Light | Bright, outdoors if possible | Dim and warm, screens down | Dark; dim red and low if needed |
| Temperature | Active, warm | Warm shower 1–2 h before bed, then cool down | Cool room, warm hands and feet |

## Example case

An adult who wakes several times a night to urinate. Each trip under a bright overhead bathroom light costs more than the trip itself: alertness spikes, the eyes lose their dark adaptation, and getting back to sleep takes far longer than the walk. A warm, overheated bedroom makes the early-morning REM hours lighter and the wakings easier to trigger. The fixes cost almost nothing: a dim red night light at floor level on the route, a cooler room, a warm shower an hour or two before bed, and morning light outdoors to make the whole rhythm stronger.

## WHAT TO HOLD ONTO

- Night means dark and cool. Day means bright and warm. Send the right signal at the right time.
- Morning light outdoors sets the clock; evening room light pushes it later.
- Sensitivity to evening light varies more than fiftyfold between people.
- One night with the room light on raises heart rate and worsens next-morning insulin sensitivity.
- A bright light at 3am costs the next stretch of sleep. Dim red, low down, is the right night light.
- Sleep begins as core temperature falls. Cool room, warm hands and feet, warm shower 1–2 hours before bed.
