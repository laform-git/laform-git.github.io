# PERFECT SLEEP — 08 Substances and Stack

*THC and the bedtime supplement stack. The behavioural side of THC use is out of scope — covered here only for its sleep mechanism. For the underlying machinery — acetylcholine as the REM switch, and how THC, alcohol and CBD each reach it — see [[PERFECT SLEEP — 08A Sedative Mechanisms: THC, Alcohol and CBD]].*

## THC — the two-night cost

**Why it feels like it helps.** 🟢 THC shortens sleep onset in the short term and reduces REM (Babson 2017). Less REM means fewer remembered dreams and a subjective impression of deeper, cleaner sleep. The impression is real; the restoration is not.

**Night one — the dose night.** REM is reduced directly. 🟢 In lab studies, THC cut REM eye-movement density and, to a lesser extent, REM duration, while deep sleep tended to rise (Feinberg 1975). Sleep looks deep but delivers less REM than normal. The next day brings yawning and an unrestored state, partly from the missing REM and partly because THC and its metabolites linger. 🟡 Measurable impairment after inhaled THC mostly resolves within about 5–7 hours, and oral THC lasts longer (McCartney 2021), so a next-day carry-over is plausible after evening edibles but not established.

**Night two — the rebound.** 🔴 Interpretation: REM rebound is documented when THC is stopped after days to weeks of use (Feinberg 1976); a rebound on the night after a *single* dose has not been measured. The idea is that the brain tracks the REM debt and pays it back. The second night runs REM-heavy: vivid dreams, lighter sleep, easier waking, more fragmentation.

**So one use plausibly costs a flat night and then a fragile one.** This is the detail almost nobody knows, because the bad next day gets noticed and the night after it never gets connected.

**Why the second night matters especially here.** REM-heavy sleep is lighter sleep, and from [[PERFECT SLEEP — 02 Nocturia and Broken Sleep]], arousal threshold is lower in REM than in deep sleep. Each bladder signal breaks through more easily. In someone already waking several times a night, the rebound night is where the damage compounds.

**Tolerance masks it.** ✅ With frequent use, CB1 receptors downregulate (Hirvonen 2012) and tolerance develops to THC's effect on REM (Feinberg 1976) — the contrast disappears. ✅ After about four weeks off, receptor density returns to normal (Hirvonen 2012). The same dose lands on a naive system and hits far harder than it did during regular use. 🟢 Occasional users are more impaired by a given dose than regular users (McCartney 2021).

**🔴 The blunt conclusion (an interpretation): once sensitivity is restored, occasional use is worse than regular use was.** There is no such thing as an isolated dose — each one carries a two-night bill.

**Stopping.** ✅ Strange or vivid dreams are the most consistent symptom of cannabis withdrawal (Allsop 2011) and fit REM rebound at scale — the same mechanism as above, stretched across weeks after years of suppression.

## The stacking lesson

A lesson that generalises well beyond magnesium.

The generalisable rule: **when several products contain the same active compound under different names, the dose is the sum, not the label.** Magnesium glycinate is magnesium bound to glycine — the glycine comes along whether or not it appears on the front of the bottle. A typical 200 mg magnesium dose as bisglycinate carries roughly 1 g of glycine (calculated from its molecular weight; less in buffered products).

## Magnesium forms — what actually differs

Once absorbed, magnesium is magnesium. The forms differ in absorption and in what they are bound to.

- **Magtein (magnesium L-threonate)** — 🟡 the claimed feature is that it crosses into the brain more readily, which was shown in rats (Slutsky 2010). One 80-person, industry-funded trial reported better sleep scores at 1 g a day for 21 days (Hausenblas 2024). Promising, not established.
- **Magnesium glycinate** — well absorbed and gentle on the gut, but 🔴 its sleep effect may be partly *the glycine* rather than the magnesium; this has not been tested.
- **Magnesium taurate** — 🔴 taurine is often called mildly calming, mainly on animal data; same principle as glycinate.
- **Magnesium malate** — malate feeds the energy cycle and is often marketed as the more **activating** form, usually for daytime. 🔴 No study shows it disrupts sleep; the bedtime advice is theory. **At bedtime it is the odd one out** only on that logic.

**Honest note on evidence:** 🟡 magnesium supplementation for sleep has weak support. A meta-analysis of three small trials in older adults found sleep onset about 17 minutes faster than placebo, on low- to very-low-quality evidence (Mah 2021). It is not a strong intervention. The forms are marketed with more confidence than the data supports.

## Inositol

Myo-inositol, used at gram doses. 🟡 Its evidence for this use is thin: small trials found benefit in panic disorder at 12–18 g a day (Benjamin 1995), a meta-analysis found no significant effect on anxiety or depression overall (Mukai 2014), and there are no good trials for sleep. Its strongest evidence base sits in other conditions. Importantly, **it is not a GABA-A compound** — ✅ it is a precursor in the phosphoinositide second-messenger pathway and does not add to the inhibitory load.

## Nicotine

Included because it is commonly mis-filed. 🟢 Nicotine is stimulant and arousing: it lengthens sleep onset, fragments sleep and in most studies suppresses REM (Jaehne 2009). Contrary to its grouping with caffeine, it ✅ **stimulates vasopressin release** (Husain 1975), making it antidiuretic rather than diuretic ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 05 Night Fluid]]).

## WHAT TO HOLD ONTO

- One THC dose plausibly costs two nights: a REM-reduced night, then a REM-rebound night that is lighter and easily interrupted (🔴 the second night is inferred, not measured).
- Tolerance hides this. After about four weeks off, receptors recover and occasional use hits harder than regular use did.
- The dose is the sum across products, not what each label says. Magnesium glycinate also delivers glycine.
- Malate is marketed as activating. There is no sleep evidence either way, so it is simply the least logical bedtime form.
- Magnesium for sleep is a weak intervention. Do not expect much from form selection.
- Incidental glycine in a magnesium pill is not a glycine dose; the research uses about 3g (Bannai 2012).
