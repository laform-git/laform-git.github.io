# PERFECT SLEEP — 08 Substances and Stack

*THC and the bedtime supplement stack. The behavioural side of THC use is out of scope — covered here only for its sleep mechanism. For the underlying machinery — acetylcholine as the REM switch, and how THC, alcohol and CBD each reach it — see [[PERFECT SLEEP — 08A Sedative Mechanisms: THC, Alcohol and CBD]].*

**In plain words:** THC (the part of cannabis that makes you high) feels like it helps sleep but cuts dream sleep, and the night after may bring a lighter, more broken sleep as the brain catches up. When several supplements contain the same ingredient, the real dose is the total. Magnesium helps sleep only a little, inositol has thin evidence, and nicotine disturbs sleep.

**Words in this note:**
- **THC**: the part of cannabis that makes you high
- **REM**: rapid eye movement sleep, the dreaming stage
- **CBD**: cannabidiol, a non-intoxicating part of cannabis
- **CB1**: the brain's main receptor for cannabis
- **GABA-A**: the receptor for GABA that sleeping pills and alcohol act on
- **NMDA**: a receptor for glutamate, the brain's main "go" messenger
- **FDA**: U.S. Food and Drug Administration

## THC — the two-night cost

**Why it feels like it helps.** 🟢 THC helps people fall asleep faster in the short term and reduces REM, the dreaming stage (Babson 2017). Less REM means fewer remembered dreams and a feeling of deeper, cleaner sleep. The feeling is real; the restoring is not.

**Night one: the night you take it.** REM is reduced directly. 🟢 In lab studies, THC cut how busy the eye movements in REM were and, to a lesser extent, how long REM lasted, while deep sleep tended to rise (Feinberg 1975). The sleep looks deep but gives less REM than normal. The next day brings yawning and a not-rested feeling, partly from the missing REM and partly because THC and what the body breaks it into stay around. 🟡 Measurable impairment after smoked or inhaled THC mostly fades within about 5–7 hours, and THC eaten in edibles lasts longer (McCartney 2021). So a carry-over into the next day is believable after evening edibles, but not established.

**Night two: the rebound.** 🔴 Interpretation: a REM rebound (extra REM to make up for lost REM) is documented when people stop THC after days to weeks of use (Feinberg 1976); a rebound on the night after a *single* dose has not been measured. The idea is that the brain keeps track of the REM it is owed and pays it back. The second night runs heavy in REM: vivid dreams, lighter sleep, easier waking, more broken sleep.

**So one use probably costs a flat night and then a fragile one.** This is the detail almost nobody knows, because people notice the bad next day but never connect the night after it.

**Why the second night matters especially here.** Sleep heavy in REM is lighter sleep, and from [[PERFECT SLEEP — 02 Nocturia and Broken Sleep]], it is easier to wake from REM than from deep sleep. Each signal from the bladder breaks through more easily. In someone who already wakes several times a night, the rebound night is where the damage adds up.

**Tolerance hides it.** ✅ With frequent use, the brain's CB1 receptors (the switches THC acts on) are cut back (Hirvonen 2012), and the body gets used to THC's effect on REM (Feinberg 1976), so the contrast disappears. ✅ After about four weeks without it, the number of receptors returns to normal (Hirvonen 2012). The same dose then lands on a fresh system and hits far harder than it did during regular use. 🟢 Occasional users are more impaired by a given dose than regular users (McCartney 2021).

**🔴 The blunt conclusion (an interpretation): once sensitivity is back, occasional use is worse than regular use was.** There is no such thing as a single, isolated dose: each one comes with a two-night bill.

**Stopping.** ✅ Strange or vivid dreams are the most consistent symptom of cannabis withdrawal (Allsop 2011), and they fit REM rebound on a large scale: the same mechanism as above, stretched over weeks after years of holding REM down.

## The stacking lesson

A lesson that applies far beyond magnesium.

The general rule: **when several products contain the same active ingredient under different names, the dose is the total, not what each label says.** Magnesium glycinate is magnesium bound to glycine (an amino acid that may calm the brain). The glycine comes with it whether or not it is printed on the front of the bottle. A typical 200 mg magnesium dose as bisglycinate carries roughly 1 g of glycine (worked out from its molecular weight; less in buffered products).

## Magnesium forms — what actually differs

Once absorbed, magnesium is magnesium. The forms differ in how well they are absorbed and in what they are bound to.

- **Magtein (magnesium L-threonate)** — 🟡 its claimed feature is that it gets into the brain more easily, which was shown in rats (Slutsky 2010). One 80-person trial, paid for by industry, reported better sleep scores at 1 g a day for 21 days (Hausenblas 2024). Promising, not established.
- **Magnesium glycinate** — well absorbed and gentle on the gut, but 🔴 its sleep effect may partly come from *the glycine* rather than the magnesium; this has not been tested.
- **Magnesium taurate** — 🔴 taurine is often called mildly calming, mostly based on animal data; the same idea as glycinate.
- **Magnesium malate** — malate feeds the cell's energy cycle and is often sold as the more **energising** form, usually for daytime. 🔴 No study shows it disturbs sleep; the advice to avoid it at bedtime is theory. **At bedtime it is the odd one out** only by that reasoning.

**An honest note on the evidence:** 🟡 the support for magnesium as a sleep aid is weak. A study pooling three small trials in older adults found people fell asleep about 17 minutes faster than with a dummy pill, on low- to very-low-quality evidence (Mah 2021). It is not a strong fix. The forms are sold with more confidence than the data supports.

## Inositol

Myo-inositol, taken in gram doses. 🟡 Its evidence for this use is thin. Small trials found a benefit in panic disorder at 12–18 g a day (Benjamin 1995), a study pooling many trials found no clear effect on anxiety or depression overall (Mukai 2014), and there are no good trials for sleep. Its strongest evidence is for other conditions. Importantly, **it does not act on GABA-A** (the brain's main calming switch, which sleeping pills and alcohol act on). ✅ It is a building block in a signalling system inside cells (the phosphoinositide pathway) and does not add to the calming load.

## Nicotine

Included because it is often put in the wrong group. 🟢 Nicotine is a stimulant and keeps you alert: it makes falling asleep take longer, breaks up sleep, and in most studies reduces REM (Jaehne 2009). Unlike caffeine, which it is often grouped with, it ✅ **increases the release of vasopressin** (the hormone that holds back urine) (Husain 1975). So it holds urine back rather than making more ([[PERFECT SLEEP — 05 Night Fluid]]).

## WHAT TO HOLD ONTO

- One dose of THC probably costs two nights: a night with less REM, then a rebound night with more REM that is lighter and easily broken (🔴 the second night is worked out, not measured).
- Tolerance hides this. After about four weeks without it, the receptors recover and occasional use hits harder than regular use did.
- The dose is the total across products, not what each label says. Magnesium glycinate also gives you glycine.
- Malate is sold as energising. There is no sleep evidence either way, so it is simply the least sensible form for bedtime.
- Magnesium for sleep is a weak fix. Do not expect much from choosing the form.
- The glycine that comes along in a magnesium pill is not a glycine dose; the research uses about 3g (Bannai 2012).

**The point to remember: THC trades tonight's comfort for two weaker nights, and no supplement here is strong enough to replace good sleep habits.**
