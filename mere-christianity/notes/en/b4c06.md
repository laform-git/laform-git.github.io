## The Insight

An offer made to everyone is not the same claim as automatic possession by everyone. Lewis's first note: being biologically human does not by itself confer the begotten, Zoe-quality life from Ch1 — the offer is real and universal, but not automatically owned apart from actual union with Christ. His second note: ancient cultures are full of death-and-rebirth story patterns, and he does not deny it or find it threatening. His answer is sharper than dismissal — Christianity claims this pattern actually happened once, historically, which is a different kind of claim than one myth among many.

Both notes do the same job from different angles: each blocks a way of flattening a demanding, specific claim into something vaguer and more comfortable. Automatic inclusion instead of an offer requiring union. One myth among many instead of a singular historical event. Lewis is guarding precision on both fronts at once, in a chapter deliberately too short to let either turn into a digression.

## Why It Matters

Notice which note you needed more — the automatic-inclusion one, or the myth-versus-history one. That tells you something about where your own picture of Christianity had quietly gone soft.

## Evidence / Support

First note: the offer is universal; possession requires union with Christ, not mere biology.

Second note: the recurring myth pattern is real and acknowledged — Christianity's claim is that it happened once, historically, a different category of claim entirely.

## Meditations (3)

- An offer made to everyone is not the same claim as automatic possession by everyone.
- A recurring pattern in myth and a specific claim in history are two different kinds of claims.
- Sometimes the honest move is a short clarifying note, not a full new argument.
