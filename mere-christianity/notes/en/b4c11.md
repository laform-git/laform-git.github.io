## The Insight

"Whoever loses his life will save it." Lewis names the mechanism the whole book has been circling — not a new argument, the plain statement of what everything before it, the moral law, the historical claims, the repeated failures of self-effort, was actually pointing toward. The step is like a mutation in that it's a genuine leap to a new kind of thing, not an improved version of the old. Unlike biological mutation, it is not blind. A mutation happens to you. This one asks you to choose it.

The sharper claim, and the one people fear backwards: clinging to your own way does not protect your individuality. It's what shrinks it. Self-will, run on its own logic, converges toward sameness — the same handful of recognizable grievances and vanities, because a closed-off self only has so many ways to arrange itself. Handed over, a person gets drawn out into the one specific thing only they were made to be. Losing yourself was never the risk. It was the only route to the thing you were afraid of losing.

## Why It Matters

The book's landing point. Everything since Book 1 has been building toward one demand: stop treating the argument as an idea to evaluate and start treating it as a process to actually undergo.

## Evidence / Support

A genuinely new step, not an improved old one — union with Christ producing a quality of person not fully continuous with unaided human nature, echoing Ch1's Bios/Zoe split.

Already present, mostly unnoticed — this new humanity is not a future hope only; it's scattered among believers now, invisible because it does not look dramatic.

The individuality paradox is the chapter's sharpest claim — surrendering self was assumed to erase identity. Lewis argues the reverse produces the more distinct, more fully realized individual.
