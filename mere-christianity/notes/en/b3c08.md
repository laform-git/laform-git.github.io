## The Insight

A glutton can be perfectly satisfied alone with food. A miser alone with money. Pride cannot work that way — it needs a rival, real or imagined, to have something over. That's the structural tell that separates it from every other vice, and it's why Lewis calls it the sin by which the devil became the devil: not an appetite gone too far, but something fundamentally competitive, anti-God at the root, satisfied only by comparison.

Every other vice announces itself to your conscience fairly quickly. Pride disguises itself as legitimate confidence, as professional standards, as simply being honest about your own competence. That's exactly why it's the most dangerous fault, not the loudest one — it's built to hide in the gap between real ambition and this.

## Why It Matters

Run the direct test next time a win feels good: separate how much of the satisfaction is in the work itself versus in having outdone someone specific. That split tells you something Lewis says most people never bother checking.

## Evidence / Support

Pride is not satisfied by being rich or clever — it's satisfied by being more so than someone else. Remove the comparison and the pleasure collapses, unlike simple self-respect.

It eats away the possibility of the other virtues coexisting with it — contentment, love, clear thinking about yourself — because it requires constant comparison and therefore constant restlessness.

Lewis does not offer a clean test for where ambition ends and pride begins — the absence of a bright line is itself the warning.

## Meditations (3)

- Pride is not satisfied by having. It's satisfied by having more than.
- Every other vice tells on itself. Pride disguises itself as your own good judgment.
- Take away the comparison, and see what's left of the pleasure.

## Socratic Question

"Think of the last time you felt genuinely satisfied with a win in your business — was the satisfaction in the work itself, or in being ahead of someone?"
