## The Insight

A feeling cannot be willed into existence on command. That single fact is why building a lifelong structure on "being in love" alone was always going to be building on something neither party can guarantee. Being in love is wonderful for getting two people to make a permanent promise — it's just not built to sustain the promise at that same intensity for fifty years. Lewis's marriage is a vow, "one flesh," carried by will and promise through the long stretches where feeling naturally comes and goes.

A promise, unlike a feeling, can be kept regardless of the emotional weather on a given day. That's not a consolation prize for when the feeling fades — it's the actual mechanism a permanent commitment needs, the only kind of foundation strong enough for the job.

## Why It Matters

Name the seasons in your own marriage where the feeling ran thin, specifically, and be honest about what actually carried things through instead. That answer tells you whether you have been running on vow or on borrowed time.

## Evidence / Support

Once made, the vow's binding power does not depend on continuing to feel the original emotion — the whole point of a promise is that it holds precisely in the seasons feeling would not.

Headship, narrowly defined: Lewis assigns the husband a final-decision role for rare genuine deadlock, tied to responsibility for outward dealings, not control — the chapter's most contested claim, acknowledged as such.

The sexual union belongs within the whole permanent commitment, not isolated as recreation — its full meaning only holds together inside the total union.

## Meditations (3)

- The feeling got you to the altar. The promise is what has to stay after the feeling comes and goes.
- A promise exists precisely for the days you would not have made it again that morning.
- What you vowed to build together outlasts whichever mood either of you woke up in.
