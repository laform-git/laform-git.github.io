## The Insight

Imagine paying for a seat to watch a covered plate slowly uncovered on stage. You'd immediately know something had gone wrong with that culture's relationship to food. Lewis's whole argument is that modern fixation on sexual display is the identical disorder — just normalized instead of noticed. The appetite is not shameful. It's oversized, the way any good appetite becomes distorted once it's fed past its right proportion.

That's the reframe worth holding onto: this was never really about purity, it's about proportion. Christianity does not treat the body as low or embarrassing — that view belongs to other philosophies entirely. The rule (abstinence outside marriage, fidelity within) is an attempt to restore a good appetite to its right size, not to suppress something dirty.

## Why It Matters

Chastity is, by Lewis's own admission, the least popular virtue to preach — the pressure to read self-denial as repression is worse now than in his day. Check whether some other appetite in your own life — work, recognition, control — has quietly outgrown its right size the same way he says sex has for the culture at large.

## Evidence / Support

Nobody keeps this rule fully by willpower alone — and that's not incidental. Failing at a rule you cannot keep is often the first place a person discovers they need help beyond effort, setting up the later Faith chapters.

Everyone treats their own particular temptation as the uniquely excusable one — worth naming rather than accepting at face value.

## Meditations (3)

- An appetite does not need to be evil to be disordered; it just needs to have outgrown its place.
- The rule you cannot keep by willpower alone is often the rule doing the most spiritual work.
- Everyone thinks their own temptation is the reasonable exception.

## Socratic Question

"Which temptation do you privately treat as more excusable than you'd allow it to be in anyone else?"
