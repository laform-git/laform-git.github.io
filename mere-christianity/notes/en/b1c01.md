## The Insight

Lewis picks the pettiest possible evidence on purpose. Not war, not murder — a squabbled-over bus seat, a broken promise. The quarrel format itself is the proof: "How'd you like it if someone did that to you?" only works as an argument if both people already agree on a rule neither invented. Nobody has ever successfully ended one of these fights by saying "there's no actual standard here, I just do not like what you did" — because that is not true, and everyone involved knows it is not true.

Lewis is doing something narrower and stranger than "morality exists." He's pointing at a specific, checkable behavior: the excuse. Catch the pattern once and you cannot unsee it — violation of the rule gets defended with reasons ("I was tired," "you started it"), never with a denial that the rule applies. A reason is an appeal to the court. Only someone who accepted the court's authority would bother filing one.

## Why It Matters

This is not abstract. It's the shape of the last argument you actually had with someone. Pull up your own last self-justification and check what it was doing — not "there's no rule," but "the rule should not count against me right now." That gap between the two is the entire chapter.

## Evidence / Support

People rarely deny the standard exists when accused of breaking it — almost always they argue for an exception, a special circumstance that should exempt them this once. That's stronger evidence for the Law than open defiance would be: they still believe the rule, they are just requesting a waiver.

A philosophical observation, not settled doctrine — the "moral argument" for God remains debated among philosophers, though the intuitive force is real.

Two people, or two nations, accusing each other of injustice are not having separate conversations — they are both bowing to the same unseen authority, disagreeing only about who's honoring it.
