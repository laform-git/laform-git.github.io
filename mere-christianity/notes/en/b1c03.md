## The Insight

Picture the conductor of an orchestra. Not a violin, not a horn — the thing that decides which instrument gets to play right now. Lewis wants you to notice that when two impulses fight (run from the danger, or go help the person screaming), something picks a winner, and that something is not itself one of the impulses. Herd instinct and self-preservation are both just instruments. The moral law is the conductor.

Here's the part people skip past too fast: being taught something does not tell you whether it's real or invented. You were taught 7×8=56 by a teacher. You were also taught fairness matters by a parent. Nobody concludes the multiplication table is a cultural preference just because someone taught it to you — so "I was taught to feel guilty about this" proves nothing either way about whether the guilt is tracking something real.

## Why It Matters

Run the actual test on something you believe. Would you still hold this conviction if your upbringing had gone differently? If the honest answer is yes — if you suspect you'd have landed here regardless — that's evidence you are tracking something, not just echoing where you came from.

## Evidence / Support

The conductor does not play an instrument; it picks which one plays. Something plays that role between competing impulses, and it cannot be either impulse.

Cross-cultural check: no tribe in history has been found that admired cowardice and betrayal the way we admire courage and loyalty. Details vary everywhere; the basic shape does not.

A law you constantly break (moral law) behaves differently than a law you cannot break (gravity) — that gap between "ought" and "is" is itself the signal something real, not descriptive, is in play.

## Meditations (3)

- The conductor does not play any instrument, yet the orchestra depends entirely on it.
- You were taught 7 × 8 is 56. You were also taught fairness matters. Only one was ever up for a vote.
- No tribe has ever been found that admired the coward who ran and despised the man who held the line.

## Socratic Question

"Think of the last time you felt genuinely guilty, not just embarrassed or caught. Who wrote the rule you broke — you, your parents, or something you only discovered?"
