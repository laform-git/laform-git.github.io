## The Insight

A mood of doubt can feel exactly as convincing as an actual argument while carrying none of an argument's evidential weight. Lewis's faith, in this first sense, is not refusing to think — it's refusing to let an unargued feeling overturn a conclusion you reached by real reasoning. The mood is real and worth noting. Noting it is a different act than treating it as new evidence about whether the original conclusion still holds.

This kind of faith only becomes necessary after a specific failure: genuinely trying, and failing, to be good through pure self-effort. The moral chapters that came before this one were not a self-improvement program that was supposed to succeed on willpower — their real function was exposing the exact gap this chapter is naming.

## Why It Matters

Name a specific conviction you have actually reasoned your way to, that still goes shaky on a bad day. Ask honestly whether anything changed besides your mood.

## Evidence / Support

Believing a proposition is true is different from relying on it the way you'd rely on a trusted person — faith in the deeper sense means the second kind of commitment.

What faith actually accomplishes once you have it is deliberately left open here — that's Ch12's payoff, not this chapter's.

## Meditations (3)

- A mood can feel exactly like an argument and still not be one.
- Trusting someone is a different act than merely believing a fact about them.
- Trying hard and failing at goodness is not the end of the moral project — it's the beginning of the next one.

## Socratic Question

"What settled conviction of yours gets re-litigated by your mood rather than by any new evidence?"
