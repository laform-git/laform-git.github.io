## The Insight

You already know how to separate the sin from the sinner. You do it for yourself constantly — tired, provoked, misunderstood, full context every time. The Lord's Prayer ties God's forgiveness of you directly to your forgiveness of others, and the entire difficulty of this chapter is just doing that same operation you already do effortlessly, pointed outward instead of inward.

Notice the double standard runs so quietly it does not feel like a double standard. One lenient track reserved for yourself, one strict track for everyone else, and both feel from the inside like simple fairness. The person you are most reluctant to forgive is not the exception to the rule — they are the actual test of whether you have one.

## Why It Matters

Take the grievance currently costing you the most energy right now — a live dispute — and try narrating the other party's failure with the same generous context you'd give your own worst moment. Not to excuse the dispute's resolution, just to see what the exercise actually reveals.

## Evidence / Support

Loving the sinner while hating the sin is a distinction you already make about yourself daily — the difficulty is doing the identical operation toward someone else.

Forgiveness does not require manufacturing affection — it requires willing the person's genuine good, the way you will your own good despite disliking your own faults.

No severity threshold where the command stops — deliberate, since exemptions are exactly where self-excusing habits creep back in.

## Meditations (3)

- You already know how to separate the sin from the sinner — you do it for yourself every day.
- Forgiveness is not a feeling you wait for. It's a will you exercise.
- The person you are most reluctant to forgive is the test case, not the exception.

## Socratic Question

"Whose wrong against you have you been narrating as pure character flaw, with none of the context you'd give your own worst moment?"
