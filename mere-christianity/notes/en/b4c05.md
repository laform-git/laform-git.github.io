## The Insight

A tin soldier's hardness feels like strength. It's actually what's keeping him from being alive. Lewis pictures God turning tin soldiers — creatures merely made, self-contained, separate — into real, living sons, using the image of a toy soldier made into a creature of real flesh. That transformation is genuinely uncomfortable, because it means losing the shell in exchange for something realer but far more exposed: actual feeling, actual vulnerability.

Lewis does not sell this as pure upgrade, and that honesty is what makes the image useful. Real flesh can be wounded in a way tin cannot. That vulnerability is the point, not a flaw in the process. The obstacle was never unclear instructions — it's obstinacy, the tin-soldier self clinging to its own hardness even though that same hardness is exactly what's keeping it from becoming genuinely alive.

## Why It Matters

Name a specific place you have been protecting the tin soldier's safety — in your marriage, at work, in how much you let anyone actually see you struggle — instead of risking the vulnerability of something more alive.

## Evidence / Support

The tin soldier's appeal is real, not imagined — hard, self-contained, feeling nothing has a genuine, if limited, comfort the transformation asks you to give up.

Becoming real costs the shell — gaining sensation and a different kind of dependence is a replacement of the material, not an improvement to the old self.

A deliberately uncomfortable image — Lewis chooses honesty over softening, worth sitting with rather than resolving too quickly.
