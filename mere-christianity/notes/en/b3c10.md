## The Insight

A short horizon makes every setback feel existential — this world is all there is to lose, so every loss reads as final. Lewis's counterintuitive claim is that a longer horizon does the opposite of what you'd expect: it does not make you check out of this world, it frees you to take bigger risks and sustain longer effort in it, because the whole stake was never riding on this world alone. The Christians who did the most good here were consistently the ones most focused on the next world, not the least.

There's a second, quieter argument running underneath: every natural desire corresponds to something real that satisfies it — hunger to food, thirst to water. Something in you keeps wanting a satisfaction nothing in this life ever fully delivers. That persistent gap is not proof the desire is broken. It might be evidence the actual object of it is somewhere else.

## Why It Matters

Check your own planning horizon directly: does it stop at the end of this life, or extend past it? This chapter's claim is that the longer horizon is what makes the shorter-term work strongest, not a distraction pulling energy away from it.

## Evidence / Support

Aim at heaven and earth gets thrown in as a byproduct; aim at earth alone and history suggests you tend to lose both.

Hope is not a license for passivity — Lewis's own examples are people whose eternal focus intensified, not diminished, their practical effectiveness here.

The historical generalization is an observation, not rigorous proof — worth holding with appropriate confidence, not certainty.

## Meditations (3)

- Aim at heaven and earth is thrown in. Aim at earth alone and you risk losing both.
- An unsatisfied desire is not proof desire is broken. It might be evidence the object is somewhere else.
- The longest horizon sharpens the shorter-term work — it does not excuse you from it.
