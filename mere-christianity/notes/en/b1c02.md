## The Insight

Two easy exits get closed off here. "It's just herd instinct" and "it's just social convention" both sound like they dissolve morality into something else, something explainable. Lewis's counter is almost annoyingly simple: you can judge an instinct as wrong (the instinct to run from someone drowning versus the pull to help othem), and you can judge a convention as wrong (a whole society's customs, not just deviations from it). Whatever's doing that judging cannot be either instinct or convention — it's refereeing both.

Notice what actually happens when a moral reformer shows up. They do not say "my culture prefers different customs." They say the old custom was wrong — measured against something neither culture invented. That only makes sense if the standard sits above both, untouched by either explanation.

## Why It Matters

Anytime a moral claim gets waved off with "that's just how you were raised" or "that's just biology," check what's actually being argued. Explaining where a conviction came from was never the same move as showing it's false — and the two keep getting quietly swapped.

## Evidence / Support

Competing instincts — fleeing danger versus running toward someone in danger — need a referee that is not itself one of the competitors. Lewis calls that referee the moral law.

Moral reformers (Lewis's example: abolitionists) claimed one society's morality was actually closer to a real standard than another's — not just different, wrong. That only holds if a real standard exists independent of both cultures.

Still a live philosophical fight (moral realism vs. relativism) — persuasive, not universally settled.

## Meditations (3)

- Explaining where a conviction came from was never the same as showing it's false.
- A moral reformer never argues for different customs — they argue the old one was measured against something real and found wanting.
- When two of your own instincts conflict, something outside both of them is picking the winner.
