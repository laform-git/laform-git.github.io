# The Glucose Journey — A Map of Insulin Resistance Through the Body

> **What this is:** A plain-language story of how glucose and insulin travel through the body, and what goes wrong when the journey breaks down. For the technical mechanism behind any part of this story, see [[Insulin Resistance]].

---

## 1. Eating — Glucose Enters the Blood
Food breaks down into glucose (sugar), which enters the bloodstream. The body now needs to move that sugar out of the blood and into cells, where it gets burned for energy. ✅ Rock solid.

## 2. The Pancreas Sends the Key — Insulin
The pancreas notices blood sugar rising and releases insulin. Think of insulin as a key that unlocks cells so sugar can get inside. Every time sugar goes up, insulin goes up to match it, and the doors open. ✅ Rock solid. (Strictly, insulin opens the doors mainly in muscle and fat cells; the brain and liver take up most of their glucose without needing insulin's key (Arnold 2018).)

## 3. When It Works — Cells Feed, No One Notices
In a healthy body, this happens quietly, all day, every day. Sugar goes up after a meal, insulin opens the doors, cells take in the sugar they need, and both numbers come back down. No symptoms, no problem. ✅ Rock solid.

## 4. The Problem Begins — The Doors Get Harder to Open
If more fuel keeps arriving than the body needs — from too much food overall, frequent refined carbs and sugary drinks, and too little movement — fat starts collecting where it does not belong (liver, muscle, around the organs), and that misplaced fat jams insulin's signal inside the cell. Over time, the cells stop responding as well. This is the start of **insulin resistance**: the key still works, but the lock is getting stiff. 🟢 Strong evidence (Samuel 2016). Genes, age, poor sleep and some medications also contribute. The idea that insulin "knocking louder and more often" is itself the first cause is a hypothesis promoted by lecturers such as Bikman: high insulin can worsen insulin resistance once it exists (Shanik 2008), but whether it starts the process is 🟡 contested.

## 5. The Body Hides the Problem — For Years
The pancreas does not quit. It just makes more insulin to force the doors open anyway. This can work for years — which means blood sugar can look completely normal on a standard test, while insulin is already working overtime behind the scenes to keep it that way. In the Whitehall II study, insulin sensitivity was already falling while fasting glucose crept up only slowly; glucose rose steeply only in the last ~3 years before diabetes was diagnosed (Tabák 2009). This hidden phase is why a normal glucose number can be misleading. 🟢 Strong evidence. (Full timeline in [[Insulin Resistance]].)

## 6. First Stop of Damage — The Liver
Fuel that muscle and fat cells will not take up ends up in the liver. Extra sugar is turned into new fat there, and insulin-resistant fat tissue also leaks extra fatty acids into the blood that the liver soaks up: in people with fatty liver, about 59% of liver fat came from those fatty acids and about 26% from fat newly made from sugar (Donnelly 2005). Some of that fat ships out into the blood as triglycerides; some of it stays and builds up inside the liver itself. That's fatty liver disease (now officially called MASLD, metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (Rinella 2023)) — imagine a warehouse taking in more boxes than it ever ships out. And once fat builds up in the liver, the liver itself becomes even more insulin resistant — making the original problem worse. 🟢 Strong evidence.

## 7. The Fat Does not Stop at the Liver — How the Body Accumulates It
High insulin is not only a "let sugar in" signal — it's also the body's strongest "store fat, do not burn it" signal. In fat tissue that still responds normally, high insulin makes fat cells pull in fat and hold back from releasing it for energy. ✅ Rock solid. As insulin resistance progresses, though, fat tissue itself becomes resistant: it leaks more fatty acids into the blood than it should, and those feed the liver fat in Stop 6 (Samuel 2016). 🟢 Strong evidence. When the safer under-the-skin fat can no longer expand, extra fat spills into the least healthy places: wrapped around the organs (visceral fat) and packed into and around the liver. 🟢 Strong evidence for this spillover pattern; 🟡 contested that high insulin by itself steers fat to these places (a lecturer view, e.g. Bikman, that goes beyond the evidence).

Once fat tissue expands past what it can healthily hold, it starts behaving like an inflamed organ in its own right — releasing inflammatory signals (cytokines) into the bloodstream. This is a distinct mechanism from the liver and heart damage below: it is not just "too much fat sitting somewhere," it's fat tissue actively generating body-wide, low-grade inflammation. That inflammation then makes cells even less responsive to insulin — closing a vicious cycle that reinforces Stops 4–5 from the inside, not just adding a side effect. 🟢 Strong evidence (Hotamisligil 2006).

## 8. Second Stop — The Heart and Blood Vessels
That same rerouted fat raises triglycerides, lowers "good" HDL cholesterol, and turns LDL cholesterol into smaller, denser particles that stick to artery walls more easily. On top of that, high insulin and the inflammation from Stop 7 directly irritate blood vessels and promote plaque buildup — a second and third hit, separate from the cholesterol change itself. (Full detail: [[Sugar vs. Cholesterol Number — Why Insulin Resistance Drives Heart Disease Risk]].) 🟢 Strong evidence for the cholesterol pattern (DeFronzo 1991); 🟡 contested how much high insulin itself, as opposed to insulin resistance and inflammation, directly damages human arteries.

## 9. Blood Pressure Climbs Too
High insulin tells the kidneys to hold onto more salt and water, which raises the total volume of fluid in the blood vessels — like adding more water to a hose (DeFronzo 1975). It is also linked to stiffer vessel walls. Two separate effects, both pushing blood pressure up at the same time. ✅ Rock solid that insulin makes the kidneys keep salt; 🟢 strong evidence that insulin resistance and high blood pressure travel together. 🟡 Contested how much high insulin itself drives long-term hypertension: in obesity, the main drivers are fat pressing on the kidneys, the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system and an overactive sympathetic nervous system, with high insulin one contributor among several (Hall 2015).

## 10. The Most Serious Stop — The Brain
Here the story turns from inconvenient to genuinely serious. Insulin resistance reaches the brain too, and it breaks three separate jobs insulin normally helps the brain do: using energy properly, clearing out its own toxic waste, and building its own cholesterol supply.

**Energy use, impaired.** The brain runs almost entirely on glucose. Most of it enters brain cells through doors that do not need insulin (GLUT1 and GLUT3), but some regions — the hippocampus especially, the brain's main memory-formation center — also carry the insulin-sensitive GLUT4 door, which seems to help during heavy demand such as learning (shown mainly in rodents) (Arnold 2018). In people, higher HOMA-IR goes with lower glucose use in memory-related brain regions and worse memory scores, even before any symptoms (Willette 2015). Short on fuel, cells cannot generate enough ATP (cellular energy currency) to run their normal workload, and mitochondria — the cell's energy-producing machinery — come under chronic stress. 🟢 Strong evidence that insulin resistance goes with lower brain glucose use; 🟡 the exact mechanism in humans is still being worked out.

**Toxin removal, impaired.** The brain has an enzyme, insulin-degrading enzyme (IDE), that does exactly what its name says — but it also happens to be one of the main enzymes that breaks down amyloid-beta, the sticky protein that clumps into the plaques associated with Alzheimer's. When insulin is chronically elevated (the same hyperinsulinemia driving Stops 4–5), IDE gets tied up processing all that excess insulin, leaving less of it free to clear amyloid-beta (Farris 2003). (In people, long-term high blood insulin may actually reduce how much insulin crosses into the brain, so this competition is not proven in humans (Arnold 2018).) The brain's routine cellular "cleanup" process (autophagy — how cells break down and recycle their own damaged parts) also depends on healthy insulin signaling; when that signaling is impaired, cleanup slows down and waste accumulates rather than getting cleared. 🟡 Both mechanisms are well supported in cell and animal studies but only partly confirmed in humans, and it is contested how much they explain Alzheimer's risk versus contributing alongside other causes.

**Cholesterol production, impaired.** This one surprises most people: the brain does not import much cholesterol from the bloodstream at all — the blood-brain barrier mostly blocks it. Instead, the brain manufactures nearly all its own cholesterol locally, mainly in support cells called astrocytes, and uses it to build and repair synapses (the connection points between neurons) and myelin (the insulation around nerve fibers that keeps signals fast and clean). That local cholesterol-making machinery is regulated in part by the same insulin-signaling pathways affected elsewhere in this story. When brain insulin signaling works properly, it helps keep this local supply running to support healthy synapses. When insulin resistance sets in, that support weakens — leaving neurons less able to maintain and repair their own connections. 🟡 Emerging area of research — shown mainly in mice lacking insulin (Suzuki 2010); this mechanism is less firmly established than the energy and toxin-clearance points above, but is an active and increasingly supported line of Alzheimer's research.

Put together: energy-starved cells, a slowed cleanup crew, and a weakened ability to maintain their own physical connections — three separate hits landing on the same tissue at the same time. That combination, not any one alone, is what makes the brain the most serious stop on this journey. 🔴 Interpretation: this three-hit picture is a synthesis; the human evidence is mostly associations. What is well established is the risk: people with type 2 diabetes have about 1.7 times the risk of any dementia and about 1.6 times the risk of Alzheimer's (Gudala 2013). 🟢 Strong evidence. The buildup and dysfunction this produces triggers further inflammation that damages brain cells over time — the same kind of inflammatory pattern already seeded body-wide back in Stop 7. The popular nickname "type 3 diabetes" for this whole picture is a researcher's shorthand, not an official diagnosis.

## 11. The Big Picture
By the end of this journey, one original problem — cells no longer responding well to insulin — has quietly produced what look like several separate diagnoses: fatty liver, expanding visceral fat that fuels its own inflammation, bad cholesterol numbers, high blood pressure, and higher dementia risk. They are not separate problems happening to arrive at the same time. They are different stops on the same trip, all starting from the same stuck door. 🔴 Interpretation: insulin resistance is a strong shared driver, but each of these conditions also has other causes (genes, age, alcohol, kidney disease, high LDL from other causes).

---

## Why This Matters
A normal lab panel checks each stop in isolation — one glucose number, one cholesterol number — without showing that they are connected. Once it's seen as one journey instead of several unrelated numbers, the next step is clearer: improve the door (insulin resistance) — mainly through weight loss, physical activity and, where indicated, medication — and several downstream stops tend to improve together. In the Diabetes Prevention Program, lifestyle change cut progression to type 2 diabetes by 58% (Knowler 2002). 🟢 Strong evidence. It does not replace treating each stop on its own merits (for example, LDL cholesterol or blood pressure that stay high); "every stop improves" would be an overstatement.
