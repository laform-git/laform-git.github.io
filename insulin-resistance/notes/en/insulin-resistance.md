# Insulin Resistance

Insulin resistance is the defining metabolic disease of the modern era. It underlies Type 2 diabetes, obesity, cardiovascular disease, non-alcoholic fatty liver disease and PCOS, and it is linked to Alzheimer's disease ("type 3 diabetes", 🟡 contested framing) and to several cancers (🟡 association, not proven cause). Understanding the mechanism is the foundation of all metabolic health work.

---

## What Insulin Is and What It Does

After you eat, sugar (glucose) fills your blood. Your body wants that sugar *inside* cells where it gets burned for energy. **Insulin is the key** that unlocks your cells so glucose can enter.

- Produced by the beta cells of the pancreas in response to rising blood sugar
- Without insulin working, glucose stays trapped in the bloodstream
- ✅ Rock solid: textbook mechanism, zero dispute

**Normal function:**

1. Glucose enters blood after eating
2. Pancreas detects rise → secretes insulin
3. Insulin binds to receptors on cells → signals cells to take up glucose
4. Liver converts excess glucose to glycogen (short-term storage) or fat (long-term)
5. Blood glucose returns to baseline

Insulin also:

- Suppresses fat burning (lipolysis) — fat cannot be burned when insulin is elevated
- Drives protein synthesis (anabolic signal)
- Regulates electrolytes (kidneys retain sodium under insulin)

---

## What Insulin Resistance Means

Over time, cells stop responding well to insulin. The lock stops working properly.

- Glucose can't enter cells easily, so it piles up in the blood
- Body's fix: produce *more* insulin to force the doors open
- This compensates for years — blood sugar can look normal while resistance is already serious
- **Fasting insulin is an earlier warning signal than HbA1c or fasting glucose**
- 🟢 Strong evidence: fasting insulin as earlier marker than standard blood sugar tests

---

## How It Develops — Timeline

- Resistance begins → Insulin rises → Blood sugar stays normal (hidden phase)
- Years pass → Insulin can no longer compensate → Blood sugar finally rises
- Prediabetes shows on tests — but resistance started much earlier

| Phase | What's Happening | Symptoms |
|---|---|---|
| Pre-diabetic insulin resistance | Insulin high, glucose still normal | Fatigue after meals, brain fog, abdominal fat gain |
| Impaired fasting glucose | Liver producing excess glucose overnight | Fasting glucose 100–125 mg/dL |
| Prediabetes | Significant beta cell strain | HbA1c 5.7–6.4%, increased hunger, fatigue |
| Type 2 Diabetes | Beta cell failure | Fasting glucose >126, HbA1c >6.5% |

**Years before diagnosis:** insulin resistance begins accumulating 10–15 years before blood glucose becomes abnormal. Standard care only measures glucose — by the time glucose is elevated, insulin has been dysregulated for a decade. 🟢 Strong evidence for the long hidden phase; the exact number of years varies by person.

## Why Cells Become Resistant — 3 Mechanisms

### 1. The Cell Is Already Full

- Cells overfull with fuel deliberately downregulate insulin response — self-protection, not a defect
- 🟢 Strong evidence: the cellular overflow model

### 2. Fat in the Wrong Place (The Most Important One)

- Fat cells overfill → fat leaks into muscle and liver cells
- That misplaced fat produces two damaging molecules:
    - **DAG (diacylglycerol)** → activates PKCθ/ε → jams the insulin signal at IRS-1
    - **Ceramide** → activates PP2A → strips Akt of its activation tags
- These break the insulin signaling chain at its two most critical relay points
- 🟢 Strong evidence: DAG-PKC and ceramide pathways

### 3. Too Much Insulin, Too Often

- Constant high insulin wears out the cell's response
- Creates a vicious cycle: more insulin → cells listen less → body makes even more insulin
- 🟢 Strong evidence for the cycle itself

### The Carbohydrate Cascade (Bikman / Lustig view)

In this view insulin resistance is not primarily caused by dietary fat but by chronic, repeated elevation of insulin from excessive carbohydrate consumption — particularly refined carbohydrates and fructose. 🔴 Advocacy as *the* primary cause; the steps themselves are mostly well supported.

1. **Repeated glucose spikes** → repeated insulin spikes
2. **Cells downregulate insulin receptors** (adaptation to chronic signal)
3. **More insulin required** to produce the same glucose uptake → pancreas compensates with hyperinsulinemia
4. **Fat cells resist insulin** → fatty acids flood the bloodstream → deposit in liver and muscle
5. **Ectopic fat in liver** → liver itself becomes insulin resistant → continues producing glucose even when glucose is already high (hepatic glucose output failure)
6. **Muscle insulin resistance** → primary site of glucose disposal is impaired
7. **Pancreas exhaustion** → beta cells fail → Type 2 diabetes

**The fructose problem:** fructose is metabolized almost entirely in the liver. Unlike glucose, it bypasses normal feedback mechanisms and is readily converted to fat (de novo lipogenesis). High fructose intake → fatty liver → accelerates insulin resistance. 🟢 Strong evidence at high intakes (sugary drinks, HFCS); whole fruit is a different case.

### Where Experts Disagree

- **Bikman:** Mechanism 3 (hyperinsulinemia) is the primary driver → 🔴 Advocacy
- **Mainstream:** Mechanisms 1 & 2 (energy excess, ectopic fat) dominate → 🟡 Contested
- **Most balanced:** All three interact → 🟢 Strong evidence

## Practical Takeaway

If fasting insulin is elevated, insulin resistance is already present — even if every blood sugar number looks normal.

## Measuring It

**Best single test: fasting insulin** (not routinely ordered — must be specifically requested). ✅ Rock solid that it is informative; the cutoffs below are 🟡 contested and tighter than most lab reference ranges.

- Optimal: <5 µIU/mL
- Borderline: 5–10 µIU/mL
- Resistant: >10 µIU/mL

### HOMA-IR (Worked Example)

Formula: (fasting glucose mg/dL × fasting insulin uIU/mL) / 405

- **Under 1.0:** optimal — Bikman's tighter target. 🔴 Advocacy-tier target, not a diagnostic consensus cutoff.
- **1.0–1.9:** mild, by looser clinical convention. 🟡 Contested — cutoffs vary by lab and study.
- **≥1.9–2.5+:** significant resistance by most commonly cited thresholds. 🟡 Contested — no single universal cutoff exists.

**Limitation:** one fasting draw is a snapshot, not a trend — real day-to-day noise exists.

HbA1c and fasting glucose alone miss early insulin resistance. Fasting insulin is the key.

---

## Key Numbers to Track

- **Fasting insulin:** target <5 µIU/mL
- **HOMA-IR:** target <1.0
- **Triglycerides:** target <100 mg/dL (high TG = carb overconsumption marker)
- **HDL:** target >60 mg/dL (low HDL = insulin resistance marker)
- **Triglyceride:HDL ratio:** target <2.0 — best single predictor of insulin resistance from a standard lipid panel (🟢 strong evidence as a screening marker; weaker in some ethnic groups)
- **Waist circumference:** most accessible physical marker; excess visceral fat drives IR

These are optimal-range targets, tighter than standard lab "normal". 🟡 Contested as universal targets.

## Systemic Reach — Organ by Organ

Insulin resistance is not a blood-sugar-only condition; chronically elevated insulin signals reach essentially every tissue with an insulin receptor.

- **Liver:** drives de novo lipogenesis → fatty liver, feeds back into worse hepatic insulin resistance. 🟢 Strong evidence. See [[Sugar vs. Cholesterol Number — Why Insulin Resistance Drives Heart Disease Risk]].
- **Fat tissue:** promotes storage, disrupts the leptin/adiponectin signaling balance. 🟢 Strong evidence.
- **Heart & vessels:** elevated insulin promotes vascular inflammation and plaque formation independent of LDL level alone. 🟢 Strong evidence.
- **Kidneys:** insulin drives sodium reabsorption, raising blood pressure and long-term kidney strain. 🟢 Strong evidence (mechanism below).
- **Skin:** acanthosis nigricans (dark, velvety skin patches, usually neck/armpits) is a visible marker of significantly elevated insulin. ✅ Rock solid, well-documented clinical sign.
- **Reproductive/hormonal:** in women, insulin resistance impairs aromatase activity, disrupting ovulation (PCOS mechanism). In men, the pathway is indirect — elevated insulin and adiposity lower SHBG (sex hormone binding globulin) and increase aromatization of testosterone to estradiol in fat tissue; this is a fat-tissue and SHBG effect, not a direct pancreatic or testicular one. 🟢 Strong evidence, corrected framing (see Key Corrections below).

---

## Insulin Resistance and the Brain

- Insulin resistance impairs glucose uptake in brain tissue, reducing available cellular energy in affected regions. 🟢 Strong evidence for the glucose-uptake mechanism itself.
- The label **"type 3 diabetes"** for Alzheimer's disease is a researcher-coined framing term describing this overlap — it is not a formal diagnostic category and the causal strength (mechanism vs. correlation vs. shared risk factor) remains actively debated. 🟡 Contested — treat the mechanism as plausible and evidence-supported, the "type 3 diabetes" label itself as shorthand, not settled fact.
- Normal age-related slowing (word-finding, processing speed) is expected and distinct from this pathway — this mechanism describes an added risk factor, not a claim that all memory change is insulin-driven.

---

## Blood Pressure: Two Additive Levers, Not Competing Ones

- **Sodium/fluid mechanism:** elevated insulin directly increases kidney sodium reabsorption, raising blood volume. 🟢 Strong evidence.
- **Vascular stiffness mechanism:** chronic hyperinsulinemia promotes vascular smooth muscle changes that reduce vessel flexibility. 🟢 Strong evidence.
- **Correction:** dietary salt and insulin resistance are not alternative explanations for the same blood pressure — both raise blood volume through sodium retention via different pathways, and their effects are additive. Someone can have normal salt intake and still show insulin-driven sodium retention, or have both mechanisms compounding.

---

## Fatty Liver: The Progression Ladder

Simple fat accumulation (steatosis) → NASH (steatohepatitis: inflammation + active cell damage) → fibrosis (scarring) → cirrhosis (functional liver failure). 🟢 Strong evidence for the sequence; 🟡 contested how reliably/quickly any one person progresses through it. Cross-link: [[Sugar vs. Cholesterol Number — Why Insulin Resistance Drives Heart Disease Risk]].

---

## Periodontal Disease — A Third Feed Into the Same Inflammatory Marker

Gum disease bacteria entering the bloodstream through inflamed gum tissue trigger vascular inflammation through a distinct pathway from insulin resistance — but both converge on the same downstream marker. 🟢 Strong evidence for the periodontal-cardiovascular inflammatory link.

## Reversal Protocol

### Dietary (most powerful lever)

| Strategy | Mechanism | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Low-carbohydrate / ketogenic diet | Eliminates glucose spikes; forces fat burning; lowers fasting insulin rapidly | 🟢 Strong |
| Time-restricted eating (16:8) | Extends fasting window; insulin drops to baseline; promotes glycogen depletion | 🟡 Mixed — benefit largely tracks weight loss |
| Eliminate refined carbs & sugar | Removes primary driver; most impactful single change | 🟢 Strong |
| Eliminate fructose | Removes accelerant (HFCS, fruit juice, agave) | 🟢 Strong for added fructose |
| Increase protein | Keeps satiety without spiking glucose; preserves muscle during fat loss | 🟢 Strong (see correction below) |

### Exercise (second most powerful lever)

✅ **Resistance training** is the single best exercise intervention for insulin resistance:

- Muscle is the largest glucose disposal organ
- Each pound of muscle = permanent increase in glucose disposal capacity
- Resistance training creates GLUT4 translocation — glucose enters muscle without insulin (insulin-independent pathway)

🟢 **Zone 2 cardio** — improves mitochondrial density; increases ability to oxidize fat and glucose; reduces liver fat.

### Supplements with evidence

| Supplement | Effect | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Berberine | Activates AMPK; lowers fasting glucose; comparable to metformin in some trials | 🟢 Strong |
| Magnesium glycinate | Cofactor for insulin signaling; deficiency worsens IR | 🟢 Strong |
| Inositol (myo-inositol) | Insulin sensitizer; particularly studied in PCOS | 🟢 Strong (mostly PCOS data) |
| Chromium | Enhances insulin receptor signaling | 🟡 Moderate |
| Vitamin D | Deficiency correlates with IR; supplementation may improve sensitivity | 🟡 Mixed — strong correlation, inconsistent trial benefit |

## Key Corrections (from initial Grok draft and the merge)

- "Insulin resistance lowers testosterone in men" — directionally right but incomplete as originally stated. The mechanism runs through reduced SHBG and increased fat-tissue aromatization of testosterone into estradiol, not a direct effect on testosterone-producing cells.
- "Type 3 diabetes" was stated as established fact — corrected above to 🟡 contested framing term, not a formal diagnosis.
- "Salt vs. insulin resistance" was framed as competing explanations for blood pressure — corrected above to additive, independent mechanisms.
- "Carbohydrate overconsumption is *the* cause" — corrected to Bikman/Lustig's view (🔴 advocacy); the mainstream view weights energy excess and ectopic fat, and the balanced view is that all three mechanisms interact.
- "Underlies most cancers" — corrected to an association with several cancers, not a proven cause.
- "Protein doesn't spike insulin" — protein does raise insulin, but alongside glucagon and without the glucose spike, so it doesn't drive the same cycle.
- "Vitamin D supplementation improves sensitivity (strong)" — corrected to mixed: the deficiency link is strong, the trial benefit is inconsistent.
