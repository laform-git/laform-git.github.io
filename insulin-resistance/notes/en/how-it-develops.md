# Insulin Resistance — How It Develops

**In plain words:** insulin is the key that lets sugar from your blood into your cells. With insulin resistance, the locks get stiff. The body keeps working, but only by making more and more insulin. This can go on quietly for years before a blood test shows anything.

Insulin resistance does not switch on overnight. It builds slowly, often for ten years or more, before blood sugar itself looks abnormal (Warram 1990; Tabák 2009). 🟢 The steps below are the order usually taught. In real people they overlap, the order varies, and experts still debate which part of the body fails first. 🟡

1. **Sugar spikes, then insulin spikes.** Refined carbohydrates and sugar raise blood sugar quickly, and the body answers with large bursts of insulin, again and again. 🟡 This "insulin comes first" idea is contested. Most mainstream reviews put more weight on eating more calories than you burn, inactivity, genes, and fat building up in the liver and muscles (Petersen & Shulman 2018). High insulin is probably both a result of insulin resistance and a cause of it (Shanik 2008).
2. **Cells turn the volume down.** When the insulin signal is loud all the time, cells reduce the number of insulin receptors (the "locks" on the cell surface). It is a normal reaction, the way your ears stop noticing a constant noise (Gavin 1974). 🟢 This explains much of the mildest stage. As blood sugar rises further, the main problem moves *inside* the cell, past the lock (Kolterman 1981). The Molecular Cascade note explains that part.
3. **The pancreas works overtime.** The pancreas makes even more insulin to push the same amount of sugar into cells. Doctors call this compensatory hyperinsulinemia (too much insulin in the blood). Many describe it as the real start of the disease, years before routine sugar tests catch it. 🟡 (Whether high insulin comes first or follows the resistance is still debated: Shanik 2008.) Fasting blood sugar still looks "normal", because the pancreas is winning the fight, at a cost. 🟢 (Kahn 2003)
4. **Fat cells stop listening too.** Normally insulin tells fat cells to hold on to their fat. When they become resistant, fat leaks into the blood and ends up stored in the liver and muscles, where it does not belong (doctors call this ectopic fat). 🟢 (Petersen & Shulman 2018)
5. **The liver stops listening.** The liver keeps making and releasing sugar even when blood sugar is already high, because it no longer "hears" insulin telling it to stop. The extra fat from step 4 makes this worse. 🟢 (Petersen & Shulman 2018)
6. **Muscles stop soaking up sugar.** Muscle is where the body normally stores most of the sugar insulin moves out of the blood, especially in people who do strength training. When muscle becomes resistant, that big sponge stops working well. In type 2 diabetes, muscle accounts for most of the body's trouble clearing sugar (DeFronzo 2009). ✅ A note on order: in young, lean people with insulin resistance, muscle is often one of the *first* places to fail. Sugar from meals then gets sent to the liver and turned into fat (Petersen 2007). So this step often comes first, not sixth. 🟢
7. **The pancreas wears out.** The insulin-making cells of the pancreas (beta cells) finally tire out and fail. That collapse is what shows up as type 2 diabetes on a standard blood test. 🟢 The decline starts earlier than "finally" suggests. People with high fasting sugar or type 2 diabetes have roughly 40–63% fewer beta cells when examined after death (Butler 2003), and inherited weakness of these cells largely decides who goes on to diabetes (Kahn 2003).

**Why routine blood tests miss it.** Diabetes and prediabetes are diagnosed by blood sugar and HbA1c (a three-month sugar average), not by insulin (ADA 2026). ✅ So most of the overtime phase, often years, does not show up on routine bloodwork. 🟢 Two other tests, fasting insulin and HOMA-IR (a score calculated from fasting sugar and insulin), can catch it earlier. 🟡 But they are mainly research tools: labs measure insulin in different ways, there is no agreed cutoff, and a single HOMA-IR result can vary a lot from one test to the next (about 31%) (Matthews 1985; Wallace 2004). Fasting sugar and HbA1c climb sharply mainly once the pancreas can no longer keep up (Tabák 2009). 🟢

**The point to remember: normal blood sugar does not always mean normal insulin. The body can hide this problem for years by working harder.**
