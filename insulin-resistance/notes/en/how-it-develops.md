# Insulin Resistance — How It Develops
Insulin resistance is not a switch that flips — it is a cascade that can build silently for years, often a decade or more, before blood glucose itself becomes abnormal (Warram 1990; Tabák 2009). 🟢 The steps below are the commonly taught sequence; in real people they overlap, their order varies, and which tissue fails first is still debated. 🟡

1. **Repeated glucose spikes** (from refined carbohydrate and sugar intake) force repeated, large insulin spikes. 🟡 This "insulin-first" starting point is contested: mainstream reviews put the main weight on chronic calorie surplus, inactivity, genetics and fat accumulating in liver and muscle (Petersen & Shulman 2018); high insulin is best seen as both a result and a driver of insulin resistance (Shanik 2008).
2. **Cells downregulate their insulin receptors** — a normal adaptive response to a chronically loud signal, the same way ears turn down sensitivity to a constant noise (Gavin 1974). 🟢 Fewer receptors can explain much of the mildest stage (impaired glucose tolerance), but as glucose rises the dominant defect moves *inside* the cell, past the receptor (Kolterman 1981) — see the Molecular Cascade note.
3. **The pancreas compensates** by secreting even more insulin to force the same glucose uptake — this stage, compensatory hyperinsulinemia, is often described as where the disease begins, years before routine glucose tests flag it. 🟡 (Whether high insulin comes first or follows resistance is still debated — Shanik 2008.) Fasting glucose still looks "normal" because the pancreas is winning the fight, at a cost. 🟢 (Kahn 2003)
4. **Fat cells become resistant too** — insulin can no longer suppress lipolysis effectively, so fatty acids flood the bloodstream and deposit in the liver and muscle (ectopic fat — fat stored where it does not belong). 🟢 (Petersen & Shulman 2018)
5. **The liver becomes insulin resistant** and keeps producing glucose even when blood glucose is already high, because it can no longer "hear" insulin's signal to stop — helped by the extra fatty acids from step 4, which push liver glucose production. 🟢 (Petersen & Shulman 2018)
6. **Muscle, the body's primary site of insulin-stimulated glucose disposal, becomes resistant** — the tissue most able to soak up glucose (especially with resistance training) stops doing so efficiently; in type 2 diabetes muscle accounts for most of the defect in whole-body glucose disposal (DeFronzo 2009). ✅ Note on order: in young, lean insulin-resistant people, muscle resistance is often one of the *earliest* defects, diverting meal carbohydrate into fat production in the liver (Petersen 2007) — so this step frequently comes first, not sixth. 🟢
7. **Beta cells eventually exhaust themselves** trying to keep up with rising demand, and fail — this final collapse is what shows up as Type 2 diabetes on a standard lab panel. 🟢 Beta-cell decline starts earlier than "final" suggests: people with impaired fasting glucose or type 2 diabetes have roughly 40–63% less beta-cell volume at autopsy (Butler 2003), and inherited beta-cell weakness largely decides who progresses (Kahn 2003).

The critical diagnostic point: because diabetes and prediabetes are diagnosed with glucose and HbA1c, not insulin (ADA 2026) ✅, much of the compensatory phase — often years — is invisible to routine bloodwork. 🟢 Fasting insulin and HOMA-IR can catch it earlier 🟡, but they are best for research and population studies: insulin assays are not standardized, there is no universally agreed cutoff, and a single HOMA-IR value has low precision (CV about 31%) (Matthews 1985; Wallace 2004). Fasting glucose and HbA1c rise steeply mainly once beta-cell compensation is failing (Tabák 2009). 🟢
