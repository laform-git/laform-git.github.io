# Insulin Resistance — How It Develops
Insulin resistance is not a switch that flips — it is a cascade that accumulates silently for 10–15 years before blood glucose itself becomes abnormal. The mechanism runs in a fixed sequence:

1. **Repeated glucose spikes** (from refined carbohydrate and sugar intake) force repeated, large insulin spikes.
2. **Cells downregulate their insulin receptors** — a normal adaptive response to a chronically loud signal, the same way ears turn down sensitivity to a constant noise.
3. **The pancreas compensates** by secreting even more insulin to force the same glucose uptake — this stage, compensatory hyperinsulinemia, is where the disease actually begins, years before any lab test flags it. Fasting glucose still looks "normal" because the pancreas is winning the fight, at a cost.
4. **Fat cells become resistant too** — insulin can no longer suppress lipolysis effectively, so fatty acids flood the bloodstream and deposit in the liver and muscle (ectopic fat — fat stored where it does not belong).
5. **The liver becomes insulin resistant** and keeps producing glucose even when blood glucose is already high, because it can no longer "hear" insulin's signal to stop.
6. **Muscle, the body's primary site of glucose disposal, becomes resistant** — the tissue most able to soak up glucose (especially with resistance training) stops doing so efficiently.
7. **Beta cells eventually exhaust themselves** trying to keep up with rising demand, and fail — this final collapse is what shows up as Type 2 diabetes on a standard lab panel.

The critical diagnostic point: because standard care measures glucose, not insulin, the entire compensatory phase (years 1 through roughly 10) is invisible to routine bloodwork. Fasting insulin and HOMA-IR catch it early; fasting glucose and HbA1c catch it only after the pancreas has already started to lose.
