# GBH-4 Grace and Mission

> **Series:** GBH · Lecture 4 of 6 · Hub: [[GBH-0 God, the Bible and Humanity (Series Hub)]] · Previous: [[GBH-3 Law and True Freedom]]

## 1 · Anchor

**David is singer, warrior and priest in one, and so he points ahead to Christ. And no one in the Bible meets God without the same three steps: grace received, sin admitted, mission given.**

*One image to carry:* David, stripped of armor, dancing before the Ark in a priest's garment.

## 2 · Pocket Version (15-second re-read)

David leads as sweet singer, warrior and priest, anticipating Jesus as prophet, king and high priest. His affair with Bathsheba shows what happens when he stops listening to God and starts acting like God. Elijah's contest on Carmel shows that false gods never answer, because only the true God can satisfy the heart's infinite longing.

Isaiah's call and Peter's call in Luke 5 share the same three-step pattern: the unbidden breakthrough of grace, the honest admission of sin, and then a mission, "your life is not about you."

## 3 · Scripture text (Douay-Rheims)

**2 Kings (2 Samuel) 23:1 (summary; Douay-Rheims numbers this 2 Kings).** David is called "the sweet psalmist of Israel."

**Isaias (Isaiah) 6:1-8.** "In the year that king Ozias died, I saw the Lord sitting upon a throne high and elevated... And they cried one to another, and said: Holy, holy, holy, the Lord God of hosts, all the earth is full of his glory... And one of the seraphims flew to me, and in his hand was a live coal, which he had taken with the tongs off the altar."

**Luke 5:8 (summary, with direct quote).** After the miraculous catch of fish, "Simon Peter... fell down at Jesus' knees, saying: Depart from me, for I am a sinful man, O Lord."

*Verified against [drbo.org](http://drbo.org). Note the Douay-Rheims numbers 1 and 2 Samuel as 1 and 2 Kings, and spells Isaiah as "Isaias" and Uzziah as "Ozias."*

## 4 · Synthesis: Text and Setting

Barron treats David as the pivot of the whole Old Testament, looking back to Adam as a kind of king and forward to Jesus, "son of David."

He organizes David's life around the three biblical offices: prophet (David the singer, who moves people through language, as Lincoln, Churchill and Martin Luther King later would), king (David the warrior against Goliath, fighting "naked," trusting only in God), and priest (David dancing before the Ark, bringing right worship to the center of the nation).

The Bathsheba episode (2 Samuel 11) shows David failing precisely when he stays home from battle and stops listening to God.

From there Barron moves to Elijah's contest with the prophets of Baal at Mount Carmel (1 Kings 18, Douay 3 Kings 18), using it to illustrate that false worship never answers, however much effort or even self-harm is poured into it. He closes with Isaiah's prophetic call (Isaiah 6) and its New Testament parallel, the call of Simon Peter (Luke 5), to establish a recurring three-step pattern of the spiritual life: grace, acknowledged sin, and mission.

## 5 · The Passage Explained, by Beat

**Beat 1 — David the sweet singer.** Called "the sweet psalmist of Israel" (2 Samuel/2 Kings 23:1), David leads through language and song, as Barron says Lincoln, Churchill and Martin Luther King later did. He cites Psalm 137 ("How could we sing the song of Zion on alien soil?") as a warning against believers today going silent.

🕯 **Reflect:** Have I "hung up my harp" on some truth of the faith I used to speak freely?

**Beat 2 — David the warrior, facing Goliath unarmed.** David refuses Saul's armor and faces Goliath with nothing but trust in God. Barron reads Goliath as worldly power, and David's nakedness as a complete lack of worldly defenses.

🕯 **Reflect:** Where am I relying on "armor" rather than trust?

**Beat 3 — David and Bathsheba (2 Samuel 11).** "In the spring, the time when kings go out to battle," David stays home, naps, and from the rooftop takes another man's wife. Barron: this is the moment David stops listening to God and starts acting like God.

🕯 **Reflect:** Where have I quietly stopped listening and started deciding for myself what I'm entitled to?

**Beat 4 — David the priest, dancing before the Ark.** Wearing an ephod, a priestly garment, David dances before the Ark as it enters Jerusalem. Barron reads this as Adam's walk with God in Eden restored: humanity moving in rhythm with the Lord.

🕯 **Reflect:** What would it look like for my own worship to be this unguarded?

**Beat 5 — Elijah at Carmel (1 Kings 18, Douay 3 Kings 18).** The prophets of Baal dance, cry out, and cut themselves, and "there was no voice, no answer, no response." Barron reads this as the pattern of addiction to wealth, pleasure, power or honor (Aquinas's four substitutes for God): real effort, real pain, no satisfaction, because only God can fill the "infinite cavern" John of the Cross describes.

🕯 **Reflect:** Where have I kept "dancing around an altar" that never answers?

**Beat 6 — Isaiah's call and Peter's call.** Isaiah sees the Lord "high and lofty," cries "Woe is me, I am a man of unclean lips," is cleansed by a coal from the altar, and then hears, "Whom shall I send?" Peter, after the miraculous catch, falls at Jesus's knees saying "Depart from me, for I am a sinful man," and is told, "you will be catching men." Barron: grace comes unbidden, then comes honest acknowledgment of sin, then always, mission.

🕯 **Reflect:** Where in my own story can I name these three steps: an unbidden grace, an honest "I am a sinner," and a mission that followed?

## 6 · Meditations

**Meditation A — The boat going into deep water.** Picture your own boat, the one thing in your life that feels like your livelihood or your control. See Jesus step into it uninvited and say, "put out into the deep." Feel the reluctance: you know these waters, you fished all night, nothing came. Let him ask anyway. Picture the nets breaking under the weight of the catch. Ask what "deep water" you have been avoiding.

**Meditation B — The coal on the lips.** See yourself in the temple with Isaiah, overwhelmed, saying "Woe is me." Watch the seraph approach with a burning coal from the altar. Feel the coal touch your lips, not to punish, but to cleanse, and hear: "your guilt has departed, your sin is blotted out." Then hear the question: "Whom shall I send?" Let your own answer form slowly, honestly, in your own words.

## 7 · Doctrine (evidence-labeled)

**1. David as singer, warrior, priest, prefiguring Christ as prophet, king and priest.** 🟡 A long-standing typological reading (David as a picture of Christ in advance; patristic in origin, used by Barron as his own synthesis), not a single defined dogma, though the Church's teaching on Christ's threefold office (CCC 783-786, munus triplex, Latin for "threefold office") is itself solemn teaching, rooted in Lumen Gentium 31 and earlier tradition.

**2. "Holy, holy, holy, the Lord God of hosts, all the earth is full of his glory" (Isaiah 6:3).** ✅ Scripture, verified. This text is also the direct source of the Sanctus sung at every Mass.

**3. God is not one of four finite substitutes (wealth, pleasure, power, honor).** 🟡 Barron attributes this fourfold scheme to Aquinas; it echoes themes in the Summa (e.g., ST I-II q.2, on whether happiness consists in wealth, honor, fame/glory, or power, all of which Aquinas treats and rejects as the final end) though Barron's precise four-term list as stated in the lecture was not independently verified against a single citation.

🕯 **Reflect:** Which of the four is my own most practiced "altar"?

**4. The three-step pattern: grace, acknowledged sin, mission.** 🟡 Barron's own synthesis drawn from parallel reading of Isaiah 6 and Luke 5; the individual elements (unmerited grace, the call to recognize sin, being sent on mission) are each solidly scriptural and traditional, but the "three-step pattern" as a named structure is his teaching tool, not a doctrinal formula.

**5. No one receives an experience of God without a mission.** 🟡 A recurring pattern Barron names across many texts (Moses, Isaiah, Peter); consonant with, but not itself, Church teaching on vocation and mission (cf. Christifideles Laici on the universal call to the lay apostolate).

## 8 · Key Corrections

- **David's sin with Bathsheba is not glossed over or minimized** by Barron; he reads it precisely as a warning about ceasing to listen to God.
- **The contest at Carmel is not primarily a "my god is bigger than your god" story**, in Barron's reading, but an illustration of the psychological and spiritual dynamics of addiction and idolatry.
- **Grace does not leave the convert dwelling on guilt.** Barron is explicit: God's interest, after the honest "I am a sinner," is cleansing and sending, not prolonged self-reproach.
- **"The glory of God is a human being fully alive"** (quoted from Irenaeus in lecture 1) continues to frame these call narratives: the point of the fire and the coal is to make Isaiah more alive, not to destroy him.

## 9 · Why This Matters

The three offices in David, and the three-step pattern in the call narratives, both describe a single rhythm. Worship (right relationship to God) grounds right action and right leadership. And every real encounter with God moves a person from unearned grace, through honest self-knowledge, into mission.

No stage can be skipped. A mission without honest self-knowledge is presumption, and self-knowledge without mission curdles into despair.

**Barron's line for the whole lecture: "your life is not about you."**
