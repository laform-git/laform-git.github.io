# GBH-6 God's Transformative Word

> **Series:** GBH · Lecture 6 of 6 (final) · Hub: [[GBH-0 God, the Bible and Humanity (Series Hub)]] · Previous: [[GBH-5 Contrasting Powers]]

## 1 · Anchor

**The Beatitudes name the cure for addiction to wealth, pleasure, power and honor. Love of enemies is the proof that God's own life has entered us. And the cross is God going all the way down into death itself, a message only the Resurrection could ever justify preaching.**

*One image to carry:* a coal from the altar, not to burn away, but to cleanse and send.

## 2 · Pocket Version (15-second re-read)

The Beatitudes can be read as the opposite of the Baal-worshippers hopping around their altars: "blessed are the poor in spirit" if wealth is your addiction, "blessed are the meek" if power is, "blessed are those who mourn" if pleasure is, "blessed are you when persecuted" if honor is.

Freed from those addictions, mercy flows through you, which is what the positive Beatitudes describe. The hardest test of love is loving enemies, because it proves God's own kind of love, which asks nothing back, has entered you.

Turning the other cheek and giving the second garment are not passivity but a provocative, nonviolent refusal to cooperate with evil, as Gandhi, King, Tutu and John Paul II lived out.

Jesus goes all the way down, into betrayal, injustice, torture and death, so that divine mercy can reach every part of human experience. And the whole message collapses without the Resurrection, which the apostles insist on as a fact, not a myth.

## 3 · Scripture text (Douay-Rheims)

**Matthew 5:3-10 (Beatitudes).** "Blessed are the poor in spirit: for theirs is the kingdom of heaven. Blessed are the meek: for they shall possess the land. Blessed are they that mourn: for they shall be comforted. Blessed are they that hunger and thirst after justice: for they shall have their fill... Blessed are they that suffer persecution for justice' sake: for theirs is the kingdom of heaven."

**Matthew 5:38-39 (summary).** "You have heard that it hath been said, An eye for an eye, and a tooth for a tooth: But I say to you not to resist evil: but if one strike thee on thy right cheek, turn to him also the other."

**Philippians 2:7-8.** "He emptied himself, taking the form of a servant... he humbled himself, becoming obedient unto death, even to the death of the cross."

**Mark 15:34.** "And at the ninth hour, Jesus cried out with a loud voice, saying: Eloi, Eloi, lamma sabacthani? Which is, being interpreted, My God, my God, why hast thou forsaken me?"

**Mark 1:1.** "The beginning of the gospel of Jesus Christ, the Son of God."

*Verified against [drbo.org](http://drbo.org); note the Douay-Rheims reads "justice" where many English Bibles read "righteousness," and "charity" where others read "love" (1 Corinthians 13:13, used below).*

## 4 · Synthesis: Text and Setting

Barron closes the course at the Sermon on the Mount (Matthew 5-7), which he treats, citing the New Testament scholar Richard Bauckham's Jesus and the Eyewitnesses, as very likely close to Jesus's own habitual teaching, memorized early by his followers.

The setting matters: Jesus goes up a mountain (echoing Sinai; he is the new Moses) and sits to teach, the ancient posture of authority, the reverse of a modern lecture hall.

Barron reads the Beatitudes against the backdrop of lecture 4's Baal-worshippers and Aquinas's four substitutes for God (wealth, pleasure, power, honor): each "negative" Beatitude blesses freedom from one of the four addictions, and each "positive" Beatitude describes what flows once a person is free.

He then turns to the sermon's rhetorical center, the command to love enemies, as the decisive test of real love, and reads the "turn the other cheek" sayings as nonviolent resistance rather than passivity, with Gandhi, King, Tutu and John Paul II as modern examples.

He closes the whole course with the downward trajectory of the Incarnation into the cross, Jesus's cry of abandonment, and the claim, resting entirely on the Resurrection, that this crucified man is Israel's Messiah and "Son of God," a title deliberately taken from Caesar.

## 5 · The Passage Explained, by Beat

**Beat 1 — "Blessed are the poor in spirit," against wealth.** Barron tells of a wealthy, successful man who told him, "All my dreams have come true, and I'm miserable." How lucky, Jesus says, if you are not addicted to wealth.

🕯 **Reflect:** If my own dreams of wealth came entirely true, would I expect to be satisfied, and is that expectation realistic?

**Beat 2 — "Blessed are the meek," against power.** Barron uses Tolkien's Ring of Power, and Gandalf's own visible desire for it, as an image of power's universal pull. "How lucky you are if you're not addicted to power."

🕯 **Reflect:** Where have I sacrificed something good, a friendship, honesty, in order to gain or keep some form of control?

**Beat 3 — "Blessed are they that mourn," against pleasure.** Pleasure, Barron says, may be the addiction that does the most damage in his pastoral experience. "How lucky you are if you mourn," meaning: if you are not addicted to pleasure.

🕯 **Reflect:** Is there a pleasure I return to for relief that has quietly become a master rather than a gift?

**Beat 4 — "Blessed are you when persecuted," against honor.** Barron: honor addicts are radically unfree, dependent on others the way an addict depends on a dealer. The reward for releasing honor is being "transposed to a higher key," even now, not only in an afterlife.

🕯 **Reflect:** How much of what I do is secretly aimed at being praised or respected?

**Beat 5 — The positive Beatitudes as what follows from freedom.** Mercy, purity of heart (single-heartedness, "a saint is someone whose life is about one thing"), hungering for righteousness, and peacemaking: all are described as flowing naturally once addiction to the four substitutes is broken, because God, who is mercy itself (Hebrew chesed), begins to move through a person aligned to him.

🕯 **Reflect:** When I wake up and ask honestly, "what am I seeking today," what is the real answer?

**Beat 6 — Salt and light; fulfillment, not abolition, of the Law.** "You are the salt of the earth" is read two ways: salt preserves what is good, and salt (as the Romans used it on conquered cities) can also render ground barren to evil. Jesus does not abolish the Law and prophets but fulfills them.

🕯 **Reflect:** Where am I called to actively "salt the earth" against something harmful, rather than staying neutral?

**Beat 7 — Loving enemies as the proof of love.** Loving those who will not love you back cannot be self-interest in disguise; Barron: this is why Jesus says "be perfect as your heavenly Father is perfect," immediately after commanding enemy-love. Of the three theological virtues, faith, hope and love, only love is eternal, since faith and hope will be fulfilled in heaven.

🕯 **Reflect:** Is there a person in my life I have never once prayed for their actual good?

**Beat 8 — "Turn the other cheek," read as nonviolent resistance.** Barron reads the right-cheek strike as a backhanded blow of contempt (reserved for inferiors in that culture); turning the other cheek refuses both retaliation and flight, instead confronting the aggressor. Giving one's cloak as well as one's coat to a legal creditor functions the same way: it shames rather than submits. He connects this directly to Gandhi, King, Desmond Tutu's "I do" to a man who would not share the sidewalk with a "gorilla," and John Paul II's unarmed confrontation of Soviet power in 1979 Warsaw.

🕯 **Reflect:** Where might a nonviolent but unmistakably firm refusal serve love better than either compliance or retaliation?

**Beat 9 — The downward trajectory of the Incarnation, to the cross.** Barron reads Philippians 2's "he emptied himself... obedient unto death, even the death of the cross" as the Son's descent not merely into humanity but into betrayal (Judas), denial (Peter), institutional injustice (the Sanhedrin, Pilate), physical torture, and finally death itself, which he names as the deepest root of human dysfunction and fear.

🕯 **Reflect:** Is there a part of my own suffering I have assumed is too dark for God to be present in?

**Beat 10 — The cry of dereliction (abandonment) and the Resurrection as the only adequate explanation.** "My God, my God, why hast thou forsaken me?" (Mark 15:34, quoting Psalm 22) is read, via Chesterton, as God himself entering the experience of abandonment by God. Barron argues that a crucified man claiming to be Messiah is not a plausible myth (unlike Hercules, no one dates a myth to a named Roman governor); only the apostles' insistence on having "eaten and drunk with him" after the Resurrection explains the emergence of the Church and its willingness to die for this claim.

🕯 **Reflect:** What difference would it make to my daily life if I treated the Resurrection as a historical claim requiring a real verdict, rather than a comforting symbol?

## 6 · Meditations

**Meditation A — The four altars, walked in order.** Picture four altars in a row: wealth, pleasure, power, honor. Walk slowly past each one. At each, notice any pull you feel, any memory of a "buzz" that faded and needed repeating. Do not judge yourself; simply notice, honestly, at which altar you have spent the most time. Then picture Christ standing some distance off, not competing with the altars, simply present, and ask what it would mean to walk toward him instead.

**Meditation B — Going down with him.** Picture the downward movement: from glory, to flesh, to healing the sick, to betrayal, to the scourging, to the nails, to the final hours, to the cry of abandonment, to the grave. Go down with him in your imagination, step by step, without rushing. At the bottom, in the dark of the tomb, wait. Then, slowly, let light return, and ask what in your own life most needs this same movement: not avoidance of the worst places, but Christ's presence going all the way into them.

## 7 · Doctrine (evidence-labeled)

**1. The Beatitudes.** ✅ Scripture (Matthew 5:3-12), verified in the Douay-Rheims, which reads "justice" for what other translations render "righteousness."

**2. Love as willing the good of the other as other; enemy-love as its proof.** 🟢 Rooted in Matthew 5:43-48 and consonant with Aquinas's definition of love (ST I-II q.26 a.4) as discussed in lecture 2; the Church's teaching on love of enemies is solemn moral teaching (CCC 1825, 2303) though Barron's specific formulation ("proof of love") is his own teaching device.

**3. Faith, hope, and love as the theological virtues, with love alone abiding eternally.** ✅ Scripture, 1 Corinthians 13:13, verified: "And now there remain faith, hope, and charity, these three: but the greatest of these is charity." The Douay-Rheims renders agape as "charity" throughout, consistent with the Church's traditional terminology (CCC 1812-1829 on the theological virtues).

**4. "He emptied himself... obedient unto death, even to the death of the cross" (Philippians 2:7-8), the kenosis (Greek for "self-emptying").** ✅ Scripture, verified. This is a central Christological text (cf. CCC 461-463 on kenosis), though Barron's reading of it as a "downward trajectory" into every form of human dysfunction, culminating in death, is his own theological development of the text, not a separate defined dogma.

**5. "My God, my God, why hast thou forsaken me" (Mark 15:34), and Chesterton's reading of it.** ✅ The Scripture itself is verified (quoting Psalm 22:1). Chesterton's gloss, "the cry which confessed that God was forsaken of God," is verified as his own words in Orthodoxy (1908); it is a striking theological meditation, not itself Church doctrine, and some theologians read the cry differently, as the human nature of Christ voicing the opening line of a psalm that ends in vindication and praise, rather than as a rupture within the Godhead. Barron does not resolve this tension explicitly in the lecture; readers should hold Chesterton's line as evocative theology, not settled doctrine.

🕯 **Reflect:** Does the cry of abandonment comfort me more as "God entering my darkest experience" or trouble me more as a mystery I cannot fully resolve, and can both be true at once?

**6. The Resurrection as a historical claim, not myth or legend.** ✅ Dogma (1 Corinthians 15:14, "if Christ be not risen again, your faith is vain"; the Creed). Barron's argument from genre (myths do not name historical governors; no one asks who ruled Rome when Hercules lived) is an apologetic argument he offers, not itself a doctrinal formula, but it supports a position the Church holds as central and non-negotiable.

**7. "Son of God" as a title deliberately claimed against Caesar's own imperial title.** 🟡 A historically well-attested point (Roman emperors, beginning with Augustus as adopted son of the deified Julius Caesar, did use divi filius, "son of the deified one"); Barron's reading of Mark's Gospel as a deliberate counter-claim to this imperial title is a widely held scholarly reading (associated especially with N.T. Wright and others), not a formally defined doctrine, but a strong exegetical argument.

## 8 · Key Corrections

- **"Turn the other cheek" is not a call to passivity or doormat behavior.** Barron is explicit that it is a provocative, dignity-asserting nonviolent act, not surrender; this reading has real support in the historical-cultural background (the right-cheek backhand as a gesture of contempt toward an inferior) but is itself one scholarly interpretation, not the only one, and should be presented as a strong reading rather than the single settled meaning of the text.
- **The Beatitudes are not a blanket condemnation of wealth, pleasure, power or honor as such.** Barron repeatedly says these are good creatures; the issue is addiction, making any one of them one's ultimate good.
- **Mourning and persecution are not treated as goods in themselves** to be sought out; they are blessed only insofar as they signal freedom from pleasure-addiction or honor-addiction, or faithfulness under opposition.
- **The claim that Jesus is "Son of God" deliberately competing with Caesar's title** is a historical-literary argument about Mark's rhetorical strategy; it does not itself prove or disprove any particular textual or theological claim beyond showing the provocative political resonance the phrase would have carried in the first century.

## 9 · Why This Matters

This lecture completes the course's single thread: right worship frees a person from addiction to finite goods, and that freedom becomes, concretely, love, up to and including love of enemies. The cross shows this love has no floor. It goes all the way into betrayal, injustice and death. None of it would matter, Barron insists, without the Resurrection, which is why the apostles' claim is not offered as poetry but as eyewitness fact ("we who ate and drank with him").

The entire six-lecture arc, from "I AM WHO AM" at the burning bush to the empty tomb, turns on whether this God, who needs nothing and therefore can only love, has in fact acted in history.

**Barron's point in one line: this love has no floor, and without the Resurrection none of it would be worth preaching.**
