# GBH-3 Law and True Freedom

> **Series:** GBH · Lecture 3 of 6 · Hub: [[GBH-0 God, the Bible and Humanity (Series Hub)]] · Previous: [[GBH-2 Right Praise and Sacrifice]]

## 1 · Anchor

**Enslavement follows bad worship; true freedom follows right worship, received as law, internalized until it becomes effortless.**

*One image to carry:* a golfer, rigid and awkward in a corrected stance, who is actually, for the first time, free to hit the ball well.

## 2 · Pocket Version (15-second re-read)

When we worship something less than God, we become addicted to it, because only God can satisfy our infinite longing. Moses is sent to free Israel from Egypt so they can worship God, and worship is the very point of the liberation. Real freedom is not "freedom of indifference," the ability to choose anything. It is "freedom for excellence," the disciplined internalizing of the law until goodness becomes easy, the way practice makes a language or a golf swing effortless. The story also warns against half-measures with evil, and the story of Eli and his sons shows corruption going unaddressed inside the community of faith itself, with disaster as a result, until grace breaks in again through Hannah.

## 3 · Scripture text (Douay-Rheims)

**Exodus 5:7 (Pharaoh's order, summary).** "You shall give straw no more to the people to make brick, as before: but let them go and gather straw... Fulfill your work every day as before."

**Psalm 118:97 (Douay numbering; = Ps 119:97 in most English Bibles).** "O how have I loved thy law, O Lord! it is my meditation all the day."

**1 Kings (1 Samuel) 15:33 (summary).** Samuel rebukes Saul for sparing King Agag of the Amalekites after being commanded to destroy them entirely; Samuel then executes Agag himself: "And Samuel hewed him in pieces before the Lord in Galgal."

*Verified against [drbo.org](http://drbo.org); note the Douay-Rheims numbers 1 and 2 Samuel as 1 and 2 Kings, and numbers this psalm 118, not 119.*

## 4 · Synthesis: Text and Setting

Barron returns to Moses, already named by God at the burning bush, now sent on a mission: "Let my people go." He stresses that in the Bible no one receives an experience of God without being sent. Drawing on Origen of Alexandria, he reads the Exodus story spiritually: Israel enslaved in Egypt stands for everything good and creative in a person; Pharaoh stands for the addiction that has enslaved it. The full line is not just "let my people go" but "that they might worship me in the desert" (Exodus 5:1, 7:16, and recurring). Liberation and worship are inseparable.

The Red Sea crossing, the giving of the Law at Sinai, the battle with Amalek, and the later story of Eli's corrupt sons and Hannah's child Samuel all extend the same theme: liberty is tied to right worship, right worship is tied to the Law received as a gift, and the Law is tied to true freedom rather than restriction.

## 5 · The Passage Explained, by Beat

**Beat 1 — Addiction as false worship.** Wealth, pleasure, power, honor, Barron names these as Aquinas's four substitutes for God. Each gives an initial "buzz" that fades, driving the addict to seek more. Only the infinite God can satisfy an infinite longing (Augustine's "restless heart," referenced again here).

🕯 **Reflect:** Which of the four keeps calling me back for "more"?

**Beat 2 — Pharaoh resists, and the old self fights back.** When Moses asks for release, Pharaoh makes the labor harder, withholding straw but keeping the quota. Barron reads this as the resistance every person meets when trying to break an old pattern.

🕯 **Reflect:** Where has my own resistance to change gotten worse right before it got better?

**Beat 3 — The Red Sea and striking the water.** Caught between Pharaoh behind and the sea ahead, Moses strikes the water in confidence rather than turning back. Barron: "where you stumble, that's where you dig for treasure."

🕯 **Reflect:** Where do I feel trapped right now between the old life and an unknown path forward?

**Beat 4 — Freedom of indifference versus freedom for excellence.** Drawing on the Dominican moral theologian Servais Pinckaers, Barron contrasts modern freedom as sheer unconstrained choice with freedom as the disciplined internalizing of a good pattern (language, music, golf) until it becomes effortless. The Law given at Sinai is this second kind of freedom.

🕯 **Reflect:** Where in my life have I resented a discipline that was actually making me freer?

**Beat 5 — Amalek, and the refusal of half-measures.** Saul defeats the Amalekites but spares their king and some livestock. Samuel rebukes him and executes Agag himself. Barron reads "Agag" as the part of a sin or habit we quietly spare, which will "raise a new army."

🕯 **Reflect:** What sin or habit have I only partly dealt with, telling myself it is a small exception?

**Beat 6 — Eli, his sons, and Hannah.** The high priest Eli hears of his sons' sexual and financial abuse of the people, speaks to them, and does nothing effective; disaster follows, the Ark is captured, Eli dies. Barron draws the parallel directly to the Church's own abuse crisis: words without action, followed by catastrophe. The rescue comes through Hannah, a woman at the bottom of the social order, who gives her long-awaited son Samuel back to God rather than keeping him.

🕯 **Reflect:** Where have I offered "nice words" instead of real action on something I know is wrong?

## 6 · Meditations

**Meditation A — The golf lesson.** Picture yourself in a corrected stance that feels stiff and strange, a teacher's hands adjusting your grip and shoulders. It feels like a loss of freedom. Now swing, and feel the ball go further and truer than it ever has on your own. Sit with the paradox: the discipline you resisted is the freedom you wanted. Name one law or discipline in your own spiritual life that you have been resisting the same way.

**Meditation B — Hannah's gift.** See Hannah at Shiloh, having begged for years for this child, finally holding small Samuel in her arms. Now watch her walk him to Eli and place him in the priest's care, giving him back. Feel the ache of letting go of what was most wanted, the moment it is given. Ask what you are being asked to give back rather than hold onto.

## 7 · Doctrine (evidence-labeled)

**1. True freedom is ordered to the good, not indifferent to it (freedom for excellence).** 🟡 This is Servais Pinckaers's influential theological distinction (The Sources of Christian Ethics, Part Three), not itself a dogmatic formula, but widely received in post-conciliar Catholic moral theology and consonant with CCC 1731-1733 on freedom and responsibility.

🕯 **Reflect:** Do I measure my own freedom by how many options I have, or by how good I have become at loving?

**2. "O how have I loved thy law, O Lord" (Ps 118/119:97).** ✅ Scripture. Verified against the Douay-Rheims.

**3. Conscience as "the aboriginal Vicar of Christ."** 🟢 Newman, Letter to the Duke of Norfolk, section 5, quoted in part at CCC 1778. Full line: "Conscience is not a long-sighted selfishness, nor a desire to be consistent with oneself; but it is a messenger from Him, Who, both in nature and grace, speaks to us behind a veil, and teaches and rules us by His representatives. Conscience is the aboriginal Vicar of Christ." This is Newman's own theological formulation, received with high respect, not a conciliar dogma.

**4. The command to "put the ban" on Amalek (1 Samuel 15).** 🟡 A historically and morally difficult text. The Church does not read this as a standing permission for such warfare; the mainstream patristic approach, which Barron follows via Origen, reads it typologically, as the uncompromising rejection of sin rather than a literal model for conduct. This spiritual reading does not resolve the historical-moral difficulty of the text itself, which remains debated among scholars and theologians.

**5. No one receives an experience of God without a mission.** 🟡 A recurring pattern Barron draws from the text (Moses, Isaiah, the apostles) rather than a single defined dogma, though it coheres with the Church's teaching on the universal call to mission (cf. Lumen Gentium 33).

## 8 · Key Corrections

- **The Law is not, first, a restriction.** Barron insists the biblical and Catholic reading is that Law disciplines desire toward freedom, against the common modern assumption that law and freedom are opposites.
- **"Hack Agag to pieces" is not a call to literal violence today.** It functions in Barron's reading as a figure for refusing half-measures against one's own sin, not a template for international or personal conduct, and Barron does not say otherwise.
- **The Eli-Hannah sequence is not simply a story about ancient corruption.** Barron draws the direct, pointed parallel to the Church's own abuse crisis; this is his own application, offered as moral challenge, not a claim about any specific case.
- **Faith is not credulity or "Bronze Age mythology."** Barron (echoing lecture 2's themes) insists biblical faith means trusting acceptance of a summons, not acceptance of claims without evidence.

## 9 · Why This Matters

The passage reframes two things people often get backward: freedom and law. If freedom is only the absence of constraint, discipline looks like oppression. If freedom is the capacity to do the good well and without struggle, discipline is how you get there. The Eli narrative also matters for institutional life: naming a wrong without acting on it is not enough, and the cost of delay compounds.
