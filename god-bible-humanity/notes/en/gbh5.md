# GBH-5 Contrasting Powers

> **Series:** GBH · Lecture 5 of 6 · Hub: [[GBH-0 God, the Bible and Humanity (Series Hub)]] · Previous: [[GBH-4 Grace and Mission]]

## 1 · Anchor

**Jonah fights God's mercy toward his enemies. God hems him in, not to punish him but to free him. And in Jesus, God and man meet without competing: a king born in weakness who outranks every Caesar.**

*One image to carry:* a baby in a feeding trough, with an army of angels at his side.

## 2 · Pocket Version (15-second re-read)

Jonah flees the call to preach mercy to Israel's enemy, is swallowed by a great fish, and is restored exactly where God wanted him. Then he resents Nineveh's repentance, because he wanted judgment, not mercy.

The Old Testament's institutions, covenant, law, temple, prophecy, never fully close the gap between God and humanity, so Israel comes to long for an anointed one (a Messiah), a king from David's line who is somehow also God himself. The Council of Chalcedon (451) defines how divinity and humanity meet in Jesus: fully God, fully human, in one person, without mixing or competing.

Luke's nativity story sets this newborn king against Caesar Augustus at every point: fed versus feeding, sheltered versus homeless, guarded by legions versus guarded by an army of angels.

## 3 · Scripture text (Douay-Rheims)

**Jonas (Jonah) 1:1-3 (summary).** "The word of the Lord came to Jonas... saying: Arise, and go to Nineve... Jonas rose up to flee into Tharsis from the face of the Lord."

**Luke 2:1, 4, 7, 13-14.** "And it came to pass, that in those days there went out a decree from Caesar Augustus, that the whole world should be enrolled... And Joseph also went up from Galilee... to the city of David, which is called Bethlehem... And she brought forth her firstborn son, and wrapped him up in swaddling clothes, and laid him in a manger; because there was no room for them in the inn... And suddenly there was with the angel a multitude of the heavenly army, praising God, and saying: Glory to God in the highest; and on earth peace to men of good will."

*Verified against [drbo.org](http://drbo.org). Note the Douay-Rheims spells the book "Jonas" and the city "Tharsis" (Tarshish) and "Nineve" (Nineveh).*

## 4 · Synthesis: Text and Setting

Barron closes the Old Testament portion of the course with Jonah, which he calls one of the shortest and strangest books in the Bible, and one disproportionately influential (Herman Melville's Father Mapple sermon in Moby-Dick, he notes, is among its best commentaries). The book's interest is not Jonah's preaching, only three words in Hebrew, but Jonah himself: a flawed figure through whom the reader learns how resistance to God's call works, and is worked through.

From there Barron pivots to the New Testament, insisting against the ancient heresy of Marcionism (the idea that the God of the Old Testament is a different, lesser god) that the Old and New Testaments reveal the same God, and that Jesus cannot be understood apart from Israel's institutions: covenant, law, temple, prophecy.

He surveys the New Testament's "high" claims about Jesus on nearly every page (forgiving sins, "you have heard it said, but I say," "lord of the Sabbath," "before Abraham was, I am") and then walks through three ancient errors about Christ's identity (monophysitism/docetism, Nestorianism, Arianism) before giving the Council of Chalcedon's answer.

He closes by reading Luke's nativity account as a sustained, deliberate contrast between the newborn king and Caesar Augustus.

## 5 · The Passage Explained, by Beat

**Beat 1 — Jonah flees the call.** Sent to preach judgment against Nineveh, Israel's great enemy, Jonah instead books passage the opposite direction, toward Tarshish. Barron: resisting the voice of conscience or of God does not leave everything else fine; storms (literal and otherwise) follow, because we are bound up with others, not isolated individuals.

🕯 **Reflect:** Has my own resistance to a clear call caused "storms" for people around me, not only for myself?

**Beat 2 — Swallowed by the fish.** Barron reads the great fish not as punishment but as God restricting Jonah's rebellious will in order to free it, consistent with lecture 3's "freedom for excellence": confinement that restores him to where God actually wanted him.

🕯 **Reflect:** Can I name a season of confinement, illness, or limitation in my own life that, in hindsight, repositioned me?

**Beat 3 — Jonah's prayer from the depths.** "I called to the Lord out of my distress, and he answered me; out of the belly of Sheol, I cried, and you heard my voice." Barron: this is honest prayer from the worst place, brought to God rather than hidden from him.

🕯 **Reflect:** Do I bring my worst moments to God in prayer, or do I go silent precisely then?

**Beat 4 — Nineveh repents; Jonah resents it.** The entire city, down to its animals, repents at Jonah's one-line sermon. Rather than rejoicing, Jonah is angry, and admits: "I knew you are a gracious God and merciful... that is why I fled." He wanted judgment on his enemies, not mercy.

🕯 **Reflect:** Is there anyone I would rather see judged than shown mercy?

**Beat 5 — High claims about Jesus on nearly every page.** "My son, your sins are forgiven" (only God forgives sins); "you have heard it said, but I say" (claiming authority over the Torah itself); "the Son of man is Lord of the Sabbath" (claiming lordship over something instituted by God at creation); "before Abraham was, I am" (echoing Exodus 3:14's Ego eimi). Barron: this is not confined to John's Gospel; it runs through all four.

🕯 **Reflect:** Do I treat Jesus mainly as a wise teacher, in practice, even while professing more?

**Beat 6 — Chalcedon's answer (451).** Against monophysitism (Jesus is only divine, humanity an appearance), Nestorianism (Jesus is an especially close human friend of God), and Arianism (Jesus is a semi-divine being, like a demigod), Chalcedon defines: in Jesus, two natures, fully divine and fully human, come together in the unity of one person, "without confusion, without change, without division, without separation."

🕯 **Reflect:** Which of the three errors do I lean toward when I imagine Jesus?

**Beat 7 — The nativity as contrast with Caesar.** Augustus commands a worldwide census; the true king is registered as a nobody in Bethlehem. Augustus is the best-fed, best-housed, most heavily guarded man in the world; the infant king is placed in a feeding trough, has no room at the inn, and is announced by an army, stratia, of angels to shepherds, the lowest social class.

🕯 **Reflect:** Where do I still measure power, safety and belonging by Caesar's categories rather than the manger's?

## 6 · Meditations

**Meditation A — In the belly of the fish.** Picture total darkness, the smell of the deep, seaweed wrapped around your head, as Jonah describes it. You cannot move or escape. In that enclosed dark, speak your own version of Jonah's prayer: name exactly where you are, without minimizing it, and ask to be heard "out of the depths." Wait for the moment the fish releases you onto dry land, exactly where you were always meant to be.

**Meditation B — The manger and the army of angels.** See the feeding trough, splintered wood, animal smell, the baby within it. Now see, stretching to the horizon, an army of angels, not decorative cherubs but an overwhelming heavenly host. Hold both images together: total vulnerability and total power, in the same scene. Ask what it would mean to trust that combination in your own life, rather than trusting only visible strength.

## 7 · Doctrine (evidence-labeled)

**1. The Old and New Testaments reveal the same God; Marcionism is rejected.** ✅ Dogma, affirmed from the earliest centuries against Marcion (condemned c. 144) and reaffirmed throughout Church teaching (e.g., CCC 123, Dei Verbum 14-16 on the unity and inspiration of both Testaments).

**2. In Jesus Christ, one divine person subsists in two complete natures, divine and human, "without confusion, without change, without division, without separation."** ✅ Dogma, the Chalcedonian Definition (451), verified. This is binding Christological doctrine, not theological opinion.

**3. "Before Abraham was, I am" and the "I am" sayings (John's Gospel) echo Exodus 3:14.** 🟢 A standard patristic and traditional reading (e.g., Augustine's tractates on John), connecting the Septuagint's ego eimi in Exodus 3:14 to Jesus's self-identification; widely received, though it rests on a reading of Greek vocabulary rather than a formally defined dogma in itself.

**4. "Glory to God in the highest; and on earth peace to men of good will" (Luke 2:14).** ✅ Scripture, verified; this is also the opening of the Gloria at Mass.

**5. Jesus as judge as well as savior.** ✅ Dogma (the Creed: "he will come again to judge the living and the dead"). Barron notes that encountering the light of Christ necessarily exposes what is "off-kilter" in us; this is consonant with, though broader than, the formal teaching on judgment.

## 8 · Key Corrections

- **The "ban" language used of Nineveh's threatened destruction is conditional, not a decree.** Jonah's sermon is a warning, and the whole point of the narrative is that repentance can avert it; it should not be read as evidence that God's judgments are irrevocable fate.
- **Jonah's resentment is the point, not a flaw in the story's logic.** The book does not resolve Jonah's anger; it leaves the reader to sit with the challenge of a God who shows mercy even to the enemy.
- **Monophysitism, Nestorianism, and Arianism are not caricatures invented to be easily refuted**; each tried to safeguard something real (Christ's full divinity, the integrity of his humanity, a workable account of the union), which is why Chalcedon's answer needed four careful negations rather than one slogan.
- **The contrast with Caesar Augustus is Luke's literary and theological strategy**, not a claim that Augustus himself was uniquely wicked; the point is about where true kingship is found, not a verdict on the historical emperor.

## 9 · Why This Matters

Jonah confronts the reader with a God whose mercy may be unwelcome, because it extends to people we would rather see judged.

Chalcedon matters because getting the identity of Jesus wrong, too divine to be really human, too human to be really God, changes what salvation even means. Only one who is fully human can heal humanity from within, and only one who is fully God can do so for good.

The nativity's contrast with Caesar turns around the reader's whole sense of where real power and real safety lie.

**Barron's reading of Luke in one line: the best-guarded man in the world was Caesar. The true king lay in a feeding trough.**
