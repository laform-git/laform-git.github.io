# CHURCH-04 Anointing of the Sick - The Invention of the Hospital

> **Sacramental root:** Anointing of the Sick · **Series:** THE CHURCH

---

## The Myth

"Hospitals are a product of modern secular medicine. The Church just told sick people to pray."

---

## The Root

✅ Christ healed the sick constantly, and he identified himself with them: "I was sick and you visited me" (Mt 25:36). He sent his apostles to heal, and they "anointed with oil many that were sick and healed them" (Mk 6:13). The Church continues this in the **Anointing of the Sick**: "Is any among you sick? Let him call for the elders of the church, and let them pray over him, anointing him with oil in the name of the Lord" (Jas 5:14).

🟢 The sacrament teaches that the sick person is not useless. Christ is present in him, and his suffering can be united to Christ's (CCC 1499–1532, esp. 1521). A Church that kneels to anoint the sick will also build places to care for them.

---

## The Story

**1. The ancient world had doctors, but not hospitals for the poor.** [Consensus] Greeks and Romans had physicians, healing temples, and military infirmaries for soldiers and slaves who had economic value. What did not exist was an institution offering free care to any sick stranger simply because he was sick.

**2. Christians stayed when others fled.** [Documented] During the plague of the 250s, Bishop Dionysius of Alexandria wrote that Christians nursed the sick and buried the dead at the risk of their own lives, while pagans pushed the sick away (preserved in Eusebius, *Church History* 7.22). Sociologist Rodney Stark argues that simple nursing (food, water, warmth) greatly improved survival rates, which helped Christianity grow (*The Rise of Christianity*, ch. 4). [Consensus on the nursing; Stark's numbers are estimates]

**3. The first hospital: the Basileias (c. 369).** [Consensus] St. Basil the Great, bishop of Caesarea in Cappadocia, built a complex outside the city with housing for the poor, a hospice for travelers, and wards for the sick and for lepers, staffed with nurses and physicians. Historian Timothy Miller (*The Birth of the Hospital in the Byzantine Empire*, Johns Hopkins UP) treats Christian institutions of this period as the origin of the hospital. Around 390 in Rome, the noblewoman **Fabiola** founded a hospital and nursed patients herself, as St. Jerome records (Letter 77).

> *Root named:* Basil's friend Gregory of Nazianzus described the Basileias as a new city where sickness was faced with love. The care was given because the patient was Christ in disguise.

**4. Medieval hospitals and the orders of mercy.** [Documented]

- Monasteries kept infirmaries and guesthouses (*Rule of St. Benedict*, ch. 36: "Care of the sick must rank above and before all else, so that they may truly be served as Christ").
- The **Hôtel-Dieu** of Paris, founded by the bishop in the 7th century, has operated almost continuously since.
- The **Knights Hospitaller** (recognized 1113) ran a large hospital in Jerusalem.
- Thousands of **leprosaria** cared for people society feared.
- **St. Vincent de Paul** and **St. Louise de Marillac** founded the Daughters of Charity (1633), who nursed the poor in their homes and in hospitals.

**5. Saints of the incurable.** [Documented]

- **St. Damien of Molokai** (1840–1889) lived with the exiled lepers of Hawaii, built homes and a hospital, and died of leprosy himself.
- **St. Marianne Cope** continued his work.
- **St. Teresa of Calcutta** founded homes for the dying whom no hospital would take.

**6. Today.** [Documented] The Vatican's 2025 statistics list **5,377 hospitals, 13,895 clinics and dispensaries, 504 leprosy care homes, over 15,000 homes for the elderly and chronically ill, and about 8,600 orphanages** run by the Catholic Church. Most are in Africa, Asia and Latin America, often where states provide little. In the United States, the Catholic Health Association reports that about one in seven hospital patients is cared for in a Catholic hospital.

## Honest Footnote

- **The "26% of world healthcare" figure** is repeated widely (including by Vatican Radio in 2013), but its original calculation is unclear. [Unverified] Use the hard counts above instead.
- **Nicaea did not order a hospital in every city.** A claim that the Council of Nicaea (325) required this rests on later, spurious Arabic canons. [Consensus]
- **Medieval medicine was often poor**, and hospitals could spread disease. Their strength was care, shelter and dignity, not cures. [Consensus]
- **Islamic hospitals** (the *bimaristan*, from the 9th century) were also important and developed medicine further in some areas. Christians did not do it alone, though they came first. [Consensus]
- **Catholic hospitals refuse some procedures** (abortion, euthanasia, sterilization) on moral grounds, which critics see as a limit on patients' choices.

## For You

In the Anointing of the Sick, the Church tells a dying person what the world often does not: your life still matters, and your suffering is not wasted. That same conviction built every hospital in this chapter. Whatever you are carrying, whether illness, age, or the fear of them, Christ comes to meet you there, and not only when you are well.
