# CHURCH-06 Baptism - Human Dignity and the End of Slavery

> **Sacramental root:** Baptism · **Series:** THE CHURCH

---

## The Myth

"The Church blessed slavery and colonialism. Human rights came from the Enlightenment, in spite of religion."

---

## The Root: What Baptism Does to a Human Being

**1. Every person already has dignity.** ✅ "God created man in his own image, in the image of God he created him; male and female he created them" (Gen 1:27). This is true of every human being, baptized or not: the unborn, the slave, the stranger, the enemy (CCC 357, 1700). Human dignity does not depend on Baptism. It depends on God.

**2. Baptism lifts that dignity higher.** ✅ Baptism does not replace our nature; it elevates it (CCC 1262–1274):

- It **forgives** original sin and all personal sin (CCC 1263).
- It makes the person a **new creature**, sharing God's own life through sanctifying grace (2 Pet 1:4; CCC 1265).
- It makes the person an **adopted child of God**: "you have received the spirit of sonship. When we cry, 'Abba! Father!' it is the Spirit himself bearing witness with our spirit that we are children of God" (Rom 8:15–16; Gal 4:4–7).
- It makes the person a **member of Christ** and of his Church (CCC 1267).
- It marks the soul with an **indelible character**, a permanent spiritual seal of belonging to Christ that can never be erased or repeated (CCC 1272–1274; Council of Trent, Session 7, canon 9).
- It is **the door to every other sacrament** (CCC 1213).

**3. Two precisions.** 🟢

- *Adopted, not equal to Christ.* Christ is the Son by nature; we become sons and daughters *in* the Son, by grace. Hence "adoption."
- *Conversion is the response, not the sacrament.* Conversion is the whole person turning to God. In an adult it usually comes before Baptism; in an infant, grace comes first and the response grows over a lifetime. The pull toward sin (concupiscence) remains after Baptism (CCC 1264), so conversion continues every day.

**4. Why this matters for history.** 🟢 If a slave can be baptized, then a slave is a child of God and a brother of his master, sharing the same altar. "There is neither slave nor free... for you are all one in Christ Jesus" (Gal 3:28). That idea, carried by the sacrament, slowly made slavery morally impossible to defend.

---

## The Story

**1. The ancient world assumed slavery.** [Consensus] In the Roman Empire, perhaps 10–20% of people were slaves. Aristotle argued some people were slaves "by nature." No ancient philosophical school called for abolition.

**2. The first voice against slavery itself (c. 379).** [Consensus] St. Gregory of Nyssa, a bishop, preached that no one can own a human being, because God made man free and in his image; how much, he asked his congregation, did you pay for the image of God? (*Homily 4 on Ecclesiastes*). Historians such as Kyle Harper regard this as the first known outright condemnation of slavery as an institution in the ancient world.

**3. Infants and the unwanted.** [Consensus] Christians rejected the Roman practice of abandoning unwanted newborns, especially girls, and took in exposed children (*Letter to Diognetus* 5; *Didache* 2). Every baptized infant was a child of God.

**4. Slavery fades in medieval Christendom.** [Consensus, with debate on causes]

- **St. Bathilde** (d. 680), a former slave who became queen of the Franks, bought and freed captives and pushed to end the slave trade in her realm.
- The **Council of London** (1102), under St. Anselm, forbade the "shameful trade" of selling men like animals in England.
- The **Trinitarians** (1198) and **Mercedarians** (1218) were founded to ransom Christian captives; Mercedarians took a vow to offer themselves in exchange if needed.
- By the high Middle Ages, outright slavery had largely disappeared from Western Europe (Marc Bloch; Rodney Stark, *For the Glory of God*, ch. 4).

**5. The New World and the School of Salamanca.** [Documented]

- In 1511 the Dominican **Antonio de Montesinos** preached to Spanish colonists in Hispaniola: "Are these not men? Do they not have rational souls?" He was condemned by the colonists, defended by his order.
- **Bartolomé de las Casas**, a former colonist turned Dominican friar, spent 50 years defending the native peoples.
- **Pope Paul III**, in *Sublimis Deus* (1537), declared that the Indians are true human beings, may not be deprived of liberty or property, and must not be enslaved.
- **Francisco de Vitoria** (University of Salamanca) argued that native peoples held real rights of ownership and self-government; historians count him among the founders of international law and natural-rights theory.
- **St. Peter Claver** (d. 1654) met slave ships in Cartagena for 40 years, nursing, catechizing and baptizing the enslaved; he signed himself "slave of the slaves forever."

**6. Formal condemnation.** [Documented] Pope Gregory XVI condemned the slave trade in *In Supremo Apostolatus* (1839). Leo XIII praised abolition in Brazil (*In Plurimis*, 1888). Vatican II named slavery among the "infamies" that poison society and insult the Creator (*Gaudium et Spes* 27).

## Honest Footnote

This chapter must be the most honest of all.

- **Popes also authorized enslavement.** In *Dum Diversas* (1452), Nicholas V gave the King of Portugal permission to reduce "Saracens and pagans" to perpetual servitude. [Documented]
- **Catholic institutions owned slaves**, including religious orders in the Americas. In 1838 the Maryland Jesuits sold 272 enslaved people to pay debts, including Georgetown's. Georgetown and the Jesuits have since apologized. [Documented]
- **Condemnations were often ignored**, and many theologians long accepted "just title" slavery (e.g., prisoners of war). A clear, universal condemnation of all chattel slavery came slowly. [Consensus]
- **Protestant abolitionists** (Wilberforce, the Quakers) led the 18th–19th-century campaign in the English-speaking world.
- The truth is not that Catholics never failed. It is that the principle carried by Baptism, every person an image of God and potential child of God, was the weapon that finally defeated slavery, even against Catholics who betrayed it.

---

## For You

Before you were baptized, or even if you never were, you were already made in God's image, and nothing you have done can erase that. Baptism offers more: to be adopted as God's own child, sealed with a mark that lasts forever. If you are baptized, that mark is still on you, however far you have drifted. If you are not, the door is open.

## 🕯 Reflect

1. If dignity comes from being made in God's image, who in your life have you treated as less than that?
2. Gregory of Nyssa asked what price you could pay for the image of God. What does that question do to the idea of owning a person?
3. What does it mean to you that Baptism leaves a mark nothing can erase?
