# CHURCH-07 Confession - Mercy and Second Chances

> **Sacramental root:** Confession (the Sacrament of Penance and Reconciliation) · **Series:** THE CHURCH

---

## The Myth

"Confession is a guilt machine the Church uses to control people. Christianity is about judgment and punishment."

---

## The Root

✅ On Easter night the risen Christ breathed on the apostles and said: "Receive the Holy Spirit. If you forgive the sins of any, they are forgiven; if you retain the sins of any, they are retained" (Jn 20:22–23). In Confession, Christ himself forgives through the priest, restores grace, and reconciles the sinner with God and the Church (CCC 1440–1449, 1468).

🟢 The deepest idea here is that **a person is not his worst act.** Sin is real and must be named, but it is never the final word. A culture shaped by Confession believes people can change, start over and be restored. That belief changed how Europe treated enemies, criminals and the guilty.

---

## The Story

**1. Mercy in war: the Peace and Truce of God (10th–11th c.).** [Documented] In an age of constant private warfare, bishops' councils, beginning at Charroux in 989, declared clergy, peasants, women, merchants and their goods off-limits to violence (the *Peace of God*). The *Truce of God* forbade fighting on certain days of the week and holy seasons. The Third Lateran Council (1179) extended the Truce to the whole Church. These were among the first organized attempts in Europe to protect non-combatants.

**2. Private confession spreads (6th–13th c.).** [Consensus] Irish monks spread private, repeatable confession across Europe. The Fourth Lateran Council (1215) required every Catholic to confess at least once a year. Historians note that the practice trained ordinary people in self-examination, conscience and the idea that inner intention matters, not only outward acts.

**3. Sanctuary.** [Documented] For centuries, a fugitive who reached a church could claim sanctuary, protecting him from immediate revenge and forcing a legal process instead of a lynching.

**4. Prisons meant for reform: the "penitentiary."** [Documented]

- The very word *penitentiary* comes from *penitence*: a place to repent and change.
- The Benedictine scholar **Jean Mabillon** (d. 1707) wrote that monastic prisons should aim at the conversion of the guilty, not only their punishment.
- In 1703–1704 **Pope Clement XI** opened the House of Correction at **San Michele a Ripa** in Rome for young offenders, with separate cells, work and schooling. Its inscription read: *"It is of little use to restrain criminals by punishment unless you reform them by education."* The English prison reformer John Howard visited it and praised it. Historians (Finzsch and Jütte, Cambridge UP) treat it as an early model of the modern reformatory and juvenile justice.

> *Root named:* "unless you reform them" is the logic of Confession applied to the criminal: the goal is not only to stop the crime but to restore the person.

**5. Today.** [Documented] Catholic prison chaplains serve in jails worldwide. In Los Angeles, the Jesuit **Fr. Greg Boyle** founded **Homeboy Industries** (1988), now among the largest gang-intervention and re-entry programs in the world, built on the conviction that no one is beyond redemption.

---

## Honest Footnote

- **Indulgences were abused.** In the late Middle Ages, preachers like Johann Tetzel effectively sold the remission of punishment for money. This scandal helped trigger the Protestant Reformation. The Council of Trent (1562) abolished the office of indulgence-sellers ("questors"). [Documented]
- **Penances were once harsh and public**, and some medieval penitentials read like legal tariffs. [Consensus]
- **The seal of Confession** (a priest may never reveal what he hears) is criticized today by some governments in abuse cases. The Church holds the seal is absolute (CCC 1467). Critics and defenders both have serious arguments.
- **Ecclesiastical courts also used prison and, in the Inquisition, torture.** See CHURCH-11.
- **Confession has been misused** by some priests to manipulate penitents, a grave sin the Church punishes with the most severe penalties in canon law.

## For You

Most people carry something they have never said out loud. Confession offers a strange freedom: you name it, plainly, to a priest who stands in for Christ, and you hear the words "I absolve you." The weight is lifted, not because you earned it but because you were forgiven. If it has been years, the door has not moved. No one is too far gone.

## 🕯 Reflect

1. Do you believe people can really change? What would society look like if no one did?
2. Why might saying a sin out loud to another person be harder, and more healing, than confessing silently?
3. "It is of little use to restrain criminals by punishment unless you reform them." Do our prisons today believe that?
