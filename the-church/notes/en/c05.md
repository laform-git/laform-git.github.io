# CHURCH-05 The Eucharist - Charity and the Poor

> **Sacramental root:** The Eucharist · **Series:** THE CHURCH

---

## The Scene

Rome, August 258. The emperor has ordered the Church's clergy killed and her property seized. The prefect of the city summons the deacon Lawrence, who keeps the Church's funds, and demands her treasures. Lawrence asks for a few days to gather them. When he comes back, he brings a crowd: the poor, the crippled, the blind, the widows and the orphans whom the Church feeds every day. "These," he says, "are the treasures of the Church." He is put to death a few days later. [Tradition — recounted by St. Ambrose, *On the Duties of the Clergy* 2.28; his martyrdom in 258 is historical]

He had learned at the altar what a treasure is.

---

## The Myth

"The Church hoards gold while the poor starve. Real help for the poor comes from governments and secular NGOs."

---

## The Root

✅ In the Eucharist, Christ gives himself completely: "This is my body which is given for you" (Lk 22:19). The Church teaches that "the Eucharist commits us to the poor" (CCC 1397): to receive the Body of Christ given away is to be sent to give yourself away.

🟢 St. John Chrysostom put it bluntly in the 4th century: if you honor Christ's body on the altar, do not ignore him naked in the street (*Homily 50 on Matthew*). Pope Benedict XVI taught that charity belongs to the Church's very nature as much as the sacraments and preaching do (*Deus Caritas Est* 22, 25).

---

## The Story

**1. Charity was built into the Sunday Mass from the start.** [Documented] Around 155, St. Justin Martyr described a Christian Sunday: the readings, the prayers, the bread and wine that become the Eucharist, and then a collection given to the presider, who "helps orphans and widows, and those who through sickness or any other cause are in want, and those who are in bonds, and the strangers" (*First Apology* 67). The altar and the care of the poor were one act.

**2. The first Christians shared everything.** [Documented] "They sold their possessions and goods and distributed them to all, as any had need" (Acts 2:45). The apostles appointed **deacons** to serve the widows' tables (Acts 6:1–6). That office still exists.

**3. An enemy's testimony (362).** [Documented] Emperor Julian, who rejected Christianity and tried to revive paganism, ordered pagan priests to copy Christian charity, complaining that "the impious Galilaeans support not only their own poor but ours as well, all men see that our people lack aid from us" (*Letter to Arsacius*, Ep. 22). The authenticity of this letter has been defended by recent scholarship.

> *Root named:* the "treasure" of the early Church was its poor. That was St. Lawrence's answer to the prefect, and it was Julian's complaint a century later.

**4. A thousand years of organized mercy.** [Documented]

- Monasteries received every guest "as Christ" (*Rule of St. Benedict*, ch. 53) and fed the poor at their gates.
- Medieval parishes, confraternities and guilds ran almshouses, burial societies and food distribution.
- **St. Francis of Assisi** (d. 1226) and his friars made poverty itself a way of following Christ.
- **St. Vincent de Paul** (d. 1660) organized charity in France on a new scale.
- **Bl. Frédéric Ozanam**, a 20-year-old student, founded the Society of St. Vincent de Paul in Paris (1833) after a critic challenged him: "Show us your works." It now works in over 150 countries.
- **St. Teresa of Calcutta** founded the Missionaries of Charity (1950) for "the poorest of the poor."

**5. Today.** [Documented] **Caritas Internationalis**, the Church's confederation of national charities (founded 1951), links over 160 member organizations worldwide and is among the largest humanitarian networks on earth. Catholic Relief Services, Catholic Charities in each U.S. diocese, and thousands of parish food pantries run alongside the schools and hospitals of CHURCH-02 and CHURCH-04. Much of this work happens where few others go.

---

## Honest Footnote

- **The Church does have great wealth in art and buildings**, much of it held in trust for the public (cathedrals, museums) and not easily sold. Critics still ask fair questions about Vatican finances, which have seen real scandals, including the London property case that led to a Vatican criminal trial (verdict 2023). [Documented]
- **Medieval charity could be humiliating or selective**, distinguishing "deserving" from "undeserving" poor. [Consensus]
- **Charity is not a substitute for justice.** The Church's own social teaching (from Leo XIII's *Rerum Novarum*, 1891) insists on just wages and fair structures, not only handouts.
- **Abuses in some charitable institutions** (e.g., Ireland's Magdalene laundries) are treated in CHURCH-11.

---

## For You

Every Mass ends with a sending: *Ite, missa est*, "Go." The bread you receive is a life given away, and it is meant to make yours a life given away too. If you have stood far from the Church because of what you heard about her wealth, consider what the evidence shows about where her hands have been for two thousand years. Then consider where yours are.

**The Church's treasure was never the gold. It was the people the gold was for.**

3. Who are the "treasures" of your parish, your family, your city?
