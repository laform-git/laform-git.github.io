
# JP2-JesusChrist-0a Sin & the Promise of a Redeemer
*Jesus Christ Cycle, Prologue — Wednesday Audiences 27 August – 17 December 1986 (nn. 1–12)*
**Series:** [[JP2-JesusChrist-0 Jesus Christ, Son of God and Saviour (Series Hub)]]

---

## Why a Christology Begins with Sin

On 27 August 1986, having finished his long cycle on God the Father and Creator, John Paul II opened the second article of the Creed — and did something that surprises most readers. Before saying a word about who Christ is, he spent twelve audiences on sin. His reason is the Creed's own grammar: the Son came down "for us men and for our salvation" (*propter nos homines et propter nostram salutem*). The Incarnation, he said, has a "salvific (soteriological) meaning," and therefore "Christology is located within anthropology and history." You cannot say what the Redeemer is for without saying what he redeems us from. According to revelation, sin is "the principal and fundamental evil": a rejection of God's will, of his truth and holiness, and of his fatherly goodness — the "image and likeness" turned against its own Maker. So the Pope draws a conclusion he will repeat to the end of the series: "the truth about sin also takes its place in the central nucleus of the Christian faith. Sin and redemption are correlative terms." Two guardrails go up in the first audience. The previous cycle on Providence (Vatican I, DS 3003) has already shown that nothing, not even sin, escapes God's government. And the whole study points toward Romans 5:20 — "where sin increased, grace abounded all the more" — so that sin, seen in the light of redemption, becomes "the occasion for a deeper knowledge of the mystery of God … the God who is love" (1 Jn 4:16), in whom justice and mercy are reconciled.

For the homily: preach sin without redemption and you preach despair; preach redemption without sin and you preach sentimentality. The Creed holds them in one sentence.

> 🕯 **Reflect:** If sin and redemption are correlative, then how lightly I take my sin is a measure of how little I understand what Christ did. Which do I actually think about more — my sins, or his mercy — and is the balance honest?

**Meditations for prayer:**

- Stand in a church on Sunday as the Creed is sung, and stop at the words "for us men and for our salvation." Hear them as the reason for everything that follows — the manger, the Cross, the empty tomb — all of it addressed to you.
- Picture a physician who refuses to name a disease for fear of frightening the patient, and the patient who dies untreated. Then picture the physician who names it plainly and holds out the cure in the same breath. Ask which one you want Christ to be.
- Recall the last time you were truly forgiven by someone you had wronged. Let the memory show you why the forgiveness meant nothing until the wrong was named.

## Man as Gift: Original Justice and the Test

On 3 September 1986 the Pope went back to the beginning. Scripture, he observed, is "the book of God and about God" but also "a great book about man," and it is futile to rename sin or reinterpret it away "as has happened under the influence of the Enlightenment and of secularism." Sin can only be understood against the background of gift. Genesis 1 calls creation good and man "very good" (Gen 1:31); Ecclesiastes says "God made man righteous" (Eccl 7:29); and Trent teaches that Adam lost "the holiness and righteousness in which he had been established" (DS 1511). John Paul II unpacks this *original justice*: sanctifying grace and friendship with God, the preternatural gifts lost through sin, an "interior equilibrium," freedom from the fear of decay and death, "dominion over himself," and an *integritas* free of the threefold concupiscence — toward sensual pleasure, toward earthly goods, and toward self-assertion against reason. The fruit was order between persons: "they were both naked … and were not ashamed" (Gen 2:25). Yet even perfect man was not exempt from the testing of his freedom. The command about the tree of the knowledge of good and evil (Gen 2:16-17) "recalls symbolically the absolute limit which man, as a creature, must recognize and respect." The Pope is careful about method: he speaks of the "beginning" "as it appears in the pages of Scripture," not "as scientific theories describe it." The test asked one question — would man accept the truth of his dignity *and* the truth of his limitation? "Unfortunately, we know the results of the test — man failed."

> 🕯 **Reflect:** Original justice began with self-mastery — dominion over oneself before dominion over the world. Where do I try to control circumstances because I have not first governed myself?

**Meditations for prayer:**

- Imagine Adam waking in the garden with nothing to hide: no shame in his body, no fear in his heart, no rivalry with the woman beside him. Sit in that unguarded morning and notice what in you aches for it.
- Picture the single tree at the centre of the garden, surrounded by every other tree freely given. Let it stand for the one limit in your own life you keep resenting, and ask why one "no" so easily eclipses a thousand "yeses."
- Recall a time you were trusted with something precious and tested by it. Bring before God, honestly, whether you passed — and remember the Pope sets even this failure "within the context of the truth of the redemption."

## The First Sin: "You Will Be Like God"

The 10 September audience is the anatomy of the Fall. Whatever the literary form of Genesis 3, "one sure element" cannot be denied: it describes "a primordial event, that is, a fact, which according to revelation took place at the beginning of human history," with decisive consequences for man's relation to God, to himself, to others and to the world. Man passes from original justice into the "state of fallen nature" (*status naturae lapsae*). Because the first parents had received sanctifying grace "not only for [themselves], but as founder[s] of the human family, for all [their] descendants," its loss is an inheritance; "the essence of original sin … consists in this privation of grace." The tempter is new to the story — a being from "the world of invisible beings," the "ancient serpent … the deceiver of the whole world" (Rev 12:9), "the father of lies" (Jn 8:44). His offer, "you will be like God, knowing good and evil" (Gen 3:5), attacks the deepest truth of creaturehood: "God alone is the eternal legislator." Man "cannot pretend to establish the moral law, to decide himself what is good and what is bad, independently of the Creator." Here the Pope links human sin to the angelic fall already treated in the cycle on angels: human sin "is in a certain sense the consequence of the sin that had already occurred in the world of invisible beings," whose "original pride" made their own spirit "the principle and rule of freedom." Augustine names the root — *amor sui usque ad contemptum Dei*, "love of self to the point of contempt of God" (*City of God* XIV, 28). Yet man, the Pope notes, did not swallow the whole hatred; he grasped at a created thing against the command, thinking he too could be "like God." Paul's word for it is disobedience (Rom 5:19), and Genesis 3 "contains the original 'model' of every sin of which man is capable."

> 🕯 **Reflect:** The serpent did not deny God's existence; he made God a suspect. Where have I started to treat God's commands as a rival's restrictions rather than a Father's protection?

**Meditations for prayer:**

- Stand beside Eve at the tree as the serpent speaks. Hear how reasonable it sounds, how small the act, how large the promise. Notice which of his phrases still sounds persuasive to you.
- Picture the fallen angels before the first man existed, choosing "themselves over God, instead of choosing themselves 'in God.'" Then picture Michael's answer — "Who is like God?" — and make it your own answer to whatever voice is tempting you today.
- Recall a moment you decided for yourself what was good, knowing the Church taught otherwise. Without excusing it, set it before Christ as one more instance of the oldest sin, and ask him to undo its root.

## Sin Invades History

On 17 September 1986 the Pope moved from the first sin to its legacy. The phrase "original sin" appears nowhere in Scripture, but Scripture narrates its effect: a sinful condition "passed on to all [Adam's] descendants," spreading by "a kind of universal infection." Cain kills Abel (Gen 4); "the wickedness of man was great in the earth" and "it grieved him to his heart" (Gen 6:5-6); the Flood follows; then Babel (Gen 11:1-9), whose lesson John Paul II draws for the modern world: "no merely human agreement can bring about union among men if it is not rooted in God." Sin can be open revolt or the quieter attempt "to act 'independently of God,' as if God did not exist." Here he folds in his own *Reconciliatio et Paenitentia* (2 December 1984, nn. 13–15): "to recognize oneself as being a sinner … is the essential first step in returning to God"; Babel and Eden share "an exclusion of God"; and the rupture with God becomes, inevitably, division between brothers — "a suicidal act," a "twofold wound" in the sinner and in his relationships. Paul's catalogue in Romans 1 describes the pagan world the Church first met; *Gaudium et Spes* 27 lists the "infamies" of ours — murder, abortion, euthanasia, slavery, degrading labour — that "do more harm to those who practice them than to those who suffer." The same truth appears from inside: Job's "Who can bring a clean thing out of an unclean?" (Job 14:4), the Psalmist's "in sin did my mother conceive me" (Ps 51:5), Paul's "I do not do what I want, but I do the very thing I hate" (Rom 7:15) — echoed even by Ovid's pagan confession, *video meliora proboque, deteriora sequor*. The Council's verdict closes the audience: "a monumental struggle against the powers of darkness pervades the whole history of man" (GS 37).

> 🕯 **Reflect:** Babel failed because its builders forgot the vertical dimension. Which of my own projects — family, work, plans — am I building as though God were irrelevant to them?

**Meditations for prayer:**

- Watch Cain and Abel walk into the field together. Feel how short the distance is from resentment to violence, and look for where that same distance runs through your own heart.
- Stand among the builders of Babel, proud of a tower that will make a name for them. Hear the languages break apart around you, and ask what in your life is being built for your name rather than God's.
- Pray Psalm 51 slowly, as David did after Nathan's rebuke. Stop at "Against you, you only, have I sinned," and let it be your own confession rather than a text.

## The Church's Dogma: Carthage, Orange, Trent

The audiences of 24 September and 1 October 1986 set out what the Church has defined. ✅ The doctrine, "transmitted implicitly in the Church's teaching from the beginning," was declared at Carthage (418) and Orange (529) against Pelagius, and "solemnly formulated" by Trent in 1546 (DS 1510-1516). Its content: the first parents sinned gravely, lost sanctifying grace and the holiness and justice in which they were constituted, incurred death, and fell under "the dominion of him who has the power of death" (DS 1511). Theologians distinguish Adam's own sin (*peccatum originale originans*) from its result in us (*originatum*), and it is the second that "modern culture" rejects as unfair to the individual. John Paul II answers that without it people "can no longer understand the mysterious and distressing aspects of evil," and "end up by wavering between a hasty and unjustified optimism and a radical pessimism bereft of hope." He is candid about Romans 5:12: the Greek *eph' hō* is better read "because all sinned" than the Vulgate's "in whom," but the doctrine stands equally on Romans 5:18-19. 🟢 He quotes Paul VI (1966) against explanations that begin "from the unproved premise of polygenism." Three precisions follow. ✅ Original sin passes "by generation and not by way of imitation" (DS 1513) — the reason the Church baptizes infants "for the remission of sin" (DS 1514). In Adam's descendants it is "not … personal guilt" but "the privation of sanctifying grace" in a nature diverted from its end — "a 'sin of nature,' only analogically comparable to 'personal sin'" (cf. CCC 404: "contracted," not "committed"). And it must "constantly be considered in reference to the mystery of the redemption": Adam is "a type of the one who was to come" (Rom 5:14).

For the homily: infant baptism is the parish's weekly catechism on original sin. A newborn has committed nothing, and the Church still pours the water — because grace, not guilt, is what the child lacks.

> 🕯 **Reflect:** The Pope says rejecting original sin leaves people swinging between naive optimism and despair. Which way do I swing when I look at the news, my family, or myself?

**Meditations for prayer:**

- Stand at a baptismal font as an infant is baptized. Look at the child who has done nothing, and understand what the water gives that the child lacks — not innocence, but divine life.
- Picture the bishops at Carthage in 418, arguing against a monk who said man needs no inner grace to be good. Ask how much of Pelagius lives in your own assumption that you can fix yourself by effort.
- Read Romans 5:18-19 aloud twice — once hearing "one man's trespass," once hearing "one man's obedience." Let the second weigh more than the first.

## Fallen but Not Destroyed

On 8 October 1986 the Pope took up Paul VI's *Credo of the People of God* (30 June 1968): human nature is "fallen, stripped of the grace that clothed it, injured in its own natural powers and subjected to the dominion of death," and transmitted "not by imitation, but by propagation." Genesis 3:16-19 sketches the result — pain in childbearing, toil, a ground that resists, return to dust — and Paul extends it to creation "subjected to futility … groaning in travail" (Rom 8:19-22). Man was made for immortality and happiness; with sin the possibility of exemption from suffering and death (*posse non mori*) was lost, and "the whole man, body and soul" was "changed for the worse" (Orange, 529; Trent's *in deterius commutatum*). The intellect is darkened; the will is weakened before sensible goods and "the false images of good elaborated by reason under the influence of the passions." But here comes the decisive Catholic qualification: "a relative and not an absolute deterioration." Man can still know "the fundamental natural and religious truths" and "perform good works." ✅ Against Luther and later the Jansenists, Trent defined that free will was "not lost and extinguished" (Session VI, can. 5). What remains, even after baptism, is concupiscence — the *fomes peccati* — which ✅ "is not yet sin," but "derives from sin and inclines to sin" (DS 1515). Without Christ fallen man cannot reach his supernatural end. And the last word belongs again to Romans 5:20: human nature is "not only 'fallen' but also 'redeemed.'"

> 🕯 **Reflect:** Concupiscence is not sin, but it pulls toward sin. Do I confuse temptation with guilt — and so either despair over feelings I did not choose, or excuse choices I did make?

**Meditations for prayer:**

- Picture a field once tended as a garden, now overrun with thorns but still fertile soil. Walk it slowly as a gardener would, seeing both what was lost and what can still grow.
- Recall a recurring temptation that has stayed with you even after confession. Hear Trent tell you it is not itself sin, and ask for the grace to fight it without being crushed by it.
- Imagine standing at a deathbed — your own, perhaps — and feel how unnatural death is to a creature made for immortality. Then hear the Church's answer: this nature is fallen, and it is also redeemed.

## Every Sin Repeats the First

The audiences of 29 October and 5 November 1986 turn from original sin to personal sin. Every actual sin repeats the "structure" of the first: "an abuse of freedom," seeking "to attain [one's] goal apart from God" (GS 13), now conditioned by inherited weakness. That weakness never cancels freedom; it varies the degree of guilt, which grounds the distinction of grave and venial sin — "grave sin is also 'mortal' because it brings about the loss of sanctifying grace." Sin is disobedience, and in revelation disobedience is the breaking of a covenant: with Adam, at Sinai with the Decalogue at its heart (Ex 20; 24:3-8), and in the "definitive covenant … sealed in the blood" of Christ. The same law is "written on their hearts" (Rom 2:14-15) — Aquinas's natural law (I-II, q.91 a.2; q.94). So sin is disobedience to "God as lawgiver, who at the same time is a loving Father" (Hos 11; Lk 15). Beneath it lies disbelief: the Spirit convicts the world of sin "because they do not believe in me" (Jn 16:9). The 5 November audience surveys the Bible's vocabulary — Hebrew *ra'* (evil), *rasha'* (the ungodly who forgets God), *pesha'* (rebellion), *'awon* (deviation, guilt), *hata'* (failing toward someone); Greek *anomia*, *hamartia*, *opheilēma* ("forgive us our debts"). Sin comes "out of the heart" (Mk 7:20-23) and is always "a conscious and free act." Then the key distinction: "the sin of the world" (Jn 1:29; 1 Jn 5:19) "is not to be identified with original sin." It is the "environment of sin" that personal sins create, which in turn draws people into new sins. 🟡 "In this sense one can perhaps speak of a sin of the structures." And from *Reconciliatio et Paenitentia* 16: "every soul that rises above itself, raises up the world," but there is also "a communion of sin, whereby a soul that lowers itself through sin drags down with itself the Church and, in some way, the whole world."

> 🕯 **Reflect:** No sin is purely private; each one adds to an environment that others must breathe. Whose spiritual air has my sin made harder to breathe?

**Meditations for prayer:**

- Picture the tablets of the Law carried down Sinai, and then the chalice raised at the Last Supper. See both as covenants, and see your sin as a signature withdrawn from a covenant written in blood.
- Walk through a city at night and notice how the lights, the advertising, the noise all invite certain choices. Recognize "the sin of the world" as something built, piece by piece, by persons — and ask what you have added to it.
- Pray the Our Father slowly and stop at "forgive us our debts." Count, honestly, what you owe, and remember Paul's word that love is the one debt we never finish paying.

## Alienation Reversed

The 12 November 1986 audience is the series' most daring. The tree was meant to remind man "remember that you are a creature … Do not wish to be what you are not!" The serpent's words planted, in "germinal" form, a criterion "to which sinful man would later turn many times": that God is "alienating" for man, so that to be himself man must discard God — the thesis John Paul II names in Feuerbach, Marx and Nietzsche. In this telling, God is "the 'usurper' and 'enemy' of creation." The Pope's answer is a reversal: "in the light of revelation and of faith, the theory of alienation should be reversed. It is precisely sin and only sin which leads to man's alienation!" Scripture shows the threefold alienation sin produces: from self ("the wicked go astray from the womb," Ps 58:3), from God ("alienated from the life of God," Eph 4:18), and from community (Eph 2:12). "Sin has diminished man" (GS 13); every sin "lessens" the dignity of the image; "every one who commits sin is a slave to sin" (Jn 8:34). Aquinas observes that as each good act makes a man better, each evil act makes him worse as a man (I-II, q.55 a.3; q.63 a.2). Sin therefore "contains its own immanent punishment": "Is it I whom they provoke? … Is it not themselves, to their own confusion?" (Jer 7:19). Literature, film and theatre, the Pope remarks, show modern man "deprived of an inner center … awaiting someone who never comes." The evil is not beyond remedy "as long as he preserves the sense of sin." Hence the warning of Pius XII he asks us to meditate on: "the sin of the century is the loss of the sense of sin."

For the homily: the modern objection is that religion makes us less human. The Pope's reply is not defensive. He turns the charge around — it is sin that makes us less human, and grace that gives us back to ourselves.

> 🕯 **Reflect:** Sin is its own first punishment. Where in my life am I already living the consequences of a sin I still call harmless?

**Meditations for prayer:**

- Picture the prodigal son in the far country, feeding pigs and envying them. Hear the Gospel say "he came to himself" (Lk 15:17), and notice that returning to the Father and returning to himself are one movement.
- Imagine a man who has lost all sense of sin, smiling and untroubled while his life comes apart. Then pray for the grace never to lose the ache of conscience, even when it hurts.
- Recall a time a sin left you feeling smaller, more divided, less yourself. Offer that experience to God as evidence the Pope is right, and ask to be given back to yourself.

## The Struggle and the Protoevangelium

The cycle closed on 10 and 17 December 1986, after the Pope's journey through Asia and Oceania. *Gaudium et Spes* 2 frames the world as "created and sustained by its Maker's love, fallen indeed into the bondage of sin, yet emancipated now by Christ." Genesis 3:9-15 is "God's response to man's sin": "Where are you?" … "Why have you done this?" — the question of motive, whose answer ("the serpent beguiled me") shows that "you will be like God" remains "the most essential and profound dimension … of every sin." God curses the serpent and announces a war that runs through history (GS 37; Eph 6:12; "through the devil's envy death entered the world," Wis 2:24). Yet "a light is shed on this mystery … from the very beginning which frees history from the nightmare of an inexorable condemnation." On 17 December the Pope quoted Eucharistic Prayer IV — "even when in disobedience he rejected your friendship you did not abandon him to the power of death" — and named Genesis 3:15 the *Protoevangelium*, "the first announcement of the Messiah Redeemer," fully understood only in the Gospel: "in the Old Testament the New lies hidden, and in the New the Old lies open" (Augustine, *Quaestiones in Heptateuchum* II, 73). It announces a struggle ending in Christ's victory "at the price of the sacrifice of the cross" ("you shall bruise his heel"), Christ as "the new Adam" (1 Cor 15:45), "obedient unto death" (Phil 2:8), and a God "infinitely just and at the same time infinitely merciful." Then the detail the Pope calls "extremely significant": the woman comes first — "between you and the woman." 🟢 Many Fathers and Doctors see in her the Mother of Christ, the new Eve (LG 56; Irenaeus), ✅ preserved from original sin (Pius IX, DS 2803). The series ends with her: "in Mary and through Mary the situation of humanity and of the world has been reversed, and they have in some way re-entered into the splendor of the morning of creation."

> 🕯 **Reflect:** God's first word after the Fall was a question — "Where are you?" — not a sentence. How would I answer it today, without hiding?

**Meditations for prayer:**

- Hide with Adam among the trees and hear God walking in the garden calling, "Where are you?" Notice that he comes looking. Step out from wherever you are hiding.
- Stand at the foot of the Cross and see the heel bruised and the serpent's head crushed in one event. Let the Protoevangelium become, for a moment, simply the Good Friday Gospel.
- Picture Mary at Nazareth, the new Eve, saying "let it be to me according to your word" where the first Eve said yes to the serpent. Ask her to obtain for you the same obedience.

## Key Corrections

- **"Original sin means I am personally guilty of Adam's act."** No. In his descendants it is "not … personal guilt" but the privation of sanctifying grace — a sin "contracted, not committed" (CCC 404), called sin only analogically.
- **"Original sin spreads by bad example."** That is Pelagius. ✅ Trent: it passes "by generation and not by way of imitation" (DS 1513) — which is why infants are baptized.
- **"Human nature is totally corrupt."** That is Luther and the Jansenists. Nature is wounded, not destroyed; ✅ free will remains (Trent, Session VI, can. 5), and man can still know natural truths and do good acts.
- **"Concupiscence is itself sin."** ✅ Trent: it "is not yet sin" but "derives from sin and inclines to sin" (DS 1515). Temptation is not guilt.
- **"Genesis 3 is myth with no event behind it."** The Pope respects literary form but insists on "a primordial event, that is, a fact … at the beginning of human history" (cf. CCC 390).
- **"Religion alienates man from himself."** The Pope reverses it: "it is precisely sin and only sin which leads to man's alienation."

## Why This Matters

These twelve audiences are the hinge between the Father and the Son. Without them, the Jesus Christ cycle that follows would answer a question no one had asked: saved from what? John Paul II's achievement is to make sin intelligible without making it hopeless. He holds the full Tridentine dogma, refuses Pelagian optimism and Lutheran pessimism alike, names the modern objection — that God alienates man — and turns it inside out. Then he lands the whole study on a woman and a promise, so that the last image of the darkest subject in the Creed is "the splendor of the morning of creation." Anyone who has ever said "I see the better and do the worse" will find themselves described here, and answered.
