# Lust & Chastity

*The seventh deadly sin, using a person instead of loving them, and the lively virtue that sees and honors every person as a gift.*

**In plain words:** sex is good, a beautiful gift from God meant for the faithful, life-giving love of husband and wife. Lust is **using another person** for my own pleasure, treating someone as an object instead of a person. Chastity isn't being against sex or pretending we don't have desires. It's **respect**: desire brought into harmony with love, so that we see every person as God's image, never as a thing. It's a lifelong path, with falls and fresh starts, and grace makes the victory real.

## Where lust sits

Lust is the **seventh** and last deadly sin, at the very top of Dante's mountain. Many people think it's the worst sin, but the tradition ranks it the **least grave in kind**. 🟡 St. Thomas Aquinas explains: the bodily sins misuse appetites that are natural and good, while pride and envy turn the soul directly against God.

That doesn't make lust harmless. Fully consented to in a serious matter, it is a mortal sin (Catechism 2351-2352), and its power to enslave and wound is enormous, as anyone who has struggled knows. **Last in kind, never last in danger.**

Bishop Barron, who jokes about the "Irish puritan" streak he grew up with, insists on the starting point: **sex is good, and sexual pleasure is God's gift.**

## What lust is

### Using a person

Barron's definition is simple: lust is **treating another human being not as an end, but merely as a means** to my own sexual satisfaction. What makes it sinful is **objectification**: turning a someone into a something.

- The philosopher **Immanuel Kant** put this at the heart of all morality: never treat a person merely as a means, always as an end.
- A young Polish priest teaching ethics in the 1950s, **Karol Wojtyła**, the future **St. John Paul II**, made it the center of his teaching on love and sexuality.
- As Pope, John Paul II said lust can happen **even within marriage**, and many laughed at him. But he was right: even spouses can treat each other as objects.

The Catechism defines it: *"Lust is disordered desire for or inordinate enjoyment of sexual pleasure. Sexual pleasure is morally disordered when sought for itself, isolated from its procreative and unitive purposes"* (Catechism 2351).

### When the body tells a lie

In God's plan, the union of husband and wife has two purposes that belong together: to **unite** them in total self-giving and to be **open to new life** (Catechism 2360-2363). St. John Paul II taught that the body has its own "language": it speaks total, faithful love. Lust uses that language to say something false. It makes the body tell a lie.

### It starts in the heart

Jesus said: *"Everyone who looks at a woman with lustful intent has already committed adultery with her in his heart"* (Matthew 5:28). Lust isn't only in actions; it begins in the **look** and in the imagination. That's why the battle is mostly fought with the eyes and the mind, before it ever reaches the body.

### Where we see it today

Barron speaks plainly about **pornography**: a multi-billion-dollar industry, now available everywhere through the internet, that turns real people into objects. It is **not a victimless sin**: many of those people are pressured or exploited. In its worst forms, the same objectification fuels human trafficking and the abuse of children, which is why the abuse scandals in the Church were so horrifying.

### Lust darkens the mind

Aquinas lists lust's "children": blindness of mind, thoughtlessness, inconstancy, rashness, self-love, and even hatred of God and despair. Notice how many are about the **mind**: lust is the most self-deceiving appetite. It promises freedom and delivers slavery. St. Augustine, who lived it before God freed him, described the chain: desire given in to becomes **habit**, and habit not resisted becomes **necessity** (*Confessions* VIII).

### Mercy, not permission

The Church is realistic and merciful: habit, anxiety, immaturity and other factors *"can lessen, if not even reduce to a minimum, moral culpability"* (Catechism 2352). That's mercy, not permission. God sees the whole struggle of the heart.

> *"Do you not know that your body is a temple of the Holy Spirit within you?"* — 1 Corinthians 6:19

🕯 **Reflect:** Where do I treat a person, in a picture or in my imagination, as a *thing* for my pleasure instead of a soul made in God's image?

## The cure: chastity

### Respect for the other

Barron defines chastity as **a deep respect for the otherness of the other**: refusing ever to turn a person into an object. The Catechism calls it *"the successful integration of sexuality within the person"* (Catechism 2337). That's the key: chastity is **not repression** and not the absence of desire. It's desire made whole, free and ordered by love, so the body tells the truth of self-giving instead of the lie of self-use.

### "I don't need you"

Barron shares a surprising saying from the spiritual masters: *unless you can turn to the person next to you and say "I don't need you," you can't really love that person.* It sounds cold, but it's the opposite. Love means **wanting the other person's good for their own sake**, not wanting my good through them. "He needs me!" can be immature; need can quickly turn into using. Only when I'm not grasping can I truly love, even my spouse, my parents, my children. That's what Jesus meant by His hard saying about putting Him before father and mother (Luke 14:26).

### A lifelong apprenticeship

The Catechism is honest: *"Self-mastery is a long and exacting work. One can never consider it acquired once and for all. It presupposes renewed effort at all stages of life"* (Catechism 2342). Chastity isn't a switch flipped once; it's a virtue that **grows**, with falls, Confession and getting back up as a normal part of the road.

And it's for **everyone**: married, single, priests and religious, each according to their state in life (Catechism 2348-2349).

### Purity of heart sees God

*"Blessed are the pure in heart, for they shall see God"* (Matthew 5:8). Lust blinds; purity clears our sight, so we see people as people, and in the end, see God (Catechism 2518-2519).

### Saints who won this battle

- **St. Augustine**, enslaved for years, became one of the greatest Doctors of the Church.
- **St. Mary of Egypt**, once a prostitute, became a great woman of prayer.
- **St. Margaret of Cortona** left years as a nobleman's mistress to become a Franciscan saint.

They prove the chains really can break, not by willpower alone, but by grace, the sacraments and never giving up.

### Mary: "How can this be?"

When the angel told Mary she would be the Mother of God, she asked: *"How can this be, since I do not know man?"* (Luke 1:34). Bishop Barron sees her purity as the opposite of lust: a heart that never makes another person an object.

On Dante's mountain, the lustful must **pass through fire**. Lust burns and hurts, but they pass through it quickly, and it purifies them. Then, at last, they reach the top, light enough to fly to heaven.

🕯 **Reflect:** When I fall, do I treat it as final proof I'm a failure, or as part of a long road where I get up, confess and continue? What one temptation can I *remove* from my life today?

## How to fight lust

### Starve the sin

- **Guard your eyes.** The battle is won or lost at the first glance (Matthew 5:28-29). Look away fast.
- **Remove the temptation; don't just resist it.** Use filters, avoid being alone with screens late at night, cut off access before the craving comes.
- **If pornography is part of your life, stop.** It isn't harmless and it isn't victimless.
- **Go to Confession often.** It's the main weapon: name it plainly and rise again (Catechism 1496).
- **Fast.** Mastering the appetite for food strengthens mastery over the body.
- **Don't fight alone.** Lust grows in secrecy and loneliness. Talk to a priest or spiritual director, and if it has become an addiction, **get help**: good support groups exist.
- **End any relationship that uses or manipulates.** This is serious.

### Grow the virtue

- **Receive the Eucharist** worthily and often. Grace, not willpower alone, is the engine of chastity.
- **Turn to Our Lady.** Pray the Rosary; her pure heart is a refuge.
- **Train your eyes to see persons:** a soul, God's image, someone's son or daughter.
- **Ask about your relationships,** even the closest: how much is "need" running things?
- **Build a simple daily rule** of prayer and good habits.

🎯 **The one move that matters most:** at the first look or first thought, *turn right away* (eyes, mind, body) and say a short prayer: *"Jesus, mercy"* or *"Mary, help."* Don't negotiate with it. Turn and pray, instantly.

### A word of hope

For many people, this is the hardest daily battle. Take heart: the sin that feels most enslaving is, in the Church's eyes, *not* the one that says the most about how far we are from God. Pride is worse. And the saints show that this exact chain can be broken. *"Where sin increased, grace abounded all the more"* (Romans 5:20). Despair is lust's last lie.

**Lust grabs a person as a thing; chastity sees a person as a gift, and is set free to love.**

## The end of the climb: "Lazarus, come out!"

At the top of Dante's mountain, the soul is finally light enough to fly. Bishop Barron ends the whole series here: God wants us **free**.

All seven deadly sins grow from **fear**. Afraid, we cling. Afraid, we try to justify ourselves. Afraid, we stuff our lives with pleasure, money, power and our own egos.

St. Augustine read the raising of Lazarus as our story. We are like Lazarus in the tomb, wrapped in the bands of pride, envy, anger, sloth, greed, gluttony and lust. Jesus calls, *"Lazarus, come out!"* and then says, *"Unbind him, and let him go"* (John 11:43-44).

So, says Barron: **realize that God is loving you into existence right now. Relax. Find your mission. And let that love flow through you to the whole world.**
