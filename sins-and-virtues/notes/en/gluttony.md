# Gluttony & Temperance

*The sixth deadly sin, letting appetite run the show, and the lively virtues of self-mastery and fasting that set us free to hunger for God.*

**In plain words:** food, drink and feasting are good gifts from God, and the Church loves a good celebration. Gluttony is letting the appetite take over: eating or drinking too much, too fast, too fussily, or using food to fill a hunger only God can fill. The cure is **temperance**, trained by **asceticism**: small, freely chosen "no's" like fasting, so that we stay in charge and our deepest hunger can turn toward God.

## Where gluttony sits

Gluttony is the **sixth** deadly sin, near the top of Dante's mountain. Bishop Barron explains that pride, envy and anger are at the bottom because they twist the *spirit*. Gluttony and lust are more about **passions overpowering reason**. They are still deadly sins, but the great spiritual masters treat them with more compassion. 🟡 Aquinas agrees: gluttony is less grave *in kind* because the appetite it disorders is natural and necessary. But less grave doesn't mean harmless; it dulls the mind and often opens the door to other sins.

## What gluttony is

### Not puritanism

Let's be clear: the Catholic faith is **not** against pleasure. The writer Hilaire Belloc joked: *"Wherever the Catholic sun doth shine, there's always laughter and good red wine."* Taste, feasts and good company are God's gifts. St. Thomas Aquinas defines gluttony as **immoderate or unreasonable pleasure in food and drink**. The sin isn't eating; it's being *ruled* by eating. St. Paul warns of those *"whose god is their belly"* (Philippians 3:19).

### Five ways to be a glutton

Aquinas noticed that gluttony isn't only about eating too much. He lists five forms, eating:

1. **Too soon:** not able to wait, grabbing a snack the moment the craving comes.
2. **Too expensively:** always needing the fanciest, most luxurious food.
3. **Too much:** plain overeating or overdrinking.
4. **Too eagerly:** wolfing food down, barely tasting it.
5. **Too fussily:** obsessing over how everything must be prepared, being picky and demanding.

So gluttony can look like the overeater, but also like the obsessive "foodie," or the person who can never wait.

### A spiritual hunger

Overeating and overdrinking have physical and psychological sides. But the great psychologist Carl Jung said that at bottom, every psychological problem is a spiritual problem.

St. Augustine explains why: *"You have made us for yourself, O Lord, and our hearts are restless until they rest in you."* We are made with an **infinite hunger**, a hunger for God. When we try to fill it with something finite (food, drink, pleasure, shopping), it never works. So we panic and want more, and more, and more, going round and round a tiny track. That's how **addiction** works, and gluttony is an addiction of the heart.

### The first temptation of Jesus

After fasting forty days in the desert, Jesus was hungry. The devil pointed at the stones (which in the Judean desert really do look like little loaves) and said: *"Command these stones to become bread"* (Matthew 4:3). The temptation was to make **satisfying the body** the center of life. Jesus answered: *"Man shall not live by bread alone, but by every word that comes from the mouth of God"* (Matthew 4:4).

And notice: the very first sin in Eden also involved eating (Genesis 3:6). Jesus begins His mission by reversing it.

### Like little children

The monk Thomas Merton compared our desires for food, drink and pleasure to **little children**: "I want it! I want it now!" Any parent knows: "No, not now, dinner is in half an hour." If you give in every time, the kids take over the house. If you lovingly discipline them, better things can grow.

Our appetites are the same. If they run everything, life shrinks to chasing them, and we forget the things that make life rich: love, friendship, good conversation, learning, beauty and God.

Aquinas also lists gluttony's "children": silly, excessive giddiness, coarse talk, impurity, babbling, and a dull, foggy mind.

> *"Do not be among drunkards or among gluttonous eaters of meat, for the drunkard and the glutton will come to poverty."* — Proverbs 23:20-21

🕯 **Reflect:** Of Aquinas's five forms, which is *mine*? Maybe not "too much"; maybe too eagerly, too fussily, too soon. Where has an ordinary good become a little master over me?

## The cure: temperance and asceticism

### Temperance: the free person's virtue

Temperance is one of the four **cardinal virtues**, the "hinges" (*cardo*) of the moral life (Catechism 1805). The Catechism says it *"moderates the attraction of pleasures and provides balance in the use of created goods,"* ensuring *"the will's mastery over instincts"* (Catechism 1809). Temperance doesn't despise food; it **frees** us to enjoy it rightly, with gratitude, as a gift instead of a master. As St. Benedict taught, it's the joy of the person who eats to live instead of living to eat.

### Asceticism: training for the soul

Bishop Barron calls the lively virtue **asceticism**: freely chosen discipline. The word sounds scary, like a gloomy monk. But think about it: we happily climb onto a stair machine or exercise bike and sweat for an hour, and follow strict diets, all for our bodies! Barron once read a health magazine on a plane and laughed: "So *this* is where the Puritans went. They edit health magazines!"

If we accept discipline so readily for our physical health, why not for our spiritual health, which matters infinitely more?

Barron recalls students who visited the Holy Land during the Muslim month of Ramadan. They said they could feel the fast everywhere: in how people walked, talked and did business. Fasting is part of every great religious tradition, including ours. Barron believes Catholics lost something when many of these practices faded after the Second Vatican Council.

### Fasting: practicing "no"

The Catechism names fasting as one of the main forms of penance (Catechism 1434, 1438), especially in Lent. Fasting doesn't say food is bad. It **trains the will**, teaching the body to obey instead of command. Jesus fasted, and said some victories come only *"by prayer and fasting"* (Matthew 17:21). Practicing "no" to good things makes it possible to say "no" to bad things.

### Feeding the deepest hunger

The Eucharist puts everything in order: our deepest hunger isn't for bread but for **the Bread of Life** (John 6:35). Temperance at the table clears the heart to hunger for God.

### "O Lord, open my lips"

On Dante's mountain, the gluttonous sing a verse from the Psalms: *"O Lord, open my lips, and my mouth shall declare your praise"* (Psalm 51:15). They used to open their lips only to feed themselves. Now they open them to praise God. Barron's suggestion: sometimes, instead of eating, **pray**.

Dante also gives Mary as the example here: at the wedding in Cana, she cared about the joy of the feast for others more than about her own mouth (John 2:3).

🕯 **Reflect:** Can I practice one small, deliberate "no" at the table today, like skipping seconds, waiting for a treat, or a real fast? It isn't about the food; it's about taking back the steering wheel.

## How to fight gluttony

### Starve the sin

- **Fast on the Church's days,** Fridays and Lent, to build the muscle of a deliberate "no" (Catechism 1438). Fasting rules are for healthy adults; children, the elderly, the sick and pregnant or nursing mothers are excused, so ask your priest or doctor what's right for you.
- **Find your form** among Aquinas's five and work on that one.
- **Leave the table a little hungry.** It's good for body and soul.
- **Eat at mealtimes.** Resist the "right now" snack and wait for the meal.
- **Confess the mastery, not just the excess.** Name where the belly has become a little god.

### Grow the virtue

- **Fast for real now and then.** Barron has friends who fast on bread and water on the Fridays of Lent.
- **Skip a meal, spend that hour in prayer, and give the money you would have spent to the poor.**
- **Instead of a night out drinking, pray the Rosary.** Its gentle repetition is meant to draw you into God's rhythm.
- **Give up a favorite food,** for Lent or even for a whole year.
- **Say grace before meals.** Gratitude turns eating back into a gift.
- **Enjoy food well:** savor it with thanks, and stop in peace.
- **Hunger for the Eucharist** above all.

🎯 **The one move that matters most:** when the craving hits, *pause once*: wait, give thanks, then choose. Gluttony grabs immediately; temperance pauses first.

**Gluttony tries to fill an endless hunger with bread; temperance makes room for the Bread of Life.**
