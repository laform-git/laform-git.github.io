# Architects of the Culture of Death — Note 4: The Secular Utopianists

## Core Idea
A perfect society can be engineered by human design once religion is cleared away. The individual person — including the unborn person — becomes a means to a future goal or an obstacle to someone else's plan.

---
## The Architects
**Karl Marx (1818–1883, German)**
- *Core claim:* History is class struggle; religion is "the opium of the people" (1844); revolution will abolish private property and produce a classless society (*Communist Manifesto*, 1848, with Engels; *Capital*, 1867).
- *Cultural footprint:* The most politically consequential thinker of the modern era. Governments built on his ideas ruled a third of humanity in the 20th century (see [[Architects 09 Political Outcomes Map|Note 9]]).

**Auguste Comte (1798–1857, French)**
- *Core claim:* Humanity passes through three stages — theological, metaphysical, and "positive" (scientific) — and a scientific elite should govern. Founded positivism, coined "sociology," and designed a "Religion of Humanity" with its own calendar of secular saints.
- *Cultural footprint:* Positivism shaped Latin American elites. Brazil's flag still carries Comte's motto, "Order and Progress," and his thought entered Mexico's schools through Gabino Barreda (see [[Architects 10 Latin America Case Study|Note 10]]).

**Judith Jarvis Thomson (1929–2020, American)**
- *Core claim:* "A Defense of Abortion" (1971) argued that even if the unborn child is a person, the mother has no obligation to sustain it — comparing pregnancy to being kidnapped and connected to a famous violinist who needs your kidneys.
- *Cultural footprint:* One of the most reprinted essays in modern ethics. The "bodily autonomy" argument it launched is still the main philosophical defense of abortion.

---
## From Philosophy to Institutions
- **Education:** Soviet and Chinese schools taught state atheism as official doctrine. Comte's positivism became the curriculum of Mexico's National Preparatory School (1867). Thomson's essay is standard reading in university ethics courses.
- **Medicine:** Soviet Russia became the first country to legalize abortion in 1920, grounded in Engels' theory of the family; the USSR went on to have among the highest abortion rates in the world. Stalin re-banned it in 1936 for population reasons, and it was re-legalized in 1955.
- **Law:** Marxist states codified atheism and restricted religion. Positivist ideas shaped the constitutions of Brazil's first republic and Mexico's reform laws. Thomson's argument moved abortion defense from "it is not a person" to "it does not matter if it is" — a shift that has become more important in abortion politics after *Dobbs* (2022).

## The Secular Case (no faith required)
- **Marx's predictions failed on their own terms.** Revolution came first in agrarian Russia, not industrial England. Workers' living standards rose under market economies instead of collapsing. Economists Ludwig von Mises (1920) and Friedrich Hayek (1945) showed why central planning cannot work: no planner can gather the information that prices carry.
- **The human cost is measured, not imagined.** The Great Leap Forward famine in China (1959–1961) killed tens of millions — historians' estimates range roughly from 15 to 45 million. The Soviet famine of 1932–1933 killed millions in Ukraine and Kazakhstan.
- **Comte was rejected by his own admirers.** John Stuart Mill, a secular philosopher who had championed Comte's early work, criticized his later Religion of Humanity as a blueprint for spiritual despotism (*Auguste Comte and Positivism*, 1865).
- **Thomson's premise fails in embryology.** Standard embryology textbooks state that a new human organism begins at fertilization. Secular philosopher Don Marquis argued in "Why Abortion Is Immoral" (1989) that killing the unborn is wrong for the same reason killing an adult is wrong: it deprives a being of a future like ours. The violinist analogy also ignores the key difference that parents are not strangers kidnapped off the street.

---
## Truth's Defenders in Their Own Era
- **Pius IX, *Quanta Cura* and the *Syllabus of Errors* (1864):** Condemned communism and socialism while Marx was alive and writing *Capital*.
- **Leo XIII, *Quod Apostolici Muneris* (1878) and *Rerum Novarum* (1891):** Defended workers' just wages and unions while rejecting class war and the abolition of property, predicting that socialism would harm the very workers it claimed to help.
- **Pius XI, *Divini Redemptoris* (1937):** Condemned atheistic communism as intrinsically wrong — in the same week he issued *Mit brennender Sorge* against Nazism.
- **Aleksandr Solzhenitsyn:** A Gulag survivor who concluded that the catastrophe happened because men had forgotten God (Templeton address, 1983).

---
## The Church's Defense — The Person Is Never a Means
The aspect of human dignity at stake is **the individual person as an end, never a tool** — whether for a classless future or for another person's convenience. John Paul II's *Centesimus Annus* (1991), written as the Soviet bloc fell, diagnosed socialism's basic error as anthropological: it treats the person as a mere cell of the social body. For Thomson's argument, the Church answers with *Donum Vitae* (1987) and *Evangelium Vitae* (1995, §58–62): the unborn child is a human being from conception and must be respected as a person (CCC 2270–2271).

The Church steps in because every utopia built without God ends up deciding which humans are in the way.

## Open Threads
- Thomson is a surprising fit for "Utopianists" — her argument is individualist, not collectivist. The common thread is engineering outcomes by removing the claims of the weaker party.
